# Anchored and accumulative — why reservation and education haven't produced equity

Working note, 2026-08-21. Companion to `Vision doc.md`, which it revises.

The starting question: why has education not achieved equity, and why has reservation not
lifted marginalised communities rather than individuals?

---

## The hypothesis this note started from

That the current structure is extractive. Once someone gets the benefit of reservation or
education they move out and away from their original community and often don't contribute
much back — partly escape, partly a belief in merit ("if I could do it, others can too"),
partly the exhaustion of still not escaping oppressive structures even with upward economic
mobility. Meanwhile privileged classes, when they struggle economically, are caught by their
community and get chances to get back on their feet; the oppressed classes, when one member
achieves mobility, can't pull others up.

The observation holds. The **locus of failure** in it was misplaced.

## Where it fails: locus, not structure

The claim was structural ("the structure is extractive") but the causal weight was put on
beneficiary disposition — merit-belief, distance, choosing not to return. That layer is real
and documented, but it is **downstream, not causal**. Remove the merit ideology entirely and
the extraction still happens, because the benefit is unusable without leaving.

Ranked:

- **Load-bearing:** the instrument is *portable* and *non-accumulative*, and no receiving
  institution exists.
- **Real but derivative:** merit-belief and stigma-distance. Worth tracking because it governs
  whether a receiving institution, once built, gets participation. BAMCEF's hard part was
  recruitment, not funding.

Consequence: if the failure is instrument design, the fix is designing a better instrument. If
it were disposition, the fix would be persuasion — which is where `Vision doc.md` currently
half sits ("create awareness, political will and a strong belief"). Belief with no vehicle to
deposit it in decays.

## The asymmetry is an empty balance sheet, not defection

A Baniya or Marwari network can catch a falling member because the network holds *surplus* —
business ownership, credit, credentialed contacts. A Dalit network can hold identical
solidarity and have nothing to redistribute. Same loyalty, different endowment. Low-caste
networks do channel jobs and insurance; they are capital-poor, not commitment-poor.

The direct refutation of "beneficiaries don't give back" is **BAMCEF** (founded 1971, launched
6 Dec 1978), built by Kanshi Ram out of educated government employees from oppressed
communities, funded on an explicit ask to contribute **2% of income** — his phrase was
*"pay back to society."* The willingness was there. What was scarce was an aggregating vehicle.
So this is a supply-of-institutions problem, not a character problem.

---

## The frame: anchored, accumulative, non-concentrable

Three conditions. Fail any one and the instrument does not produce durable community change.

### 1. Anchored — non-portable by construction

Not "gives back to the community" (that is behavioural and re-imports disposition).
**Anchored means the value cannot be realised elsewhere**, so staying is not generosity, it is
the only way to use the asset.

- A degree or a government job is **portable**. Its value is realised elsewhere, so exit is the
  rational use of it. Extraction isn't a moral failure of the recipient; it is what the
  instrument was built to do.
- A local elected office is **anchored**. The seat *is* the constituency. You cannot take a
  sarpanch-ship to Gurgaon, and re-election dependency keeps the holder accountable to the same
  population the quota was for.

### 2. Accumulative — a pledgeable stock

Three sub-tests: **stock not flow** (survives the earner's job loss or death), **transmissible
without re-qualification** (the next generation inherits rather than re-earning from zero), and
**claim-held** (someone has enforceable title).

Operational proxy for all three: **pledgeability.** Can it be borrowed against? That is what
turns a stock into compounding — it lets the next generation start from a balance sheet instead
of zero. A degree cannot be pledged. Land can. A cooperative share can. A group's repayment
record can.

### 3. Non-concentrable — the claim stays broadly held

An anchored, accumulative asset **defaults to capture by the community's own elite.** This is
the same creamy-layer mechanism from the reservation literature, one level up. Evidence sits
inside the positive controls themselves (see case table): caste-association institutions drift
toward family control, and Maharashtra's sugar cooperatives became instruments of Maratha
dominance and produced *internal* stratification within the Maratha cluster rather than
reducing it. The asset existing is not sufficient; whether the claim can be concentrated or
alienated decides whether it stays a community asset for a second generation.

**Inclusive change = all three.** This is a tighter definition than the term usually carries,
and it is testable against cases rather than asserted.

**The load-bearing quadrant is anchored + accumulative + non-concentrable, and almost nothing
in Indian education policy occupies it.**

## What this means for the vision doc

`Vision doc.md` defines equity as *"when General category cut-offs and Reserved category
cut-offs become similar."* That targets **credential access**, which is non-accumulative by
construction, and it is a metric measured at the entry gate — downstream of ~15 years of
unequal formation, so it lags any intervention by more than a political term.

The same goal pursued as **community ownership of educational institutions** clears all three
conditions and has an Indian track record: the groups that actually moved — Nadars, Lingayats,
Vokkaligas, Marathas via Vidya Prasarak Samaj — did it through associations that *owned* the
schools, colleges and banks. A century of individual credential access has produced mobility
that does not transmit.

**Decision to make:** is the thing being built credential access, or institutional ownership?

## Formation lag: the capability half

`Vision doc.md` targets credential access. Underneath it sits a prior constraint — whether the
child can use a seat at all — and that is settled long before the entrance exam.

**The gap is fully visible by Std III (~age 8).** ASER 2024, rural India, government schools:
the share of Std III children able to read a Std II level text was 20.9% in 2018, fell to 16.3%
in 2022, and recovered to **23.4% in 2024** — the highest since ASER began. Subtraction, Std III
all-India: 28.2% (2018) → 25.9% (2022) → **33.7% (2024)**; government schools only, 20.9% →
20.2% → **27.6%**.

Two cautions on these numbers, both mechanism-relevant:

- **Government-school and all-school series are different and get mixed up in reporting.** The
  23.4% reading figure is government schools; the all-school figure is ~27.1%. Always state which.
- **The direction is up, not down.** Post-COVID recovery is real and 2024 beats 2018 on both
  measures, with government schools recovering faster than private and the government-private
  reading differential narrowing from ~20 points in 2018 to ~12 in 2024. Any framing that says
  Indian foundational learning is deteriorating fails against this data. It is low, and improving.

**Why the gap doesn't close: the curriculum outruns the child.** Pritchett & Beatty (CGD WP 293)
— education systems "are attempting to push children through curricular material faster than
their teachers can teach it and their students can learn it. Students fall behind and eventually
stop learning." Their India illustration: the average Std VIII student has mastered roughly Std
IV material, so about half sit at Std II–III level while instruction is pitched years above them.
Their counterintuitive result is that a *slower* curriculum yields more total learning.

**What that looks like inside one classroom.** Muralidharan, Singh & Ganimian (Mindspark RCT,
Delhi): "the learning levels of the highest and lowest achieving students in the same grade
typically spanned five to six grade levels in preparation," and the deficit against grade norms
is larger in later grades. Grouping children by measured level rather than by grade produced
**+0.37σ in maths** and **+0.23σ in Hindi**. Grade-based instruction cannot serve a six-grade
spread, so it serves the top of it.

**Persist or diverge — never converge.** Two strands of the longitudinal evidence differ in
degree, not direction:

- Young Lives (Andhra Pradesh/Telangana, same children at ages 5, 8, 12): SES disparities in
  vocabulary emerge early and stay roughly **constant**.
- *Learning in India's primary schools: How do disparities widen across the grades?*: gaps driven
  by household wealth and parental schooling **increase in magnitude across grades**; wealth
  dominates; gender gaps widen only among the poorest.

This is not a contradiction and should not be written as one. Both say the gap never closes; they
disagree only on whether school is neutral toward it or compounds it. The result that *would*
have undercut the argument is convergence, and neither finds it. Young Lives Round 7 (2023-24,
cohort at 22) supplies the downstream link: "Early life inequalities predict education outcomes,
particularly higher education enrolment and school completion by age 22."

### Two consequences for the project

**1. Cut-off convergence is a lagging indicator by ~15 years.** Fix Std III today and entrance
cut-offs move in the 2040s. It cannot discipline any actor on a five-year horizon — the same
conclusion the political-will section reaches, arriving from the data side instead.

**2. The binding constraint is instructional level, not access.** Reservation, scholarships and
school buildings all operate on access. Pritchett–Beatty and Mindspark both locate the lever in
teaching children where they actually are — which Pratham/TaRL built and Mindspark automated,
both with measured effects. Against the frontier screen in the root `CLAUDE.md` this is an
*instrument keyed to the wrong object* case, and the unserved object is **the child two grade
levels behind sitting in a grade-level classroom**. Unusually for this problem set, the blocker
is not political: Pratham operates at scale as a non-state actor.

### But early education alone reproduces the extraction

Early education is the critical lever on **capability** and does nothing on **accumulation**. It
is portable by construction: fix foundational learning perfectly and the result is more people
*able to exit*, at higher volume and better quality. Both downstream constraints still bind —
Thorat & Attewell's callback gap, and near-total Dalit landlessness. Learning produces wage
capability, not a balance sheet.

| | Lever | Status in `Vision doc.md` |
|---|---|---|
| **Capability** — can they use the seat? | early education, taught at measured level | proxied by "political will" — the weakest link |
| **Accumulation** — does it transmit? | community-owned institutions | absent |

**The move that does both is the community owning the institution that delivers the early
education.** That is the Nadar structure: the Sangam owned the schools, so the learning was
delivered *and* the asset stayed and compounded. One project, not two — provided the ownership
question is answered at the outset rather than bolted on.

## Why "political will" is not the lever

`Vision doc.md` rests on manufacturing political will. Decompose it and it stops being a lever.
Will reliably moves **inputs** (buildings, uniforms, laptops, meal expansion) because those are
within-term and visible. It does not move learning:

1. **Horizon mismatch.** Learning gains materialise over 10–15 years; the term is 5. Even
   sincere will has no payoff inside the accountability window.
2. **No local signal.** A voter can see a school building; they cannot see whether their child
   can read. Absent a ward-level legible learning number there is nothing for will to be
   rewarded against, so parents demand the proxies they can see — enrollment, pass rates,
   English medium — and boards oblige. This is a measurement failure wearing a will costume.
3. **Nobody has tenure.** Iyer & Mani: 49% annual transfer probability for IAS officers,
   average tenure in post ~16 months, only 56% of District Officers spending over a year in
   their district; transfers spike when a new CM takes office. Career return on a decade-long
   education gain is zero; on loyalty and caste affinity to the ruling party's base it is
   positive.
4. **Organised veto on the cost side.** Teachers are the state's largest organised workforce and
   a working electoral machine. Reforms whose cost lands on them meet a coordinated veto; the
   beneficiaries are children, who are unorganised.

**And the cases that worked aren't explained by will.** Kerala and Himachal both had prior
social structure — land reform, relatively egalitarian land distribution, no dominant landlord
class, an existing social movement. In both, will was **downstream of community structure**,
not upstream of it. Which returns to the same conclusion: the receiving institution comes first.

**Productive inversion:** don't manufacture will, manufacture the **signal** it responds to. A
within-term, ward-level, publicly legible learning number makes will endogenous. That also makes
the measurement layer the investable piece — a missing detection layer with an identifiable
buyer, per the frontier screen in the root `CLAUDE.md`.

## Evidence

Verified 2026-08-21. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is written rather than resolved.

**Political reservation shifts budgets — Pande (2003), AER 93(4):1132–51.** Identification: the
share of state assembly seats reserved for SC/ST is set mechanically by census population share
and revised at delimitation, so variation is demographic bookkeeping rather than political
demand. 16 major states, ~1960–1992. Findings, and the asymmetry matters: **ST reservation
significantly raised spending on ST welfare programmes but did not affect job reservations; SC
reservation raised state-government job quotas but did not affect SC welfare spending.** Pande
reads this off geography — SCs dispersed and dependent on mandated seats, bargaining for
portable individual entitlements; STs concentrated, bargaining for place-based goods. Outcome
variable throughout is **budget allocation**, not literacy or income or assets.

**…but does not shift outcomes — Jensenius (2015), AEJ: Applied 7(3):196–220, and *Social
Justice through Inclusion* (OUP, 2017).** Development indicators for 3,100+ state assembly
constituencies across 15 states, 1971 and 2001: **30 years of quotas produced no detectable
constituency-level effect on overall development or on redistribution to SCs.** Her interviews
attribute this to the power of political parties and to the electoral incentives the quota
system itself creates — most SC politicians see themselves as representatives of their party and
their whole electorate, not of their group.

Pande and Jensenius are both right and the gap between them is the finding: **money moves, harm
doesn't.** This is the empirical basis for calling political reservation anchored but not
accumulative.

**Post-mobility discrimination persists — Thorat & Attewell (2007), EPW.** Correspondence study,
500+ private-sector employers, identical applications differing only in surname. Applicants with
Dalit surnames received callbacks at roughly **two-thirds** the rate of upper-caste-surname
applicants; Muslim names fared worse still. Equally or better-qualified low-caste applicants are
significantly less likely to pass hiring screens in the modern formal sector. Reservation does
not reach this sector at all.

**Merit-belief and stigma are real but downstream — Deshpande & Newman, "Where the Path Leads."**
Surveyed upper- and lower-caste university students in Delhi with comparable credentials:
lower-caste students held lower expectations and self-assessments, and some opted out of the
placement cell entirely to avoid the humiliation of group discussions. Also documented
microaggressions — separate hostel seating, not being invited home. Note the counter-current in
their own interviews: beneficiaries were largely glad to have had the chance. So this supports
the *derivative* reading, not the causal one.

**Assets, not wages, are the gap — Zacharias & Vakulabharanam (Levy WP 566), 1991–92 and
2002–03 wealth surveys.** SC/ST wealth substantially below "forward" castes, OBCs and
non-Hindus in the middle; SC/ST share of the *upper* wealth deciles far below their share of the
lower ones. Between-caste inequality accounts for only ~13% of overall wealth inequality in
2002–03, in part because of large within-group heterogeneity — which is itself the
non-concentrability point: the category is not the unit of benefit. **An earlier stated figure of
"~7% of wealth vs 20% of population" is not confirmed by this source and should not be used.**

**Landlessness — sources disagree, and the definitions differ.** *(Figures below are from
search-result summaries of the cited reports, not verbatim-checked against the reports
themselves. Do this before use — see open question 5.)* Census 2011: **58.4%** of rural
Dalit households own no land. NSSO 2004–05: **78%** landlessness among rural SCs vs 57% for
non-SC/ST. NSSO 66th round (2009–10): only **17.1%** of rural SC households own land, and
**92.1%** of rural SCs were landless *or held ≤1 ha*. Agricultural Census 2015–16: 92% of SC
holdings are marginal (0–2 ha), together just **9% of total agricultural land**, average holding
0.78 ha. The 58% / 78% / 92% figures are not in conflict once "landless" vs "landless or
marginal" is separated — but the spread is large enough that any single number must be quoted
with its definition and round.

**Positive control, community-owned institutions — Nadar Mahajana Sangam, founded 1910.** Now
controls 9 schools, 5 ITIs, 2 arts colleges, a pharmacy college and 2 polytechnics; proposed a
bank in 1920, opened it in Tuticorin on 11 Nov 1921 — later Tamil Nadu Mercantile Bank. Over
3,000 scholarship loans issued by 1964. The community moved from near-untouchability to social
and economic power within roughly two centuries, and the schools were deliberately opened to
non-Nadar children as a goodwill strategy. Owned institutions, not individual credentials.

**Positive control, member-owned margin — Amul / GCMMF.** Three-tier structure: village
societies → district unions → state federation, controlled by **3.6 million** milk producers
across 18,600 Gujarat villages, procuring ~300 lakh litres/day, **₹59,545 crore (≈USD 7bn)
turnover in FY2023-24** (₹65,911 crore FY25). The producers own the processing and the brand, so
the downstream margin accrues to the member-owner rather than a trader. Ownership of the
*margin* is the test, not ownership of the raw supply.

**Positive control, collectively-held credit standing — Kudumbashree.** Three-tier and
women-only: neighbourhood groups (NHGs) → Area Development Societies → Community Development
Societies. Verbatim-verified figures: **~291,507 neighbourhood groups**, **~₹47.52 billion
(≈₹4,752 crore) collected as thrift**, and **~2,600 small-scale enterprises** managed by
members. *Unverified — do not use until checked:* the commonly-cited 4.3 million member count
and the ADS/CDS counts (19,489 / 1,064) appear only in secondary summaries, not on the
Kudumbashree or Participedia pages fetched. Loan terms, rates and repayment periods are decided
by the women themselves, which is why monitoring is internal and default is rare. The group's
repayment record functions as a network-held collateral substitute — a genuinely accumulative
asset that no individual holds.

**Failure mode of condition 3 — Maharashtra sugar cooperatives.** *(Verbatim excerpts not yet
obtained — both the IGIDR and TISS PDFs failed to fetch on 2026-08-21. The characterisation below
is from search-result summaries and must be quote-checked against Baviskar and the IGIDR draft
before it is used in anything outward-facing.)* Political control has been
characteristic since the first cooperative mill in the 1950s. Marathas hold decisive dominance
in the cooperatives, the panchayat bodies, the ruling party and through it the state government,
and the cooperatives *sharpened* socio-economic stratification within the Maratha cluster rather
than reducing it — only some clans, particularly those near cities, reproduce the privileged
position. Anchored and accumulative, and captured anyway. See also Baviskar, "Co-operatives and
Caste in Maharashtra."

**BAMCEF — the receiving-institution proof.** Founded 1971 by Kanshi Ram, D.K. Khaparde, Ram
Khobragade and Dinabhana; formally launched 6 December 1978 (Ambedkar's death anniversary). An
organisation of educated employees from oppressed communities, chosen deliberately because they
had stable incomes and organisational skill; funded on a 2%-of-income ask — *"pay back to
society."* Led to DS4 and then the BSP. **The conversion into a party is the moment the asset
stopped compounding**: office-holding is anchored but not accumulative, so the base did not build
a balance sheet.

## Open, to resolve next

1. **Is condition 3 achievable, or does capture always win?** Kudumbashree and Amul look like
   the two designs that resist it. Isolate what does the work — member-level decision rights?
   non-tradeable shares? a federated tier that can't be bought out? Cross-check Nadar, Lingayat,
   Vokkaliga and Maratha trusts for what failed.
2. **Every caste association that succeeded already had a trading or landholding surplus to
   start from.** Nadars had toddy-trade capital, Marwaris had trade. If the input is a
   pre-existing surplus, what substitutes for it for a landless SC population? Kudumbashree's
   answer is aggregated micro-thrift — check whether that scales to institution ownership or
   caps at working capital.
3. **What is the smallest sufficient owner?** Root `CLAUDE.md` requires naming who owns the
   object. For an owned school, is it a society, a producer company, a Section 8, a trust? The
   legal form determines whether the claim is concentrable.
4. **The learning-signal business.** Does a ward-level learning number have a payer? Candidates:
   state SCERT, a municipal body under a performance mandate, or parents directly. Test against
   the payer-identity axis in root `CLAUDE.md`.
5. **Reconcile the landlessness series properly** against a single definition before any of these
   numbers is used in an outward-facing draft.
6. **Does teaching-at-level survive community ownership?** TaRL and Mindspark are both delivered
   by outside organisations with their own assessment discipline. If a community-owned school runs
   it, does the instructional rigour hold, or does local governance revert to grade-based
   teaching and rote pass-rate optimisation? The Nadar colleges are the place to check.
7. **Verify the Young Lives constant-gap vs widening-gap papers directly.** ScienceDirect (403)
   and the Cambridge repository (504) both blocked verbatim retrieval on 2026-08-21; the
   characterisations here come from search summaries. Confirm whether the difference is
   vocabulary-vs-achievement or metric choice before relying on either.
