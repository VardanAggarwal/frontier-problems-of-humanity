# Water / Fluid Balance — Tier 1 Physiological

> Potable water in sufficient quantity, and the aquifers and systems that deliver it.

Humans die of dehydration in 3-5 days without water. Unlike food, there is almost no storage buffer in the body. This makes water threats faster-acting and more politically volatile than food threats.

## How this need has been threatened

### Drought / climate-driven scarcity
- **c. 2200 BCE, Akkadian Empire collapse** — a multi-century megadrought across northern Mesopotamia (documented in sediment cores from the Gulf of Oman) is linked to the collapse of the Akkadian state and mass abandonment of settlements (Cullen et al., *Geology*, 2000).
- **c. 850-1150 CE, "Great Drought," American Southwest** — tree-ring records show sustained drought that drove Ancestral Pueblo depopulation of Chaco Canyon (Benson et al., *Kiva*, 2007).
- **1876-78, Global "El Niño" famine drought** — failed monsoons hit India, China, Brazil, and Ethiopia simultaneously, killing an estimated 30-50 million people from thirst and famine combined (Mike Davis, *Late Victorian Holocausts*, 2001).
- **1930s Dust Bowl, US Great Plains** — decade-long drought plus soil mismanagement dried up wells and rivers, displacing roughly 2.5 million people (Worster, *Dust Bowl*, 1979).
- **2011-2017 Cape Town/Day Zero and 2015-2018 São Paulo crisis** — modern megacities came within weeks of taps running dry from reservoir depletion tied to drought and population growth.
- **2006-2011 Syrian drought** — a record-breaking multi-year drought destroyed 75% of farms in some regions and is widely cited as a contributing pressure to the pre-civil-war rural-to-urban migration (Kelley et al., *PNAS*, 2015).

### Contamination / waterborne disease
- **1854, Broad Street cholera outbreak, London** — over 600 deaths in days from a single contaminated pump, traced by John Snow.
- **1892, Hamburg cholera epidemic** — untreated Elbe River water killed over 8,600 people while neighboring Altona, which filtered its water, was largely spared — the clearest natural experiment for filtration's effect.
- **1908-present, typhoid and cholera in colonial and post-colonial cities** — recurring epidemics in Kolkata, Cairo, and Lagos tied to open sewers mixing with drinking supplies.
- **2010-present, Haiti cholera epidemic** — introduced by UN peacekeepers after the 2010 earthquake; killed nearly 10,000 people over the following decade due to damaged sanitation infrastructure (PAHO/CDC reports).
- **India, Swachh Bharat Mission (2014-present) as a rare partial success** — a UNICEF-commissioned independent study found non-open-defecation-free villages had 11.25x more fecal contamination in groundwater than ODF villages, a real measured gain; but India missed its October 2019 open-defecation-free target and behavior-change lagged the infrastructure build-out, so the contamination-reduction effect is real but incomplete.
- **2014-2019, Flint, Michigan water crisis** — a cost-cutting switch to the corrosive Flint River as a water source leached lead from pipes into the public supply, poisoning thousands of children.

### Siege / war weaponization of water
- **701 BCE, Assyrian siege of Jerusalem** — King Hezekiah dug the Siloam Tunnel to secure water inside the city walls in anticipation of the siege, a defensive countermeasure to well-cutting tactics.
- **1189-1191, Siege of Acre (Third Crusade)** — both sides fought over control of wells and aqueducts feeding the besieged city.
- **1503, Machiavelli/Leonardo da Vinci plan to divert the Arno River** to cut off Pisa's water and undermine the city during the Florentine-Pisan war (partially executed, ultimately failed).
- **1938, Chinese Nationalist destruction of Yellow River dikes** at Huayuankou to slow the Japanese invasion — flooded farmland and killed hundreds of thousands, later poisoning wells with saltwater intrusion downstream.
- **1991 Gulf War and 2003 Iraq War** — allied bombing of water treatment plants, and later ISIS's 2014-2015 seizure of the Fallujah Dam and Mosul Dam to control flow to downstream populations, are documented cases of dams and water infrastructure used as military leverage.
- **2022-present, Russia-Ukraine war** — destruction of the Kakhovka Dam (June 2023) cut irrigation and drinking water to over a million people in southern Ukraine and Crimea.

### Infrastructure collapse
- **537 CE onward, decline of Roman aqueducts** — after the Gothic War siege of Rome, aqueducts were cut, and the city's population collapsed as it lost piped water, reverting largely to Tiber River use for centuries.
- **1854, Rome's Cloaca Maxima and aqueduct disrepair** cited as a driver of malaria/typhoid persistence in the Papal States pre-unification.
- **2000s-present, urban water-loss ("non-revenue water")** — cities like Karachi and Lagos lose 30-40%+ of piped water to leakage and theft, functionally recreating scarcity despite adequate source supply (World Bank data).
- **2019, Newark, New Jersey lead crisis** — aging pipe infrastructure and corrosion-control failure paralleled Flint.

### Distributional / political failure
- **c. 1790s BCE, Code of Hammurabi water-rights clauses** — some of the earliest written law codifies penalties for negligent irrigation flooding a neighbor's field, showing water disputes as an ancient legal category.
- **1922, Colorado River Compact** (US) — allocated river flow between seven states based on overestimated historical flow data; the resulting overallocation still drives conflict a century later as reservoirs like Lake Mead hit record lows (2021-2022).
- **1960, Indus Waters Treaty tensions** — pre-treaty disputes between India and Pakistan over irrigation canals crossing the new 1947 partition border nearly triggered water cutoffs to Pakistani farmland.
- **1967-present, Golan Heights and Jordan River water disputes** — control of headwaters was a stated factor in Israeli-Syrian tensions preceding the Six-Day War (Wolf, *Hydropolitics along the Jordan River*, 1995).
- **Nile Basin disputes, 1929/1959 treaties vs. Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (2011-2023)** — Egypt and Sudan's colonial-era claims to nearly all Nile flow clashed with Ethiopia's dam construction, producing a decade of unresolved brinkmanship.
- **2000s Bolivia "Water Wars" (Cochabamba, 2000)** — mass protests forced reversal of water utility privatization after price spikes made water unaffordable for poor households.
- **India's interstate river tribunals** — the Cauvery dispute (Karnataka vs. Tamil Nadu) took 17 years (1990-2007) to reach a tribunal award, then further Supreme Court litigation through 2018; the Mahanadi Water Disputes Tribunal (constituted 2018) still hadn't ruled 7+ years later, now on its third chair; the Krishna Water Disputes Tribunal-II's Telangana-Andhra Pradesh decision remains un-notified — showing India's water-tribunal system as a chronic, multi-decade failure mode rather than a resolution mechanism.
- **India's urban "water mafia"/tanker economy** — in Delhi, illegal tanker operators seize government borewells (~2,000 illegal tankers/day), coinciding with the water table crashing from 60ft to 250ft depth over a decade; Chennai and Bengaluru show similar tanker cartels (with alleged official collusion in Chennai) charging ₹500-2,000/load in pipeline-less areas — privatized black-market water filling a public-infrastructure gap.

### Salinization / desertification
- **c. 1700 BCE, southern Mesopotamia (Sumer)** — millennia of irrigation without adequate drainage salinized fields, contributing to agricultural decline and southward population shift documented in cuneiform records (Jacobsen & Adams, *Science*, 1958).
- **1960s-present, Aral Sea desiccation** — Soviet-era diversion of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers for cotton irrigation shrank the sea by over 90%, salinizing regional groundwater and causing respiratory and health crises across Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan.
- **Contemporary Indus and Ganges delta, Bangladesh** — sea-level rise and upstream damming drive saltwater intrusion into coastal aquifers, degrading drinking water for millions.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **c. 2600 BCE, Indus Valley Civilization (Mohenjo-daro)** — one of the earliest known systems of household wells, covered drains, and public baths, showing engineered sanitation predating Rome by two millennia.
- **c. 1000-500 BCE, Persian qanats** — gently sloping underground tunnels tapped aquifers and carried water by gravity across arid Iran and beyond without evaporation loss; the technology spread across the Middle East, North Africa, and into Spain.
- **c. 800 CE onward, Indian stepwells (baoli/vav)** — architecturally elaborate multi-story wells in Gujarat and Rajasthan stored monsoon water and buffered seasonal scarcity for centuries.
- **312 BCE onward, Roman aqueducts** — Aqua Appia and ten successive aqueducts brought water tens of kilometers to Rome via engineered gradient, arches, and lead piping, supporting a population of over a million.
- **1854, John Snow's cholera map** — spatial epidemiology tracing the Broad Street outbreak to a single pump established the waterborne theory of disease transmission and launched modern epidemiology.
- **1858-1875, London's "Great Stink" and Bazalgette's sewer system** — engineered a citywide sewage network separating waste from drinking supply after cholera and stench crises, a model copied across industrializing cities.
- **1908, Jersey City, NJ** — first continuous chlorination of a US public water supply, followed by widespread municipal adoption that cut typhoid mortality by over 90% in treated cities within decades.
- **1848 (UK) onward, Public Health Acts** — legislated minimum sanitation and water-supply standards, precedents for WHO/UN drinking-water guidelines today.
- **1960, Indus Waters Treaty** — brokered by the World Bank between India and Pakistan, dividing the six Indus rivers between the two countries; it has survived multiple wars between the signatories, making it one of history's most durable water-sharing agreements.
- **1959/1929 Nile treaties and ongoing Nile Basin Initiative (1999)** — attempts, imperfect, at multilateral river governance among riparian African states.
- **1997, UN Convention on the Law of the Non-Navigational Uses of International Watercourses** — codified customary international law on equitable and reasonable use of shared rivers, entering into force in 2014.
- **Riparian rights vs. prior appropriation doctrine** — English/Eastern-US common law (riparian: landowners bordering water share rights) versus Western-US "first in time, first in right" prior appropriation, two competing legal frameworks for allocating scarce freshwater that still structure water law today.
- **1959, first commercial desalination plants (Kuwait, Saudi Arabia)** and subsequent reverse-osmosis technology (1960s-70s) — let arid, oil-wealthy Gulf states decouple population growth from local freshwater limits; Israel's National Water Carrier and desalination program similarly turned a water-scarce state into a net water exporter by the 2010s.
- **Rainwater harvesting traditions** — from Roman impluvium/cistern systems, to Indian *johad* check-dams and Rajasthani *tankas*, to modern mandated rooftop-harvesting codes (e.g., Chennai post-2001 legislation) — decentralized, low-tech buffering against seasonal and drought-driven scarcity.
- **2010, UN General Assembly Resolution 64/292** — formally recognized access to clean water and sanitation as a human right, reframing water access as a legal/political entitlement rather than a purely infrastructural or charitable matter.

### Where it worked

- **Gujarat's Jyotirgram Yojana, 2003–06 — the one intervention in this tier that fixes two needs at once.** Gujarat spent ~US$290 million separating agricultural electricity feeders from domestic ones, then gave villages 24-hour metered three-phase power while tubewell owners received **eight hours a day of full-voltage supply on a pre-announced daytime schedule**. Groundwater withdrawal fell roughly **37%** (15.7 → 9.9 billion units/year, 2001–2006) and farm power subsidies roughly halved.
- Two things make this the most important positive control in the tier. First, it demonstrates that groundwater draw responds to *how* power is supplied rather than to price alone — rationing quality-of-supply worked where tariffs had failed politically for decades. Second, the same intervention is the fix for the night-irrigation sleep failure in [sleep](/need/sleep) (§India) — daytime scheduling was adopted here as a byproduct of metering discipline, not as a welfare measure, and it removed the need for farmers to irrigate at night.
- **Write the disagreement, not a verdict.** The withdrawal reduction is well documented; whether aquifer *storage* recovered is contested — district-level analysis 1996–2011 finds storage continuing to decline despite the scheme. The honest claim is that Jyotirgram changed the extraction rate and the farmer's working day, not that it reversed depletion.
- **Alternate wetting and drying in paddy — the remedy for the largest single draw in this file, working at scale, and not in India.** The Punjab/Haryana entry below names rice-paddy cultivation as the driver of tube-well depletion. The agronomic remedy for that draw is settled: alternate wetting and drying (AWD) and direct-seeded rice (DSR) stop the continuous flooding of the field. **Rize** (Singapore-headquartered, founded by Dhruv Sawhney, who also runs `nurture-farm`'s Punjab stubble programme) has put agronomists and an MRV stack behind exactly that switch for **17,000–18,000 smallholders across ~50,000 hectares in Vietnam and Indonesia**, funded by a $31M Series B (2026) and targeting 300,000 ha by 2030. Farmers are not charged and are reported to gain ~30% income with no yield loss.
- **What makes it belong in this file is who pays, and it is not a water buyer.** Rize's revenue is **methane-avoidance carbon credits** — flooded paddy is a major methane source, and that is the quantity with a market. The water saving is a co-benefit riding along, unpriced and, in what has been found so far, unquantified: no verified figure for the irrigation draw avoided per hectare is available from the company or its coverage, and one should not be assumed from the AWD literature. **Absence of that number is itself the finding** — the same shape as the missing blue-water figure for ethanol and the missing aquifer-draw disclosure for data centres below, and the same shape as the ZLD-vs-CGWA contrast above. Methane manufactured a payer for a practice that saves water; water could not manufacture one for itself.
- **Held as a positive control elsewhere, not an Indian result.** Rize has no India operations as of 2026-09-09, so this is evidence that the practice and its financing both work, not evidence that they transfer to Punjab — where the binding constraints are free farm power and MSP procurement, neither of which Vietnamese or Indonesian paddy faces. The transfer question is open and worth a leaf when one is written. Actor record: `problems/actors/rize.md`.
- **Guinea worm eradication, certified 2000.** India eliminated dracunculiasis — a purely water-borne parasite — through case containment, filtration and water-source conversion. Distinct from the chlorination story because it required no treatment infrastructure at all, only interrupting one transmission route.

## Where this fails today

- **Mexico City, 2024-2025** — reservoirs feeding the Cutzamala system fell below 40% capacity in early 2024; officials warned of an approaching "Day Zero" for parts of the metro area, and rationing/truck deliveries (*pipas*) became routine for millions, per Reuters and *The Guardian* reporting through 2024.
- **Chennai, 2019 and recurring** — the city's four main reservoirs hit zero storage in June 2019, forcing dependence on tanker trucks and train-delivered water; Chennai's monsoon-dependent supply remains structurally fragile into the 2020s, with IT-corridor growth outpacing groundwater recharge (CGWB reports).
- **Bengaluru, 2023-2024** — a weak monsoon plus over-extraction of borewells (many running dry below 1,000+ feet) triggered an acute crisis in early 2024, with the city trucking in water and capping borewell drilling; Karnataka's Cauvery Water Regulation Committee cited "severe" shortfall.
- **Cape Town** — the 2018 near-Day-Zero crisis reshaped municipal policy, but WWF and city reports through the 2020s note dam levels remain volatile and demand-management measures (pressure reduction, tiered tariffs) are now permanent, treating scarcity as the ongoing baseline rather than a one-off event.
- **Gaza, 2023-2025** — UNICEF and OCHA reported that Israeli bombardment and siege since October 2023 destroyed or damaged the majority of Gaza's water and sanitation infrastructure (desalination plants, wells, pipelines), with UN agencies estimating over 80-90% of water/sanitation facilities non-functional by 2024, driving disease outbreaks amid daily supply far below WHO minimums (roughly 3 liters/person/day at points, versus a 15L emergency benchmark).
- **Ogallala Aquifer, US Great Plains** — USGS and Kansas Geological Survey data through 2023 show parts of the aquifer, which took millennia to fill, have declined 40-70% since large-scale pumping began in the 1950s, with some Texas/Kansas sections projected to become largely unusable for irrigation within decades at current pumping rates.
- **North India (Punjab/Haryana) groundwater**, 2022-2024 — NASA GRACE satellite data and CGWB reports show continued rapid water-table decline (roughly 0.3-1 meter/year in parts of Punjab), driven by free/subsidized electricity for tube wells and rice-paddy cultivation incentivized by MSP procurement policy.
- **Flint, Michigan** — a 2024 EPA/court-monitored assessment found the city had not fully completed pipe replacement promised after 2014-2019, with the consent decree's final compliance deadlines slipping repeatedly; residents' trust in tap water remains measurably low a decade on (AP, 2024 coverage).
- **Jackson, Mississippi, 2022** — catastrophic treatment-plant failure after flooding left the majority-Black capital city (~150,000 people) without reliable water pressure for weeks; a federal takeover (third-party manager) followed, and boil-water advisories recurred into 2023-2024.
- **Nile/GERD dispute, 2020-2025** — Ethiopia completed and inaugurated the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam in 2025 without a binding filling/operation agreement with Egypt and Sudan; talks brokered by the African Union have repeatedly stalled, leaving the basin's largest unresolved transboundary water conflict still live.
- **Indus Waters Treaty, 2023-2025** — India issued formal notices to Pakistan seeking treaty modification (2023) and then announced it was "holding in abeyance" the 65-year-old treaty following the April 2025 Pahalgam attack, marking the first time a signatory has suspended participation — an unprecedented rupture in what had been called history's most durable water-sharing agreement.
- **Mekong River, 2020s** — Mekong River Commission and Stimson Center satellite monitoring (Eyes on Earth) continue to document upstream Chinese dam operations altering flow and trapping sediment, worsening drought and fisheries collapse for downstream Cambodia, Laos, and Vietnam.
- **Himalayan/glacier-fed rivers** — the 2023 ICIMOD "Water, Ice, Society, Ecosystems" report projected Hindu Kush Himalaya glaciers could lose up to 80% of volume by 2100 under high-emission scenarios, threatening the long-term flow reliability of the Indus, Ganges, and Brahmaputra basins that support roughly 2 billion people.
- **US aging infrastructure** — the EPA's 2023 Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey estimated $625 billion is needed over 20 years to maintain and upgrade US drinking-water systems; the ASCE 2025 Infrastructure Report Card again graded US drinking water a "C-," citing a water-main break roughly every two minutes nationwide.
- **The 2023-24 El Niño — the same monsoon deficit behind Chennai and Bengaluru's crises above also broke through as a global food-price shock, not just a local supply one.** India's pre-emptive rice-export bans (citing El Niño risk) pushed global rice prices up over 20%. The crop-yield and price mechanism — India's kharif acreage response, the 2002/2009/2018-19 historical spread (2009 held as an open disagreement rather than resolved), and a finding that the state's ENSO forecast (IMD's live bulletin) is not yet wired to an anticipatory buffer-stock or import trigger — is developed in [food](/need/food)'s India: El Niño/ENSO-driven crop and price shocks section, to avoid duplicating it here.

### India: groundwater, contamination, and dam displacement

- **Groundwater depletion from irrigation + urban use** — India extracts ~241 billion m³/yr of groundwater, over 25% of the global total; agriculture is 89% of that draw, domestic use 9%. Punjab water tables are falling up to 46 cm/yr in places; Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan combined lost 88 million acre-feet of groundwater over the last decade (NASA GRACE satellite data).
- **Industrial pollution + poor sewage management** — the Yamuna receives ~800 million liters/day of untreated sewage plus 44 million L/day of industrial effluent (Delhi's stretch alone via 18 drains, dissolved oxygen often at 0 mg/L). The Ganga basin generates 8,250 MLD of wastewater against only 3,500 MLD of treatment capacity — roughly 2,550 MLD discharged raw, despite ₹30,458 crore spent under Namami Gange. Of 353 Namami Gange projects launched, only 178 are complete; the Yamuna remains "biologically dead" through Delhi. CPCB's 2025 report counts 296 polluted river stretches nationwide.
- **Seasonal acute urban shortage** — already covered in the global list above (Chennai 2019 zero-reservoir crisis, Bengaluru 2023-24 borewell crisis).
- **Dam/canal displacement — human cost** — Sardar Sarovar Dam alone displaced 200,000-250,000 people (41,000+ families, 56% Adivasi), decades later still without full, intact resettlement — the case that produced the Narmada Bachao Andolan, India's most prominent anti-dam movement.
- **Dam/canal displacement — ecological cost** — India is the world's third-largest dam-owning nation (4,408 large dams per ICOLD; 5,700+ by some counts), so this is a *recurring structural cost*, not a per-project anomaly: every new project reproduces the same bundle of harms.
  - *Forest submergence* — the Indira Sagar and Omkareshwar projects alone caused irreversible loss of 45,609 ha of forest; submergence converts pristine, often contiguous forest into reservoir.
  - *River fragmentation and fish collapse* — dams break longitudinal river connectivity, blocking migration to feeding and spawning grounds. Documented in India on the Bhagirathi and Bhilangana (post-Tehri dam species-diversity decline); fast-flowing riverine species have collapsed or disappeared entirely from fragmented sections. Seven hydropower stations on the Teesta and its tributaries have altered downstream flora and fauna.
  - *Regime conversion* — reservoirs convert free-flowing (lotic) upstream stretches into static (lentic) impoundments, changing physico-chemical properties; deep-release outflows also alter downstream water temperature, affecting aquatic life.
  - *Sediment starvation of deltas* — the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta depends on ~1 billion tonnes/yr of sediment to offset relative sea-level rise. Planned dams and diversions could cut sediment delivery 15-80% (up to 21% for the GBM under currently-planned dams alone), and proposed operations could trap 30-50% of upstream sediment while cutting dry-season flow into Bangladesh 10-30%. This links directly to the saltwater-intrusion entry in *Salinization* above — dam-building upstream is a *cause* of delta drinking-water degradation, not a separate problem.
  - *Reservoir sedimentation as self-defeat* — the trapped sediment also destroys the dams' own utility: by 2050 at least 10 east-flowing and 7 west-flowing Indian reservoirs may cross a 50% storage-loss threshold, concentrated in the Himalaya, Narmada-Tapi, Western Ghats, and Indo-Gangetic plains.
  - *Greenhouse gas release* — reservoirs are a documented methane/GHG source, making large hydropower's climate credentials weaker than its "clean energy" framing implies.
- **Arsenic and fluoride contamination** (not previously in this file) — ~70 million people exposed to arsenic above 10 μg/L in groundwater (69% of cases in West Bengal); on fluoride, roughly 411 million people live in 201 fluoride-endemic districts across 20 of 29 states — but that is a residency figure, not an exposure one: an estimated ~66 million people actually suffer from fluorosis (including ~6 million children under 14), with other estimates putting the at-risk population near 120 million (~9%). Worst-affected are Rajasthan, Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, and the southern states of Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu.
- **Data centres: a new demand class arriving with no allocation instrument.** India had roughly **1.5 GW of operational data-centre capacity at end-2025**, heading to **8–10 GW by 2030** (Deloitte). At the 25 million litres per MW per year figure used by Karnataka's IT ministry that is ~37.5 billion litres a year now, rising six-fold. Sources disagree on the level by an order of magnitude and the disagreement is worth preserving: other 2026 reporting puts 2025 consumption at ~150 billion litres rising to ~358 billion by 2030, a gap that reflects different assumptions about cooling type and whether embedded power-generation water is counted. What is not disputed is the siting. **Mumbai and Chennai took 70% of 2025 capacity absorption** (Savills India) — Chennai being the city whose reservoirs hit near-zero in 2019 (above) — and roughly **75% of Indian data centres sit in water-stressed regions**, with Hyderabad facing a projected **870 MLD deficit by 2027**. Evaporative cooling dominates and roughly **80% of the water evaporates**, making this consumptive draw rather than the return-flow use that industrial water accounting generally assumes.
- **The regulatory gap is the point, not the volume.** Data centres are classified as IT/ITeS, which routes them around the standard environmental-impact assessment; no state policy requires water-stress mapping as a clearance condition, MoEFCC requires no thermal impact assessment, and no agency requires disclosure of how much a facility draws from a shared aquifer. Peri-urban siting puts them on the same groundwater as adjacent farming communities who have no mechanism to learn the number. This is the tier's clearest live instance of **instrument keyed to the wrong object**: the entity is regulated as software and consumes as heavy industry. It is also the cleanest test of whether a demand class can be brought inside a water budget *before* the deficit arrives rather than after — every other entry in this file is a retrofit.
- **Ethanol: a water demand created deliberately, by target.** India reached **E20 blending in 2025, ahead of schedule**, and is working toward **E27 by 2030**, which needs upward of 1,200 crore litres a year. Installed capacity is already ~**1,900 crore litres/yr** — roughly 900 from sugar feedstock and 1,000 from grain. The feedstock composition is the part that matters for this file: **grain-based ethanol rose from under 12% of supply in 2019 to ~48%**, and production is concentrated in **Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and Karnataka**, three of the states this file already names for depletion. Every other demand class here grew and was then discovered; this one was commissioned, with a published schedule for growing further.
- **The per-litre water figure is disputed across three orders of magnitude, and the dispute is the finding.** Three numbers are in public circulation for the water cost of one litre of ethanol: ~**3–5 litres** (the grain-ethanol producers' association, GEMA); ~**1,634–2,469 litres** for sugarcane and ~**4,500** for maize (an ISMA–ICAR study, ISMA being the sugar mills' association); and ~**3,630 litres for sugarcane, 4,670 for maize, 10,790 for rice** in the figures critics cite. These are not competing measurements of one quantity. They are three different **system boundaries** — distillery process water only; crop plus process; and total crop water footprint including rainfall — and each party has selected the boundary that favours it. The 10,790 figure is broadly right *for rice* and roughly a 3–5× overstatement applied to sugarcane molasses; the 3–5 litre figure excludes the crop entirely, which is where essentially all the water is. **No agency publishes the number that would settle it — the blue-water (irrigation and groundwater) draw attributable to ethanol feedstock.** That absence is the same shape as the missing aquifer-draw disclosure for data centres above, arrived at from the opposite direction: there, nobody measures; here, three parties measure and each picks a boundary.
- **The one figure that does not depend on the boundary is a ratio, and it is severe.** In Maharashtra — a largely drought-prone rain-shadow state — **sugarcane occupies ~6% of cultivable area and takes ~70% of the state's irrigation demand.** That is blue water, measured as a share, and it is unaffected by whether rainfall is counted. It is also the clearest instance in this file of the *primary vs derivative burden* mechanism: the state's water politics are fought over dams, canals and tanker supply, while a single crop on a fifteenth of the land moves two-thirds of the irrigation.
- **The effluent side is a second, independent route.** Molasses distilleries generate **8–15 litres of spent wash (vinasse) per litre of ethanol** — a high-COD, high-nitrogen, high-phosphate stream that eutrophies receiving water. CPCB classifies distilleries **Red category** and mandates **Zero Liquid Discharge**. The mandate is the right instrument; whether ZLD compliance scales with a sector expanding to meet E27 is untested, and the pattern to watch for is the tier's **second half never built** — the treatment obligation written and the capacity not funded, exactly as with sewage above.
- **Farmer suicides tied to groundwater depletion/irrigation debt** — satellite data shows a 2-year lag correlation between groundwater decline and suicide rates; Maharashtra alone recorded 60,750 farmer suicides 1995-2013, with groundwater falling 1-2m/yr in 25 taluks over 5 years. The mechanism is a debt trap: sunk cost in failed borewells pushes farmers into riskier cash crops (chilly, cotton) to recoup losses. Telangana and Andhra Pradesh show the same pattern.

### India: who is already commercially working on this, and who pays them

Required by the commercial-landscape pass.

- **Household RO is the largest private water market in India and it treats the wrong end of the pipe.** The point-of-use purifier market runs **$1.5–3.5B a year (2024–25) heading to $7–7.7B by 2032–34**, with **Kent, Eureka Forbes (Aquaguard), HUL Pureit, Livpure, A.O. Smith and Havells holding roughly 65%** between them — all large, profitable, none distressed. It is the healthiest commercial layer in this file and it does nothing about the 61%-vs-28% treatment gap upstream; it also generates an unaddressed reject-water stream of its own.
- **The hypothesis worth holding about that, stated as a hypothesis:** every household that installs an RO exits the constituency that would otherwise demand functioning municipal treatment. Private filtration absorbs the visible harm — bad water at the tap — before it can escalate politically, which would make a thriving purifier market a *cause* of continued public under-investment rather than merely a response to it. This is plausible and not demonstrated; the alternative reading, that purifiers are a rational private hedge with no effect on public demand, is equally consistent with the same facts. Naming it because it is testable and nobody appears to have tested it.
- **Arsenic and fluoride have no durable commercial layer at all.** Against roughly **70 million arsenic-exposed and 66–120 million at risk of fluorosis**, what exists is DIY and academic — IIT Kharagpur's Nilogon and Arsiron Nilogon filters — plus patchy state-run centralised plants. No venture-scale player. The population is rural, poor and dispersed, so per-litre RO or water-ATM economics do not clear, and unlike industrial effluent **no compliance mandate exists to manufacture a third-party payer**. This is the file's clearest case of a severe, irreversible, well-characterised harm with no wallet attached to it.
- **ZLD is the counter-case, and it is the important one.** CPCB's Red-category classification and consent-to-operate regime built an entire industrial effluent-treatment sector — **Ion Exchange, Thermax, VA Tech Wabag, L&T Water, Aquatech, Gradiant, Veolia**, with AMRUT 2.0 carrying **₹500 cr in ZLD incentives**. Polluters have no independent incentive to treat; the mandate created the customer, and the industry followed. Regulation here did not merely restrain a market, it *constituted* one.
- **The contrast within the same file is the finding.** **CGWA groundwater-extraction permits exist on paper and have manufactured no comparable market** — no metering-as-a-service, no compliance-auditing sector, nothing resembling what ZLD produced. Two instruments, one harm each, one built an industry and one did not. Whatever distinguishes them is worth more than another shortlist row, and the likely difference is that ZLD attaches to a small number of identifiable, inspectable, permit-dependent facilities while extraction is diffuse across millions of tubewells.
- **Sewage treatment is a real business whose customer is a ministry.** VA Tech Wabag holds a **₹1,187 cr Patna STP contract with a 15-year O&M tail (NMCG-funded, 40% grant)**, roughly ₹600 cr of BWSSB Bengaluru orders and ₹360 cr from GAIL and IOCL. Capacity gets built where a central grant programme pays for it, on DBO/BOOT/HAM structures — which explains why the *second half never built* problem persists selectively: the EPC gets funded and the recurring O&M depends on a municipal balance sheet that often cannot carry it.
- **Irrigation efficiency is subsidy-shaped, and one vendor has said so commercially.** **Jain Irrigation** carries **₹5,790 cr FY25 revenue with net profit down 64% to ₹33.5 cr**, after a ~₹2,000 cr restructuring in 2019 and with a subsidiary now facing NCLT petitions. **Netafim India launched a subsidy-free drip line ("Toofan") in 2024 specifically to route around PMKSY disbursement delays** — a vendor redesigning its product because the state's payment cycle, not the farmer's demand, was the binding constraint. That is a precise, dated instance of the payer being the problem.
- **Sensing sells to industry, not to aquifer managers.** **FluxGen (~$4M raised)** sells water-intelligence SaaS to factories and hospitals pursuing cost and ESG outcomes. **Uravu Labs (~$3.5M+)** sells atmospheric water generation to corporate and hospitality clients. Both are technically adjacent to the failures above and priced for exactly the wrong user — the technology for water-scarce households exists and is sold to breweries.
- **Community water is philanthropy-shaped.** **Piramal Sarvajal** (CSR-funded via Nestlé, Tata Motors, HSBC, the Dell Foundation) and **WaterHealth International** (500+ centres) reach bottom-of-pyramid users through franchise models, but neither is a venture-scaled business and both depend on external capital rather than customer revenue alone. Recorded as the honest middle case: the harmed population *can* be reached, but so far only where someone else pays for the reaching.

### India: stored risk, not yet realised

- **China's upstream control of the Brahmaputra (Yarlung Tsangpo)** — the mirror of the Nile/GERD dynamic above, but with India downstream this time. No water-sharing treaty exists between India and China; China approved a mega-dam project (2024) upstream of Arunachal Pradesh. This is the least-covered "siege/war weaponization" risk in India's basket.
- **Full Indus Waters Treaty collapse** — already "held in abeyance" since April 2025; the failure mode is India actually restricting flow to Pakistan, historically one of the more reliable interstate-war triggers (cf. Jordan River case above).
- **Himalayan glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs)** — distinct from slow glacier decline: sudden catastrophic floods. 2021 Chamoli (Uttarakhand) disaster is a preview of this failure mode recurring as glaciers destabilize.
- **Groundwater exhaustion crossing an irreversible line** — some Punjab/Haryana aquifers are fossil water that won't recharge on human timescales; the risk is agricultural collapse plus mass rural-to-urban migration once wells go dry, not a gradual adjustment.
- **Pharmaceutical-industrial antibiotic pollution** — Hyderabad's Musi River/Patancheru pharma belt shows antibiotic concentrations in surface water high enough to be linked to antibiotic-resistant bacteria emergence, a contamination mode distinct from generic sewage with global public-health spillover.
- **Coastal aquifer saltwater intrusion** — the same mechanism already noted for the Bangladesh delta applies to Mumbai, Kolkata, and Chennai groundwater as sea levels rise and extraction continues.
- **Day Zero spreading beyond Chennai/Bengaluru** — Delhi and Hyderabad show the same reservoir/borewell stress pattern, raising the risk that acute urban water crisis becomes routine across most large Indian metros rather than an isolated event.
- **Flood-drought whiplash** — increasingly erratic monsoons (severe wet years alternating with severe drought) are stressing infrastructure built for a more stable historical rainfall pattern, a distinct failure mode from steady drought.
