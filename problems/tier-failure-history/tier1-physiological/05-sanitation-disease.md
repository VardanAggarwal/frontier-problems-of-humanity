# Sanitation & Disease — Tier 1 Physiological

> Separation of human waste from water, food and living space, and the disease load when it fails.

Short-horizon survival threat: pathogens (bacterial, viral, parasitic) exploiting density, filth, war, and contact between previously isolated populations.

## How this need has been threatened

- **1347–1351, Europe/Middle East — Black Death (plague, *Yersinia pestis*).** Killed an estimated 25–50 million in Europe, roughly one-third to one-half of the population, spread along Silk Road trade routes and by fleas on black rats. Recurred in waves for 300+ years (Great Plague of London, 1665, ~100,000 dead).
- **1520–1600, Americas — "virgin soil" smallpox/measles pandemics following European contact.** Smallpox introduced by Spanish contact killed an estimated 5–8 million in the Aztec Empire alone within a few years (1520–21 epidemic coincided with fall of Tenochtitlan); across the Americas, disease (smallpox, measles, typhus, influenza) is estimated to have killed 90%+ of indigenous populations over the following century — a demographic collapse on genocidal scale, whether or not deliberately caused, that some scholars term the deadliest event in human history.
- **1629–1631 and recurring, India/Deccan — famine-linked epidemics**, and more generally, cholera endemic to the Ganges delta for centuries before global spread (first pandemic wave began 1817).
- **1793, Philadelphia — yellow fever epidemic.** Killed roughly 5,000 of the city's 50,000 residents in a few months; exposed the new US capital's total absence of public health infrastructure.
- **1830s–1860s, industrializing Europe/UK — cholera pandemics in cities without sanitation.** London's 1854 Broad Street outbreak killed 616 in ten days; John Snow traced it to a single contaminated water pump, founding epidemiology. Paris, Hamburg, and New York suffered comparable epidemics; raw sewage flowing into drinking-water sources was standard practice.
- **1858, London — the Great Stink.** The Thames, an open sewer for a city of 2 million, became so foul that Parliament nearly evacuated — the proximate trigger for London's sewer system (below).
- **1853–1856, Crimean War — typhus, cholera, dysentery.** Of roughly 21,000 British deaths, more than 16,000 were from disease rather than combat, exposing appalling camp sanitation (Florence Nightingale's reforms followed directly).
- **1861–1865, US Civil War.** Disease killed roughly twice as many soldiers as combat — dysentery, typhoid, and camp fevers spread through unsanitary encampments and field hospitals.
- **1914–1918, WWI trenches and 1918–1922 refugee/POW camps — typhus and trench fever.** Lice-borne typhus killed an estimated 2–3 million in Eastern Europe and Russia in the years around WWI, particularly among refugees, POWs, and in the Russian Civil War; trench conditions were a mass incubator for dysentery and trench foot infection as well.
- **1918–1919, global — influenza pandemic ("Spanish flu").** Killed an estimated 50–100 million worldwide (2.5–5% of the global population), more than WWI itself, spread rapidly by wartime troop movements and inadequate public-health response.
- **1980s–present, global — HIV/AIDS.** Zoonotic spillover from primates (likely 1900s-era, identified 1981); has killed an estimated 42 million people to date and remains endemic in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, where lack of access to antiretrovirals sustained mass mortality through the 1990s–2000s before treatment scale-up.
- **Present day, developing world — endemic disease burden absent infrastructure.** Malaria kills roughly 600,000 people a year (mostly African children under 5); cholera causes an estimated 1.3–4 million cases and 21,000–143,000 deaths annually, concentrated where safe water/sanitation is absent (e.g., Yemen's 2016–2020 cholera epidemic, over 2.5 million suspected cases amid war-collapsed infrastructure); typhoid, dysentery, and other fecal-oral diseases remain leading child-mortality causes wherever open defecation and unsafe water persist — roughly 2 billion people still lacked safely managed sanitation as of recent UN/WHO estimates.
- **2010, Haiti — post-earthquake cholera outbreak.** Introduced (likely by UN peacekeepers) into a population with collapsed water infrastructure after the earthquake; infected over 800,000 and killed nearly 10,000, illustrating how disaster-driven sanitation collapse still causes epidemic disease in the 21st century.
- **2014–2016, West Africa — Ebola outbreak.** Zoonotic spillover (bats, likely via bushmeat) killed over 11,000 across Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone, overwhelming health systems with essentially no sanitation/isolation infrastructure for hemorrhagic fever.
- **2019–2023, global — COVID-19 pandemic.** Zoonotic-origin coronavirus; over 7 million confirmed deaths (excess-mortality estimates run to 15–20+ million), the largest global pandemic shock since 1918, exposing gaps in surveillance, hospital capacity, and international coordination even in wealthy states.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **14th century onward, Venice/Ragusa — quarantine and lazarettos.** Venice instituted 40-day isolation (*quarantena*) for ships in 1377 after plague; dedicated quarantine islands (lazarettos) followed across Mediterranean ports — the first systematic, institutionalized disease-control infrastructure, centuries before germ theory explained why it worked.
- **1796, England — Edward Jenner's smallpox vaccination.** Used cowpox exposure to confer smallpox immunity; became the template for vaccination as a technology, eventually adopted worldwide and mandated in many jurisdictions through the 19th century.
- **1847, Vienna — Ignaz Semmelweis and handwashing.** Semmelweis showed that doctors washing hands with chlorinated lime between deliveries cut maternal mortality from puerperal fever roughly tenfold; rejected by contemporaries, vindicated only after germ theory, but the origin of clinical hand hygiene norms.
- **1854–1858, London — John Snow's epidemiology and Bazalgette's sewers.** Snow's Broad Street investigation (1854) demonstrated waterborne transmission of cholera; the 1858 Great Stink forced Parliament to fund Joseph Bazalgette's London sewer network (completed through the 1870s), removing sewage from the drinking-water supply and effectively ending cholera epidemics in the city — the founding case for modern urban sanitation engineering.
- **1860s–1880s, France/Germany — germ theory (Pasteur, Koch).** Louis Pasteur's experiments (1860s) and Robert Koch's identification of specific bacterial pathogens (anthrax 1876, tuberculosis 1882, cholera 1883) established that specific microorganisms cause specific diseases, replacing miasma theory and grounding both hygiene practice and vaccine/antiseptic development in mechanism rather than empiricism.
- **1865, Glasgow — Joseph Lister and antisepsis.** Applied germ theory to surgery via carbolic acid sterilization, collapsing post-operative sepsis mortality and founding modern surgical hygiene.
- **1928–1940s — antibiotics.** Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin (1928), mass-produced for WWII wounded by the early 1940s, turned previously fatal bacterial infections (sepsis, pneumonia, wound infection) into treatable conditions — arguably the single largest mortality-reduction technology of the 20th century.
- **1851 onward — International Sanitary Conferences and the IHR.** Following repeated cholera pandemics, European powers began coordinating quarantine and reporting standards from 1851; this lineage led to the WHO's International Health Regulations (IHR, adopted 1969, revised 2005) — the current binding framework requiring states to report and coordinate on outbreaks of international concern (invoked for SARS, H1N1, Ebola, COVID-19).
- **1948 — founding of the WHO** as a permanent international body for disease surveillance, vaccination campaigns, and health-system coordination, replacing ad hoc sanitary conferences.
- **1959–1980 — WHO smallpox eradication campaign.** Using ring vaccination and surveillance-containment (rather than universal vaccination), smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980 — the only human disease eliminated entirely, saving an estimated 5 million lives per year in the decades since.
- **1988–present — Global Polio Eradication Initiative.** Cut polio cases by over 99% (from ~350,000/year in 1988 to a handful of wild-type cases today), using oral vaccine campaigns coordinated across nearly every country, though final eradication remains incomplete due to conflict-zone access and vaccine-derived strains.
- **20th century, global — piped water and sewage treatment as default urban infrastructure.** Chlorination (introduced early 1900s) and filtration of municipal water, combined with sewage treatment plants, made waterborne disease (cholera, typhoid) essentially eliminated in wealthy countries — a public-works response following directly from Snow/Bazalgette's model, still absent for roughly 2 billion people globally.
- **2000s–2020s — oral rehydration therapy (ORT) and rapid vaccine platforms.** ORT (a simple salt-sugar-water solution, developed 1960s–70s) cut child cholera/diarrheal deaths dramatically at negligible cost; mRNA vaccine platforms (used at scale from 2020 for COVID-19) demonstrated a new, much faster response cycle for novel pathogen outbreaks — vaccines developed and deployed within roughly a year of pathogen identification, versus a decade-plus historically.

### Where it worked

- **India certified polio-free, 2014.** In 2009 India recorded roughly half the world's polio cases; the last case was January 2011. Delivered through repeated mass immunisation rounds reaching ~170 million children, with surveillance to match — the single largest public-health logistics operation the country has run, and the strongest evidence that Indian state capacity can execute at national scale when a target is unambiguous and externally coordinated.
- **Tuberculosis incidence down 21%** over the 2015–2024 period, faster than the global rate of decline, even while India retains the world's largest absolute burden. A partial win worth naming precisely: incidence is falling, and India still accounts for ~25% of global cases.
- **Open defecation, genuinely reduced.** Whatever the disputes over ODF *declaration* documented below, the underlying trend is real — household sanitation access rose steeply after 2014, and the NFHS-5 measured change is large. The failure recorded below is that verification was replaced by self-declaration and that treatment was never built, not that nothing happened.
- These sit alongside the global set already listed above — smallpox eradication, ORT, chlorination — which makes this the tier's best-supplied file for positive controls, and the reason "sanitation is hard" is not a defensible reading of what follows.

## Where this fails today

- **2021–2025, global — cholera resurgence.** WHO recorded a sharp reversal of the long decline in cholera: case counts and case-fatality rates rose across more than 30 countries from 2021 onward, with global CFR exceeding the 1% emergency threshold for three straight years. Haiti's outbreak, first case October 2022, produced over 100,000 suspected cases in a country whose water infrastructure never recovered from 2010. Yemen's epidemic, active since 2016, continued generating tens of thousands of new cases a year amid ongoing war damage to water systems. Malawi's 2022–2023 outbreak was its worst on record, over 1,700 deaths. DRC has run continuous outbreaks tied to displacement camps and conflict in the east.
- **2023–2026, Gaza — sanitation infrastructure collapse.** Israeli bombardment and siege since October 2023 destroyed sewage treatment plants, water pipes, and desalination capacity; UNICEF and WHO reported raw sewage flowing through displacement camps and streets, and over a million cases of infectious disease (acute watery diarrhea, hepatitis A, skin infections) recorded among Gaza's roughly 2 million residents by mid-2024, a rate WHO called unprecedented for the population size and timeframe.
- **2022–2026, global — mpox and H5N1 as ongoing zoonotic threats.** WHO declared mpox a Public Health Emergency of International Concern twice: in July 2022 for the global clade IIb outbreak, and again in August 2024 for a more severe clade Ib strain spreading from DRC into neighboring African states. Separately, H5N1 avian influenza spread into US dairy cattle herds in 2024 for the first time, with dozens of confirmed human infections among farm workers by 2025 — raising sustained concern among virologists about mammalian adaptation and pandemic potential.
- **Ongoing, global — antimicrobial resistance (AMR) as a growing crisis.** The Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance (GRAM) project estimated AMR was directly responsible for 1.27 million deaths and associated with 4.95 million deaths in 2019; The Lancet's follow-up modeling projects AMR deaths could reach roughly 10 million a year by 2050 without intervention. WHO's updated Bacterial Priority Pathogens List (2024) flags drug-resistant *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Acinetobacter*, and others as critical threats with a thinning antibiotic pipeline behind them.
- **As of 2022 data, South Asia/sub-Saharan Africa — persistent open defecation.** The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme estimated 419 million people still practiced open defecation in 2022, concentrated in India, Nigeria, Ethiopia, and other high-burden countries, despite large gains from programs like India's Swachh Bharat Mission.
- **2022–2025, US/Europe — vaccine-preventable disease resurgence.** WHO and CDC reported measles cases rose roughly 79% globally in 2023 versus 2022, driven by falling vaccination coverage post-pandemic. The US saw its largest measles outbreak in over three decades in 2025, centered in West Texas, with multiple deaths among unvaccinated children — the first US measles deaths in a decade. European countries including the UK, Romania, and France reported similar multi-fold increases in measles cases across 2023–2024.
- **2020s ongoing — long COVID and pandemic-era health system strain.** WHO estimates tens of millions of people globally live with long COVID symptoms, and health systems in multiple countries report backlogs and workforce shortages that trace directly to pandemic-era overload, compounding capacity for routine disease surveillance and outbreak response.

### India: caste, waste, and density

**Manual scavenging — sanitation maintained by sending people into lethal confined spaces**
- Sewer and septic-tank cleaning in India is still performed by hand, and it kills. NCSK recorded **608 deaths between 2013 and 2017**; government figures record a further **443 deaths from 2018 to 2023** (76, 133, 35, 66, 84, 49 by year). The Supreme Court has directed ₹10 lakh compensation to the families of everyone who has died in sewerage work **since 1993** — an acknowledgement that the practice has been fatal and continuous for over three decades.
- The work is caste-assigned, not merely low-paid. Those performing it are almost entirely Dalit sub-castes — Valmiki, Mehtar, Dom, Bhangi, Hela, Halalkhor, Thoti and others — for whom the occupation is hereditary. The Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, 2013 outlawed it; as of November 2023, **714 of India's 766 districts had declared themselves manual-scavenging-free** while deaths continued to be recorded.
- **The official count is contested by an order of magnitude.** NCSK reported 123 deaths nationally for January–August 2017; the Safai Karamchari Andolan documented **429 deaths in the National Capital Region alone** over the same period. When a self-declaration regime ("our district is free of it") is also the primary data source, the measurement is structurally incapable of contradicting the claim — the same self-reporting defect as the district-level ODF declarations below.
- **Counting deaths undercounts the harm.** The fatality figures above capture only acute asphyxiation and drowning events. The chronic toll is larger and almost entirely unrecorded: the average life expectancy of an Indian sanitation worker is **under 50 years**, and those specifically engaged in cleaning sewage drains and septic tanks frequently die **before 40** — a 2005 Centre for Education and Communication study of 200 Delhi sewage workers found almost none still working past 60 and a sharp drop-off after 50. Documented occupational morbidity includes leptospirosis, cardiovascular degeneration, chronic respiratory and skin disease, and musculoskeletal injury from hydrogen sulphide, methane and pathogen exposure. Roughly **90% of sanitation workers hold no health insurance**, and about two-thirds received no safety training even during COVID-19. An entry that counts only the deaths in the manhole reproduces exactly the undercount it is documenting.
- **Mechanism note (from the author's own framing, `Public Sense in India`, 2026-02-22):** manual scavenging and the municipal filth in the next entry are not two problems but one. When cleaning is permanently externalised onto a caste assumed to exist for that purpose, the incentive for anyone else to avoid creating mess is removed. Public sanitation behaviour is downstream of who is assumed to be responsible for cleaning — which makes the caste assignment a *cause* of the waste failure, not a separate injustice alongside it.

**Municipal collection failure — cities as open dumps**
- India generates roughly **1.85 lakh tonnes per day** of municipal solid waste, projected to reach ~165 million tonnes annually by 2030. Official reporting claims ~61% is processed; independent assessments put the share of collected waste receiving any treatment before disposal closer to **28%**, with only ~30–35% segregated at all. Both figures should be cited with their source — the gap between them is itself the finding.
- Capacity is missing at the base: **over 60% of urban local bodies lack engineered landfills, material recovery facilities, or trained staff.** Door-to-door collection is inconsistent in exactly the low-income areas where the consequences concentrate, so uncollected waste accumulates in drains and open ground.
- Because collection depends on a single municipal workforce with no redundancy, **sanitation-worker strikes convert immediately into city-scale public health emergencies** — waste piles within days, blocking drains ahead of monsoon and creating vector-breeding sites. That the workers hold this leverage is a symptom of the same structure as the entry above: the function is essential, the workforce is caste-concentrated, and the terms are poor enough that strikes recur.

**Waste disposal fails at two distinct scales**
- *Household waste becomes topography.* Delhi's Ghazipur landfill stood at roughly **65 metres** in CPCB's 2024 assessment, receiving ~2,300 TPD against a processing capacity of ~2,100 TPD — so it grows by design. Only **0.96 million tonnes, about 6.9%, of its legacy waste has been bio-mined.** Nationally there are **3,000+ legacy dumpsites holding on the order of 1 billion tonnes** of unmanaged waste.
- These sites are also air-pollution events: the 2022 Bhalswa landfill fire raised local **PM2.5 by 45–55%** and nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and sulphur dioxide by 25–100% (cross-reference [air](/need/air)). Waste failure and air failure are the same incident measured by two different agencies.
- *Hazardous industrial waste is dumped without containment.* India generates roughly **8.14 million tonnes of hazardous industrial waste** annually, against an enforcement backlog of approximately **21,000 industrial pollution cases pending in Indian courts.** The disposal route for a large share is simply the nearest land or watercourse.

**Industrial effluent discharged onto populations with no notice and no recourse**
- **Bichhri, Udaipur district, Rajasthan** — fertiliser and acid plants contaminated groundwater to a depth of **70 feet across roughly 7 sq km, affecting ~8,000 people in seven villages.** The litigation is over **35 years old** and the aquifer is still not restored; this is the case that produced the Supreme Court's "polluter pays" jurisprudence in India, and it demonstrates that winning the principle did not deliver the remediation.
- **Eloor–Edayar, Kerala** — roughly **400 industries** discharging into the Periyar, one of the most heavily contaminated industrial stretches in the country. **Patancheru–Bollaram, Telangana** — **224 industrial units** polluting soil and water across 20+ villages; the same cluster's pharmaceutical effluent carries antibiotic concentrations high enough to be implicated in resistance emergence (see [water](/need/water), which covers the water-supply dimension).
- The distinguishing features are **non-consent and non-detection**: the exposed population is rural, frequently Adivasi, downstream rather than adjacent, and learns of the contamination through disease clusters rather than through disclosure. Livestock and wildlife dependent on the same water are affected first and counted least — no Indian monitoring framework attributes wildlife mortality to identified industrial discharge as a routine matter, so that share of the harm has no number attached to it at all.

**Density as a transmission multiplier**
- **Tuberculosis is the clearest case.** India carries the world's largest TB burden — roughly **25% of global cases and ~28% of global TB deaths in 2024** — an airborne pathogen whose transmission is governed by crowding, ventilation and duration of shared indoor air, i.e. precisely the housing conditions in [shelter](/need/shelter). Trajectory is genuinely improving: incidence fell **21% from 237 to 187 per lakh (2015→2024)**, roughly twice the global rate of decline, with treatment coverage rising from 53% to 92%. Drug-resistant cases are nonetheless rising.
- **Dengue has exploded since 2001**, and the mechanism runs directly through the waste failure above: *Aedes* breeds in stored domestic water, open drains and accumulated garbage, all of which are densest in peri-urban settlements. Delhi studies find the highest transmission in dense low-income areas but infection also in affluent zones, carried by the daily mobility network — so containment cannot be localised to where the breeding occurs.
- The general form: density is not itself a failure, it is a *multiplier* applied to whatever sanitation, housing and air conditions exist. The same population density that is efficient for service delivery is efficient for pathogen transmission — which means density is only safe in the presence of the infrastructure that is missing above.

**Waterlogging and urban flooding — the one exposure event that is forecast in advance**
- Everything above treats the sanitation failure as a *standing* condition. Flooding converts it into a dated event: stormwater drains and sewers share the same overwhelmed capacity, so floodwater in Indian cities is sewage-mixed by default, and the disease bundle that follows is consistent — leptospirosis, gastroenteritis, hepatitis A and typhoid within weeks, then *Aedes*-borne dengue and malaria from the standing water left behind.
- **Leptospirosis is the measurable one.** Maharashtra recorded **2,355 cases and 167 deaths in 2005**, the year of the Mumbai deluge, concentrated in Mumbai, Thane, Kolhapur, Sangli and Sindhudurg; across **1998–2005 the state recorded 4,484 cases and 394 deaths**. After the 2018 Kerala floods the state reported **2,598 suspected cases and 95 suspected deaths by 11 September**, of which **1,318 were confirmed with 53 confirmed deaths (~4% CFR)**. The suspected-to-confirmed ratio is roughly 2:1 in both directions and has never been reconciled — the confirmatory test is not routinely available at the facilities where these patients present, so the true denominator sits somewhere between the two counts and no agency publishes which. Spatial analysis of the Kerala event (*International Journal of Health Geographics*, 2024) establishes a dose-response: heavy flood events produce a larger case increase than moderate ones, so this is a graded exposure, not a threshold one.
- **The remedy is settled, cheap, and reaches under 40% of the people it is written for.** Post-flood chemoprophylaxis is doxycycline 200 mg weekly. A case-control study in an endemic area found intake within two weeks of the flood strongly protective (**OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.01–0.72, p=0.023**), against risk factors of occupational exposure (OR 4.22) and any wound in the preceding month (**OR 27.6**). A rapid coverage assessment in Kozhikode district after the 2018 floods found that of those *eligible* for chemoprophylaxis, **only 38.2% reported consuming the prescribed doses**. The global evidence base on post-exposure prophylaxis is genuinely inconsistent in effect size and should be cited as such; the direction of association is protective, and the Indian constraint is visibly delivery rather than efficacy.
- **This is the file's cleanest instance of a hazard that is known in advance.** Rainfall is forecast, the waterlogging points are mapped ward by ward in every major municipal corporation, the exposed occupations are identifiable, and the drug costs a few rupees a dose. The failure is not detection and not knowledge — it is that no standing instrument converts a rainfall forecast into a prophylaxis and surveillance action with a coverage target attached. **Solution at hand, blocked**, with the blocker measured at 38.2%.
- **It is also a within-tier loop.** The land that absorbed the water was built on — see [shelter](/need/shelter), *shelter built on the land that prevented flooding, with the state as encroacher*. That entry currently records the shelter and drainage consequence and stops. The disease consequence is this entry, and the two are one causal chain split across two files.
- No routine post-flood syndromic surveillance produces a national count of flood-attributable illness in India. The 2024 monsoon alone was associated with **1,878 deaths and 351,000+ displaced**; the mortality is counted as drowning and building collapse, and the disease that follows is counted, if at all, by state programmes on their own schedules. **The absence is the finding**, and it is the same absence as the missing ODF verification below.

**Open defecation: declared ended, measured otherwise**
- India was declared **Open Defecation Free on 2 October 2019** on the basis of Swachh Bharat Mission latrine-construction data. NFHS-5 (2019–21), which measures where household members *actually* defecate rather than what was built, found roughly **25% of rural households with toilets still practising open defecation**; a rural Madhya Pradesh study found 27.7%. Real gains are not in dispute — improved sanitation access rose from 49% (NFHS-4) to 70% (NFHS-5) — but the declaration measured construction and the target was behaviour.
- SBM counted latrines; the binding constraints on use are water availability, pit-emptying arrangements, and the caste associations of handling one's own waste. The programme's instrument and its objective were measuring different things — structurally the same defect as compensation keyed to land title in [shelter](/need/shelter).

**The second half of sanitation was never built**
- A toilet contains excreta; it does not treat it. ODF++ status requires safe faecal sludge and septage management with no dumping of untreated matter, and this is where the chain breaks: India built household containment at enormous scale without building the conveyance and treatment behind it. The Ganga basin alone generates **8,250 MLD of wastewater against ~3,500 MLD of treatment capacity** (see [water](/need/water)), so roughly 2,550 MLD is discharged raw.
- The consequence is that pathogens are **relocated rather than removed** — out of the household, into the river that the next settlement drinks from. This is why the sanitation and water files describe the same failure from opposite ends, and why ODF status can rise while waterborne disease persists.

**Zoonotic spillover as a recurring, not exceptional, event**
- Of six Nipah virus outbreaks in India since 2001, **four have occurred in Kerala**, three of those in Kozhikode district, with spillover events recorded in **2018, 2019, 2021, 2023, 2024 and 2025**. Case counts stay small and containment has been fast and competent — but the recurrence is the signal, not the toll.
- Kerala detects and contains Nipah because it has the surveillance capacity to do so. The open question this raises for the rest of the country is inverted: recurring spillover in the one state best equipped to see it implies undetected spillover elsewhere, which is an argument about surveillance coverage rather than about virology.

**Antimicrobial resistance, India-specific**
- The global AMR picture is above; India's position within it is distinct enough to state separately. ICMR's 2024 AMR Surveillance Network report finds routine drug classes — fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins, and **carbapenems**, the last-line option — increasingly failing against common hospital infections. In ICMR's HAI surveillance network across 26 tertiary hospitals, carbapenem resistance in organisms causing central-line bloodstream infections ranged from **53% (*Pseudomonas*) to 77% (*Acinetobacter*)**.
- The mortality is concentrated in newborns: an estimated **58,319 neonatal deaths in India were attributable to ESBL-producing pathogens and MRSA** in 2013, and current estimates hold that roughly **one-third of neonatal sepsis deaths in South Asia are directly attributable to AMR**. This is resistance killing at scale now, not a projected 2050 risk.
- Three drivers are India-specific and mutually reinforcing: over-the-counter antibiotic sale without prescription enforcement; pharmaceutical manufacturing effluent discharging active antibiotic compounds into surface water at concentrations implicated in resistance emergence (Patancheru and the Musi, see [water](/need/water)); and hospital infection-prevention practice weak enough that ICMR's own reports identify it as the proximate cause. India is therefore generating resistance through the environment and the clinic simultaneously, which is why it cannot be addressed as a prescribing problem alone.

**Sanitation outside the household — schools, public toilets, and menstrual management**
- Swachh Bharat measured household latrines. The sanitation people need while *outside* the household is separately provisioned and separately failing. On official UDISE+ figures ~97.5% of schools have separate girls' toilets, but **5.3% of Indian schools — roughly 78,854 of them — have no *functional* toilet for girls**, and state functionality ranges from 76.7% (Arunachal Pradesh) to 99.8% (Sikkim). The gap between "has a toilet" and "has a working toilet" is the entire finding, and it is the same access-vs-adoption defect as the ODF declaration above.
- **Menstrual hygiene is a sanitation failure with a school-attendance consequence.** NFHS-5 (2019–21) reports **77.3% of women aged 15–24 using hygienic methods**, up from 58% in NFHS-4 — real progress. But *exclusive* use is far lower: only **~50% use hygienic methods exclusively**, 22.7% rely on unhygienic methods, and 27.5% use both. In rural India only **42% of adolescent women exclusively use hygienic methods**. State variation is extreme: 95% in Kerala against 32% in Bihar; 85% in Tamil Nadu against 23% in Uttar Pradesh.
- The statistically significant predictors of unhygienic method use are rural residence, illiteracy, poverty and — directly — **absence of a toilet facility**. So this is not a product-distribution problem layered on top of sanitation; it is downstream of the same infrastructure gap. Menstrual management also has no disposal pathway in most of India: neither household toilets nor school toilets were designed with it, which pushes waste into drains and septic systems that cannot process it.

**Closed conditioned spaces — the transmission route with no case definition**
- Every other entry in this section concentrates harm on the poor. This one does the opposite, and that is why it is invisible. Mechanically ventilated buildings — hospitals, malls, offices, hotels, airports — recirculate air and run wet cooling systems, which makes them the standard habitat for *Legionella pneumophila* and a shared-air environment for anything else airborne. The variable is the same one that governs TB above: ventilation rate and duration of shared indoor air. TB is that variable failing at the bottom of the income distribution; this is the same variable failing at the top, engineered rather than improvised.
- **The organism is demonstrably present in India.** Clinical and environmental surveillance at a tertiary healthcare centre isolated ***L. pneumophila* serogroup 1** — the serogroup responsible for the large majority of Legionnaires' disease worldwide — recurrently from patient areas, cooling towers, residential blocks and other locations on the hospital campus. **No hospital-acquired legionellosis cases were identified during those study periods.**
- **Two readings survive that result and the file should carry both.** Either incidence really is low — plausible, given warmer ambient water, less centralised hot-water distribution and different plumbing stock than the temperate settings where the disease was characterised — or it is occurring and not being seen. The published position of Indian respiratory and infectious-disease researchers is the second: Legionnaires' disease is *"undoubtedly underdiagnosed and underreported"*, for three named reasons — **no common case definition, no routine diagnostic method, and no successive surveillance**. Note what that means for the first hypothesis: there is no instrument that could currently distinguish the two. Low reported incidence is not evidence of low incidence when the test is not ordered.
- **The control is standard elsewhere and not routine here.** Proactive environmental surveillance of cooling towers and scheduled biocide treatment of installations are recommended and practised in many countries; in India neither is routine, and the studies monitoring *Legionella* in hospital water systems are few enough to be counted. There is no registry of cooling towers, so there is also no denominator — the same shape as the missing mesothelioma registry in [air](/need/air) and the missing slum count in [shelter](/need/shelter), arrived at in a setting where the exposed population can afford to be counted.
- The adjacency worth holding: the healthcare-associated infection burden in the next entry — **over 2 million infections a year** — is measured in the same buildings whose water and air systems are not. HAI is attributed to hands, catheters and devices, which are the transmission routes India has diagnostics for.

**Biomedical waste and the health system as a transmission site**
- India generates roughly **700–719 tonnes of biomedical waste per day** across ~322,000 healthcare facilities. Compliance is partial — CPCB reporting has found ~13% of facilities in breach of biomedical waste rules, and during the pandemic **22 of 36 states and union territories generated more biomedical waste than they had the capacity to treat**. The NGT has since had to press states for basic disclosure on treatment, meaning the regulator cannot currently establish the size of the gap it is regulating.
- The facility itself is a transmission site: healthcare-associated infections account for an estimated **over 2 million infections annually in India**, with attributable mortality in the 10–30% range and cost estimates around $1.5 billion a year. Hand-hygiene compliance is documented as low in Indian tertiary care, and the AMR entry above is the same failure observed at the microbial level — HAI and carbapenem resistance are one problem described twice.
- Cross-cutting note: the workers handling this waste stream are largely the same contract sanitation workforce described at the top of this section, with the same absence of PPE, insurance and training. The waste-handling failure and the labour failure are not adjacent — they share a workforce.

**Who is already commercially working on this, and who pays them**
- Required by the commercial-landscape pass. This file produces the tier's cleanest test of *capital follows the mandate, not the harm*, because it contains two waste streams of very different lethality treated by very different instruments.
- **EPR plastics recycling is a real, profitable industry built entirely by a compliance obligation.** **Banyan Nation** runs ~₹100 cr revenue with **80% of it EPR-driven**, a sold-out Hyderabad plant, DFC backing and a 48,000 TPA Bangalore expansion while raising $30–50M; **Lucro Plastecycle** has diverted 100,000+ tonnes and supplies HUL and Amazon. The recycled-plastics market runs **$4.24B (2024) → $6.52B (2033)**, and the **EPR compliance market itself $1.5B (2025) → $5B (2030) at ~20% CAGR**. The payer is a brand owner under a CPCB mandate with tradeable credits behind it.
- **Faecal sludge management, which sits directly upstream of the fecal-oral disease burden that dominates this file, has no equivalent.** No named scaled private desludging company; what exists is CDD India's demonstration FSTP at Devanahalli and the Gates-seeded NFSSM Alliance, a policy-coordination body rather than an operator. Service is ULB-run or informally provided. **Plastic got a legal obligation with a tradeable instrument; septage did not.** The disease burden runs the other way. That is the comparison, and it is decisive: capital sorted on instrument design, not on mortality.
- **Waste-to-energy is a capital graveyard with a specific, diagnosable cause.** Only about **5 of India's early WtE plants ever functioned**; Okhla operates under documented violations (a 2024 investigation found dioxins at ten times the legal limit and illegal ash dumping), Ghazipur never took off. The root cause is feedstock: unsegregated Indian municipal waste has a calorific value around **1,324 kcal/kg against the ~1,800 kcal/kg combustion threshold**. The technology did not fail — it was bought for an input that does not exist, which is the segregation failure recorded earlier in this section reappearing as an investment loss.
- **Sewer robotics is real technology on a grant-shaped market.** **Genrobotics' Bandicoot** has grown ₹12 cr → ₹14 cr → ₹20 cr, holds a ₹17 cr Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation tender and an ₹80 cr Singapore export deal, with 300+ units across 180+ cities and Zoho as an equity investor. But 300 units is trivial against national manhole volume, and the revenue is municipal capital tender rather than recurring service income. It substitutes a machine for the worker; it does not change who is assumed responsible for cleaning, which the mechanism note above identifies as the actual cause.
- **Manual scavenging has no market by definition.** It is unpaid or informally paid caste-assigned labour — the opposite of a market, and not a gap a business can enter.
- **Biomedical waste works, and it works because hospitals are mandated.** **200+ CBWTFs** — Synergy Waste Management, SMS Water Graca, Mumbai Waste Management, GreenTech Environ — in a market running **$2.32B (2024) → $3.53B (2030)**, fragmented and thin in rural and hill districts. Third mandate-created market in the tier after ZLD and CEMS, with the same property: it attaches to countable, licensable facilities.
- **AMR has no commercial ally anywhere in the chain.** No Indian actor was found whose revenue *falls* when antibiotic use falls. Manufacturers, pharmacies and even diagnostic vendors all earn more from more prescribing or more testing around prescribing; stewardship is ICMR- and grant-funded. This is structurally worse than "the harmed party cannot pay" — here every party with capital is aligned against the remedy, and that distinction belongs on the shortlist.
- **Where the customer is an urban consumer, the market is healthy and orthogonal to the harm.** Sanitaryware (Hindware, Cera, Jaquar, Parryware, Somany) runs **$2.15B (2025) → $3.85B (2032)** on institutional and premium residential demand, not rural ODF need. Menstrual products run **$900M (2024) → $1.79B (2029)** — Whisper and Stayfree at the top, Sirona, Nua ($15.1M raised), Carmesi and Pee Safe below — while the constraint identified above for the 42% of rural adolescent women not using hygienic methods exclusively is **absence of a toilet**, which no product in that market supplies.
- **Sulabh is the closest thing to a working model for the harmed population**: roughly **15 million users a day at ₹1–2 per use, ~₹550M annual revenue**, cross-subsidised, with municipal land and CSR top-ups. Like Amul in [food](/need/food), it reaches the bottom of the distribution and is not a venture-financed company — two of the tier's three genuine bottom-quintile successes are cooperative or nonprofit in structure. That recurrence is worth more attention than either instance alone.

**Framework note: how a toxin arrives (from the author's own framing, 2026-07-27)**
- This file's subject is pathogens; toxins are distributed across [food](/need/food) (adulteration), [water](/need/water) (arsenic, fluoride, effluent), [air](/need/air) (asbestos, lead, silica) and the industrial-effluent entry above. That distribution is by *carrier*. A second and orthogonal sort is by **how the toxin got to a person**, and it produces five channels, each of which already has instances written up somewhere in this tier:
- **Lack of knowledge — not previously studied.** The only channel with a live Indian instance rather than a historical one, and the tier has no entry for it. **PFAS**: an IIT Madras study (2024) detected PFAS at up to **136.27 ng/L** in Chennai surface water and groundwater, and short- and long-chain compounds are documented in the Ganges, Yamuna, Cauvery and Krishna. **India has no PFAS drinking-water standard and no PFAS discharge limit**; PFAS, phthalates and BPA are absent from Indian drinking-water standards and from routine monitoring requirements altogether. This is not the lead pattern (known, then acted on late) — it is exposure accumulating with no threshold against which a measurement could even be judged non-compliant. It belongs in [water](/need/water) as stored risk and is currently written nowhere.
- **Lack of policy — known and not restricted.** The tier's canonical case: **asbestos**, banned in 60+ countries, imported into India at scale and sold as affordable roofing (see [air](/need/air), and the toxic-exposure class in the [tier-1 summary](/tier/1)). Lead and silica are the same channel at different stages.
- **Accident.** Thin in tier 1 and worth naming as an absence: **Bhopal sits in [disaster / institutional protection](/need/disaster-protection)**, not here, and tier 1 carries no entry for infrastructure failure releasing a toxin — a ruptured sewer or chemical line after an earthquake, a landfill fire (the Bhalswa event above is written as an air-quality incident, not as an accident-channel toxin release).
- **Poor judgement — dumped or leaked knowingly.** **Bichhri, Eloor–Edayar, Patancheru** above. The distinguishing feature recorded there — non-consent and non-detection — is a property of this channel specifically, not of toxins in general.
- **Deliberation.** Aum Shinrikyo's sarin (see [air](/need/air)) and the destruction of Gaza's water and sewage infrastructure (above). Rare, and the only channel where the harm is the objective rather than the byproduct.
- **What the sort earns, stated honestly.** Three of the five channels restate the frontier criterion in different words — "lack of policy" *is* solution-at-hand-blocked, "lack of knowledge" is its complement, and "deliberation" is a blocker that no technical fix addresses. The taxonomy's real yield is the other two: it makes *lack of knowledge* a **live** category with PFAS in it rather than a historical one closed by the leaded-petrol story, and it separates **accident** from **poor judgement**, which this tier currently collapses. Bhopal and Bichhri are filed as the same kind of event and are not — one has a movable blocker (containment engineering, disclosure duty) and the other has a legal principle already won and unenforced for 35 years. Provisionally placed here; the natural home is the toxic-exposure class in the [tier-1 summary](/tier/1) if it survives contact with tier 2.
