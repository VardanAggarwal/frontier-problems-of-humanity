# north-india-winter-smog

- Delhi has ranked the world's most polluted capital city every year since 2018 — eight consecutive years as of IQAir's 2025 report. Annual average PM2.5 has sat in a narrow band across that period, ~92–100 µg/m³ over 2021–2025 (2025: 99.6 µg/m³, roughly 20x the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³). Winter peaks are driven by crop-stubble burning in Punjab/Haryana, vehicle emissions, construction dust, and temperature inversions trapping pollutants near ground level.
- This recurs annually: November 2023, 2024 and 2025 each produced multi-day "severe" AQI episodes (readings above 450) in Delhi-NCR, prompting school closures, construction bans (GRAP Stage IV), and odd-even vehicle rationing — the same emergency-response playbook repeated each winter rather than a structural fix. Delhi's 2025 annual average fell ~8% year over year, the first meaningful move in the series, but off a base so high that the city remained the world's worst capital; see the NCAP block for why the decline is probably meteorological rather than structural.
- The Lancet Planetary Health (2021) estimate of ~1.67 million annual Indian deaths attributable to air pollution remains the standard figure; no subsequent national assessment has revised it materially downward.
- *Data caution*: the 2025 IQAir report gives Delhi both 82.2 µg/m³ (as "New Delhi", capital-cities ranking) and 99.6 µg/m³ (as "Delhi", cities ranking), reflecting different administrative boundaries. Always specify which.

# small-industrial-town-air

- Delhi absorbs the attention, but it is not the worst case even within its own country. IQAir's 2025 report puts **66 of the world's 100 most polluted cities in India**, and five of the global top ten: Loni, Byrnihat, Delhi, Ghaziabad and Ula. The world's 25 most polluted cities are all in India, Pakistan or China. Delhi ranked only third in India.
- **Loni (Ghaziabad district, UP) is the world's most polluted city** — annual PM2.5 of 112.5 µg/m³ in 2025, over 22x the WHO guideline, and up ~23% year over year while the national average edged down. Loni hosts 1,000+ small manufacturing units (garments, leather, plastics, e-waste, auto parts), most running diesel gensets against grid gaps, and functions as a truck transit corridor between Delhi and western UP. The pollution is locally produced by informal-sector industry operating below the threshold where emission regulation is enforced.
- **Byrnihat (Meghalaya/Assam border, PM2.5 101.1 µg/m³) exposes an airshed-jurisdiction failure.** Meghalaya's own pollution control board attributes the pollution to roughly 20 red-category industries on the *Assam* side of the border, while Byrnihat itself (in Meghalaya) has two. The regulator with authority over the affected residents has no authority over the emitters. The Meghalaya government publicly disputed the IQAir ranking, describing local air quality as "satisfactory" — the state simultaneously blames another state and denies the measurement. Byrnihat received its first government continuous monitoring station only in **December 2025**, *after* being ranked the most polluted town on Earth for 2024 on the basis of non-governmental sensors.
- **These rankings are a floor, not a ceiling.** Per the 2026 State of India's Environment report, only ~15% of India's population (~200 million) lives within 10 km of a continuous air quality monitor; the other ~1.2 billion live outside any measurable range. India operates 562 real-time monitors across 294 cities and 966 manual stations across 419 towns — against roughly 7,900 census towns and cities nationally. Small towns entering the rankings therefore reflect *newly acquired visibility* at least as much as newly acquired pollution — and the unmonitored tail is unknown by construction.
- **The same resolution problem operates within cities — the Mumbai case.** Coastal metros were assumed to be self-cleaning via sea breeze; Mumbai's 2023 winter season broke that assumption, briefly running worse than Delhi as construction dust load rose and winter winds slackened. But the trend did not hold: per CSE's winter 2024-25 analysis, Mumbai's peak daily PM2.5 was 81 µg/m³ (28 December 2024), a **44% decrease** from the average of the previous three winter peaks. What persists is hyperlocal — Deonar averaged 80 µg/m³ across the winter and Malad W spiked to 194 µg/m³, alongside a distinct NO2 rise from November to January. Mumbai is thus not a city-wide failure but a set of ward-level ones concealed by a city-wide average.
- Internationally the same pattern holds: Lahore, Pakistan and Dhaka, Bangladesh recur among the most polluted major cities year over year (Lahore's November 2023 smog crisis pushed AQI past 1,900 on local scales), while sub-Saharan African cities such as N'Djamena, Chad rank high on thin monitoring infrastructure that has historically undercounted the true toll.

# ncap-spend-misallocation

- India's National Clean Air Programme is not an unfunded aspiration — it is a funded programme misallocated at its core. Per CREA's *Tracing the Hazy Air 2026*, of ₹13,415 crore released, ₹9,929 crore was spent (74% utilisation), and **68% of that went to road dust management**, 14% to transport, and negligible amounts to industry and domestic fuel.
- Road dust is predominantly coarse PM10; the mortality burden is driven by fine combustion-derived PM2.5 from industry, vehicles and biomass. Spending is therefore concentrated on the most *visible* fraction of the problem rather than the most lethal one. The political economy explains the choice: mechanical sweepers and water sprinklers are procurement decisions with contractors and no identifiable loser, whereas closing or retrofitting an industrial cluster creates one. Loni and Byrnihat are exactly the cases this allocation cannot touch.
- The spending often precedes the diagnosis: only 90 of 130 NCAP cities have completed source apportionment studies, meaning roughly 40 cities have been allocating abatement budgets without established knowledge of what is polluting them.
- Outcomes match the allocation. Of 130 NCAP cities, only 51 achieved even the original 20–30% PM10 reduction target and just 23 met the revised 40% target — while a separate 23 cities **recorded PM10 increases** over the programme period (the two counts of 23 are coincidental, not the same cities). In 2025, 190 of 229 cities with adequate PM10 data exceeded India's own NAAQS. On PM2.5, 103 of 231 cities exceeded the standard — but India's annual PM2.5 NAAQS is 40 µg/m³, eight times the WHO guideline of 5, so "compliant" cities are largely still at levels the WHO considers hazardous.
- The measurement layer is underfunded alongside: 28 NCAP cities still have no continuous monitoring station, and India remains 465 stations short of its own 1,500 manual-station target. A programme cannot be held to outcomes it has not instrumented itself to observe.
- Net effect at national level: India's average PM2.5 moved from 50.6 to 48.9 µg/m³ (2024→2025) and Delhi's fell ~8%, but IQAir's own lead scientist characterises the national decline as not statistically significant and likely weather-driven. Seven years into NCAP, the observable change is within meteorological noise.
- **The same selection pressure produces the visible-remedy class.** Delhi's ₹25 crore Connaught Place smog tower, inaugurated August 2021, was measured to cut pollution by at most **17% within a 100 metre radius**; the DPCC told the NGT that smog towers are not a practical solution and that on that performance the city would need on the order of **40,000 of them**. The tower has spent long stretches non-functional through the same winters it was built for, and October 2025 cloud seeding over Delhi produced no measurable change. India separately lost **2.33 million hectares of tree cover between 2001 and 2023** (Lancet Countdown 2025) — the plantation counts and the canopy-loss figure are not reconciled anywhere in official reporting, which is itself the finding.

# asbestos-import-legal

- Unlike the smog problems, this is a *solved* problem being actively un-solved. Roughly 68 countries have banned all forms of asbestos; India banned only *mining* (phased out from 1986) while leaving import and use of chrysotile ("white asbestos") fully legal. India has been among the world's largest importers for over a decade, taking in roughly **360,000–485,000 tonnes a year** across recent reporting years (379,043 t in 2014; 361,164 t in 2019–20; ~485,000 t cited for 2023) — sourced ~85% from Russia, with the remainder from Brazil, Kazakhstan, Hungary, Poland and South Africa (IBAS / British Safety Council India). Treat the year-on-year series with caution: figures differ by source depending on whether they count chrysotile only or all asbestos-containing imports, so the reliable claim is sustained scale, not a specific growth rate. Most of it goes into asbestos-cement roofing sheets, explicitly marketed as an affordable building material for poor households — the hazard is being installed into low-income housing stock as a *development* product.
- The disease burden is structurally invisible. India has no central mesothelioma registry, no systematic occupational-disease surveillance, few pathologists trained to diagnose asbestos-related disease, and low general awareness that chrysotile causes asbestosis, lung cancer and mesothelioma. Officially reported case counts are therefore near-zero in a country consuming hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year — absence of data is being read as absence of harm.
- Latency compounds the invisibility: mesothelioma typically presents 20–40 years after exposure. The UK's continuing asbestos death toll (~2,500/yr) is the delayed bill for use that peaked in the 1960s–70s, decades after the material was banned there. India's exposure is peaking *now*, meaning the mortality wave arrives in the 2040s–2060s, after the political window for prevention has closed.
- **Representation: present at the right unit, unmoved for decades.** BANI (Ban Asbestos Network India) and IAVA have campaigned centrally since ~2000 — the correct unit for a 20–40 year latency harm no individual sufferer can attribute at the point of exposure. Representation is eliminated as the explanation for inaction; the blocker isolates cleanly to the import trade itself, which is why this file (with `00-summary.md`) treats asbestos as the cleanest diagnosis in the tier.

# ambient-asbestos-demolition-dust

- **Open question — ambient vs. occupational exposure.** The literature frames asbestos as an occupational hazard (miners, shipbuilders, insulation workers). India's situation may add a second route: construction and demolition dust as an *ambient* urban exposure, affecting people who never worked with the material. Indian cities already run high PM10 loads dominated by construction dust; if fibre release from asbestos-cement demolition and cutting is a meaningful fraction, the exposed population is orders of magnitude larger than the occupational cohort. This would also offer a partial explanation for lung cancer in non-smokers. Speculative — ambient asbestos fibre measurement in Indian cities is thin to nonexistent, which is itself the finding. The mechanism generalises: other mineral dusts released by mass construction may carry undiscovered hazards on the same detection lag.
