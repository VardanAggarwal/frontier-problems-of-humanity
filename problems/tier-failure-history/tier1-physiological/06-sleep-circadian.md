# Sleep / Circadian Regulation (Tier 1 — Physiological)

Thinner historical record than food or water. Sleep leaves no ruins, no
famine registers, no archaeological layer. Most of what follows is 20th
century onward — either because sleep loss was weaponized deliberately, or
because industrial and electric technology made circadian disruption a mass
condition for the first time. Pre-industrial sleep disruption (illness, cold,
predators, infant care, war) certainly existed but is not well documented as
a distinct threat category — folded into other tier-1 threats instead.

**The structural property that separates this need from the other five.**
Sleep requires nothing to be supplied. Food, water, air and shelter all fail
through **non-provision** — something must be produced, treated, delivered or
built, and the failure is that it wasn't. Sleep requires only that
interference be **removed**. That single asymmetry explains most of what
follows. It is why sleep has no ministry, no scheme, no budget line and no
procurement pipeline: there is nothing to buy. It is why every entry in the
India section below is *someone else's activity* — an employer's roster, a
discom's load schedule, a landlord's roof, a neighbour's generator, a
platform's dispatch algorithm — rather than an absent service. And it is why
the harm is systematically misfiled: a need that fails by imposition rather
than by scarcity has no natural claimant, so its damage is recorded under
whatever it later causes.

**A distinction this file must hold throughout: clinical vs imposed.** Total
or primary insomnia is a medical condition with a diagnostic pathway, however
poorly resourced (see the measurement note at the end of the India section).
Insufficient and poor-quality sleep in an otherwise healthy person is the far
larger band, and it is not a disease — it is an exposure. Collapsing the two
is what routes the entire problem to clinicians, where the environmental band
cannot be treated, instead of to the parties who impose it. Almost everything
below concerns the second category.

## How this need has been threatened

- **Pre-industrial "segmented sleep" as the default, not a disorder.**
  Historian A. Roger Ekirch's research (*At Day's Close: Night in Times
  Past*, 2005) documents that before artificial lighting, Europeans
  routinely slept in two bouts — "first sleep" and "second sleep" — with an
  hour or more of wakefulness in between, attested in diaries, court
  records, and medical texts from the 16th–19th centuries. This wasn't
  disruption; it was the biological norm. The threat came later, when it
  was erased.
- **Artificial light collapsing the natural sleep window.** Gas lighting
  (widespread in cities from the 1810s–1840s) and then electric lighting
  (post-1880s, Edison/Swan) extended usable evening hours and pushed sleep
  onset later. Segmented sleep disappears from the historical record by the
  early 20th century — Ekirch ties this directly to lighting, not biology
  changing.
- **Industrial night-shift work.** Factories running continuous shifts
  (textile mills, steelworks) from the mid-19th century onward forced
  workers onto schedules misaligned with circadian biology. Night-shift
  labor became normalized in mining, rail, and manufacturing well before
  anyone understood circadian physiology.
- **Sleep deprivation as interrogation/torture technique.** Documented use
  by the Soviet NKVD in the 1930s–50s ("conveyor" interrogation — prisoners
  kept awake for days in relays). British Army use in Northern Ireland
  (1971, the "five techniques," ruled inhuman treatment by the European
  Court of Human Rights in *Ireland v. UK*, 1978). CIA use post-9/11 —
  documented in the 2014 Senate Intelligence Committee report on the
  "enhanced interrogation" program, including forced standing and sleep
  deprivation up to 180 hours for some detainees.
- **Combat sleep deprivation.** Extended operations deliberately or
  incidentally deny soldiers sleep; military research (US Army research at
  Walter Reed and elsewhere, published from the 1970s onward) documents
  measurable cognitive and decision-making degradation after 24–72 hours
  without sleep, comparable to legal alcohol intoxication thresholds.
- **Fatigue-linked industrial disasters.** Three Mile Island (1979, 4 a.m.
  shift), Chernobyl (1986, test conducted around 1 a.m. by a night crew),
  and the Exxon Valdez grounding (1989, third mate at the helm, fatigue
  cited by the National Transportation Safety Board) all occurred during
  night or rotating-shift hours with fatigue named as a contributing factor
  in official post-incident investigations.
- **Urban light and noise pollution.** 20th-century electrification and
  car/street noise created continuous ambient light and sound that
  epidemiological sleep research (from the 1990s onward) links to
  measurably shorter, more fragmented sleep in dense urban populations.
- **The 24/7 economy.** Late-20th/early-21st-century globalization and
  always-on service industries (call centers, e-commerce fulfillment,
  global-market trading desks) normalized shift patterns spanning all 24
  hours, extending the same disruption that began in factories to a much
  larger share of the workforce.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **1919 — ILO Hours of Work Convention.** One of the first international
  labor standards, limiting working hours generally; later conventions
  specifically addressed night work.
- **1948 (UK), and equivalents elsewhere — night-work protections.**
  Post-WWII labor law in industrialized countries began restricting night
  and shift work for certain groups (initially women and children in many
  jurisdictions, later broadened), plus mandated rest periods between
  shifts.
- **1990 — ILO Night Work Convention (No. 171).** Set international
  standards for health assessment, transfer rights, and compensation for
  night workers.
- **FAA/EASA flight and duty time regulations.** Aviation became the
  proving ground for circadian science translating into hard law — flight
  crew duty-time limits, mandatory rest periods, and (in the US) the 2011
  FAA rule overhaul following the 2009 Colgan Air crash (crew fatigue cited
  by the NTSB) tightened rest requirements industry-wide.
- **Hospital resident duty-hour limits.** The US Accreditation Council for
  Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) capped resident physician shifts
  (80-hour weekly cap introduced 2003, further limits in 2011) after
  research and the 1984 Libby Zion case linked resident fatigue to medical
  errors.
- **Sleep medicine as a formal field.** The field professionalized from the
  1970s–80s — the American Academy of Sleep Medicine formed in 1975 as the
  Association of Sleep Disorders Centers; sleep labs and polysomnography
  became standard diagnostic tools for disorders tied to circadian
  disruption.
- **Circadian science entering occupational health.** Research on
  shift-work tolerance and fatigue risk management (accelerating after
  Chernobyl and Exxon Valdez) fed into formal Fatigue Risk Management
  Systems now required in aviation, rail, and nuclear-plant operations.
- **Consumer light-control technology.** Blackout curtains, and later
  blue-light-filtering screen software (widespread from the mid-2010s), are
  informal, individual-level adaptations to an environment of engineered
  artificial light — treating the symptom of a threat institutions only
  partly regulate.
- **Cultural practices predating and outlasting industrialization.** The
  siesta (Mediterranean and Latin American) and similar midday-rest customs
  in hot climates represent an older institutional adaptation to circadian
  and thermal load, one that industrial scheduling has generally eroded
  rather than absorbed.

### Where it worked

- **Gujarat's Jyotirgram Yojana, 2003–06, already solved this file's most tractable failure — in another state, for another reason.** Feeder separation gave tubewell owners **eight hours a day of full-voltage power on a pre-announced daytime schedule**. The night-irrigation problem described in the India section below simply does not arise where feeders are separated. The scheme was designed for power-subsidy and groundwater management (see `02-water.md`); the circadian benefit was an uncosted byproduct that nobody claimed credit for.
- That is the important part. **The solution exists, is two decades old, has a published cost (~US$290 million for a state), and was adopted for reasons unrelated to sleep.** No new knowledge, technology or behaviour change is required to extend it — which makes night irrigation the clearest "solution at hand" case in Tier 1.

## Where this fails today

- **Gig-economy and app-dispatched workers.** Uber/Lyft drivers and food-delivery
  couriers set their own hours but chase surge pricing into late-night and
  early-morning windows; several US sleep-medicine surveys (2022–2023, e.g.
  National Sleep Foundation polling) report gig and app-based workers logging
  under 6 hours of sleep at higher rates than traditional shift workers, with
  no employer-mandated rest period since they're classified as independent
  contractors, not employees covered by fatigue rules.
- **Medical resident duty-hour violations persist.** Despite the ACGME's
  80-hour weekly cap, resident surveys (e.g. a 2022 JAMA Network Open study
  and ongoing ACGME resident surveys) continue to find a meaningful minority
  of residents — commonly cited in the 20–40% range depending on specialty —
  reporting shifts or weekly hours that exceed the cap, with under-reporting
  suspected due to fear of program consequences.
- **Teen sleep loss tied to screens and social media.** CDC Youth Risk
  Behavior Survey data (2021, published 2022) found about 77% of US high
  schoolers get less than the recommended 8–10 hours of sleep on school
  nights. Pew Research (2022–2023) found most teens are online almost
  constantly, with a majority checking phones after bedtime; multiple
  pediatric sleep studies from this period link late-night phone/social-media
  use directly to delayed sleep onset and shortened total sleep.
- **Shift-work disorder remains common and under-diagnosed.** Clinical
  estimates (American Academy of Sleep Medicine, ongoing) put shift-work
  disorder — insomnia or excessive sleepiness tied to a misaligned schedule —
  at roughly 10–40% of night and rotating-shift workers, depending on
  industry, with most cases never formally diagnosed.
- **War-zone sleep deprivation as an ongoing condition.** In Gaza
  (2023–2025) and Ukraine (2022–2026), UN and WHO situation reports and
  humanitarian-agency surveys describe chronic sleep disruption from
  bombardment, displacement, and blackouts as a widespread, sustained
  condition among civilians — not an isolated incident but a feature of daily
  life for the duration of the conflict. Precise sleep-duration statistics
  for these populations are harder to find than for the interrogation cases
  above; most reporting is qualitative (trauma and mental-health surveys)
  rather than polysomnographic.
- **Overwork/"hustle culture" health effects, quantified.** A joint WHO–ILO
  study (May 2021, the most-cited figure still in use through 2026) attributed
  745,000 deaths per year globally to stroke and heart disease linked to
  working 55+ hours a week — sleep curtailment is one of the mechanisms cited,
  alongside stress. This is workload rather than sleep data directly, but it's
  the closest quantified bridge from "hustle culture" to physiological harm.
- **Data quality note.** Statistics for gig workers, war zones, and
  hustle-culture harms are thinner and more indirect than the tighter
  clinical numbers for teens and shift workers — this section leans on
  survey and mortality-attribution studies rather than direct, universal
  sleep-tracking data, because that data mostly doesn't exist at scale for
  these groups.

### India: night work, no place to sleep, and heat

**Gig work at national scale — and the class asymmetry in a 24/7 city**
- The gig-worker entry above is US-framed; India is the larger case. NITI Aayog puts gig and platform workers at **7.7 million (2020–21) rising to 23.5 million by 2029–30**. The "Prisoners on Wheels" report finds **78% working over 10 hours a day** under algorithmic dispatch pressure; Fairwork India (2023) found monthly earnings of **₹15,000–20,000**, below minimum wage for the hours actually worked, and a 2024 NITI assessment found **90% of gig workers hold no savings**. Long hours plus no savings buffer means rest is not economically optional, which is the mechanism by which flexible scheduling produces less sleep rather than more.
- **The distributional point is the important one.** The 24/7 city is experienced as liberty by the strata that consume it — late-night delivery, ten-minute commerce, round-the-clock services — and as circadian disruption by the strata that staff it. These are not two effects of urbanisation; they are the same transaction viewed from either end. One group's convenience *is* the other group's night shift, which means the harm cannot be reduced by informing or nudging the worker. It sits in the demand structure.

**An export industry built on circadian misalignment**
- India's BPO and global-capability-centre sector is staffed to foreign business hours by design: 11 a.m. in New York is 8:30 p.m. in India, so serving US daytime requires Indian agents working Indian night. The time-zone gap is not a side effect of the industry — it is the product being sold.
- Indian studies on BPO night-shift cohorts document weight gain and obesity within 12 months of starting nights, vitamin D deficiency from reduced daylight exposure, **type-2 diabetes risk 30–40% higher than day-shift comparators**, and elevated anxiety and depression among long-tenure night workers. This is the same shift-work disorder described above, but institutionalised as a national comparative advantage rather than an occupational exception — which makes it the hardest entry in this file to frame as a fixable defect, since fixing it removes the business rationale.

**Agricultural power scheduling forces farmers into night work**
- The least-known circadian disruption in India is a load-management artifact. Three-phase power to agricultural feeders is supplied at night in many states — starting after midnight in parts of Marathwada, on rotating 8-hour-day/10-hour-night patterns elsewhere — because non-agricultural consumers hold daytime priority and agricultural demand is treated as shiftable to off-peak. Farmers therefore irrigate at night, waking around 4:30 a.m. or walking miles in darkness to switch on pumps, in winter cold and through wildlife territory. Karnataka's forest minister has formally asked for daytime agricultural supply specifically to reduce fatal leopard, tiger and elephant encounters.
- Two features make this analytically distinct from every other entry in this file: the circadian disruption is **entirely policy-created** — no market pressure, no consumer demand, no technology, just a tariff-and-load decision — and it is therefore **entirely policy-reversible**. Solar agricultural feeders, which generate during daylight and let states supply farm power by day, are being deployed for exactly this reason. A solution is at hand, and the obstacle is subsidy and distribution-company economics rather than physics.

**People with no place to sleep, and shelter that does not permit sleep**
- India's last count of houselessness was **Census 2011: 1.77 million people, 938,000 urban** — with no update since, and the next census due only in 2027 (see `04-shelter-thermoregulation.md`). Pavement and public-space sleeping is therefore uncounted at present, on top of being undercounted at the time.
- The distinction worth preserving: `04` documents the *lethal* end of thermal failure — hypothermia and heatstroke deaths. This file covers the far larger sub-lethal band. Conditions in jhuggi and informal housing are usually not cold or hot enough to kill, and are routinely hot, noisy, crowded and lit enough to **destroy sleep every night for years**. That harm has no death certificate, so it appears in no mortality statistic, which is precisely why it is absent from policy. Chronic sleep restriction's downstream effects — metabolic, cardiovascular, cognitive, psychiatric — are then attributed to other causes.

**Heat and temperature volatility as sleep destroyers, distributed regressively**
- The quantified global result is Minor et al. (2022, *One Earth*), using wristband data from over 47,000 people across 68 countries matched to local weather: sleep duration begins declining once **outdoor night temperature exceeds ~10°C**, and above **25°C the share of people sleeping under seven hours rises by ~3.5%**. Each +1°C in monthly night-time temperature produces roughly **three additional nights of insufficient sleep per 100 people per month**. Effects are strongest in **lower-income countries, warmer climate regions, older adults and women** — every vulnerability axis the study identifies converges on India.
- India then adds a housing multiplier the global study does not capture: tin and asbestos-cement roofs run **4–6°C hotter indoors than outdoor air**, and pre-monsoon indoor temperatures in slum housing exceed safe physiological limits **even at night**, removing the overnight recovery window (see `04-shelter-thermoregulation.md`). With **~8% household air-conditioning penetration**, thermal sleep loss is distributed almost perfectly by income: the households with insulation and cooling are buffered, and the households whose roof is the amplifier are not.
- Increasing volatility matters independently of mean warming. Unseasonal cold snaps and early heat spikes hit hardest where there is no insulation in either direction — a dwelling with no thermal mass tracks the outdoor swing, so temperature *unpredictability* removes even the adaptive strategies (sleeping outdoors, on roofs, at different hours) that populations have historically used against predictable seasons.

**Hunger as a sleep destroyer — the loop back to `01-food.md`**
- The file already carries one within-tier loop, from shelter into heat. This is the second, and it runs from food. It is hard to sleep on an empty stomach, and the effect is measurable rather than folkloric: the WHO **Study on global AGEing and adult health (SAGE)** — **33,460 adults aged 50+ across six low- and middle-income countries, India among them** — finds food insecurity significantly associated with poor sleep quality, with sleep disturbance, and with long sleep duration (≥9 hours). A systematic review and meta-analysis in adults finds food insecurity associated with elevated risk of **both** short (≤6h) and long (≥9h) duration.
- **The bidirectionality matters more than the association.** Short sleep is itself an established driver of appetite dysregulation and metabolic dysfunction, so undernutrition and sleep loss reinforce each other rather than sitting in sequence. That makes this the third route in a cluster already documented in this tier: overcrowding, household solid fuel and undernutrition all converge on the same households, and TB decomposition in `05-sanitation-disease.md` finds them entering the same disease. Sleep is the fourth input to that cluster and is measured in none of its programmes.
- Note the direction of the long-duration finding, which is counter-intuitive and should not be smoothed away: food insecurity associates with *both* tails. Extended time in bed under deprivation is not rest — it is fragmented, low-quality sleep and, plausibly, energy conservation. An intervention evaluated on hours slept would score this as adequate.

**Chronic pain and mental health — where sleep loss becomes self-sustaining**
- Every other entry in this file describes an exposure that ends: the shift finishes, the heat breaks, the generator switches off. Pain and psychiatric distress are different in kind, because both are **bidirectional with sleep** — each degrades the other, so the loss continues after the original cause is removed. This is the mechanism by which an episode of imposed sleep loss becomes a permanent deficit.
- **Chronic pain is the larger and more neglected half.** Point prevalence in India is **13%**, with mean intensity **6.93 on the NRS** and **63% of those affected in the severe band**; the commonest sites are knees (32%), legs (28%) and joints (22%). Respondents report being unable to sleep, exercise, or maintain an independent lifestyle. Clinically the overlap is near-total: **50–88% of chronic-pain patients report sleep difficulty, and at least 40% of people with insomnia have chronic pain.**
- **It is managed almost entirely outside clinical care.** **68% of Indian chronic-pain sufferers treat with over-the-counter drugs and 95% of those with NSAIDs** — self-medication at population scale, with no prescriber, no follow-up, and no point at which the sleep consequence is asked about. The instrument that exists (pharmacy retail) is keyed to the pain and not to the disability, which is the *instrument keyed to the wrong object* mechanism appearing in a consumer market rather than a government scheme.
- **On the psychiatric side the file has been recording one direction only.** The BPO entry above notes elevated anxiety and depression among long-tenure night workers as an *outcome*. The reverse arrow is equally well established and belongs here explicitly: distress delays and fragments sleep, and the resulting deficit deepens the distress. Recording the loop rather than one arrow of it matters for the same reason it does for pain above — an intervention that removes the original imposition does not close it.
- The practical consequence for this file's shortlist logic: pain and mood are the two entries where *removing the imposition is not sufficient*, because the loop persists on its own. They are therefore poor frontier candidates by the solution-at-hand test and important context for why the good candidates must be caught early.

**Screens and stimulants — the two individually-held inputs**
- Distinguished from everything else in this section, which is imposed by another party. These are consumed, which changes both the ethics and the available instruments — and is the reason they are the two entries most likely to be offered as *the* explanation for Indian sleep loss, displacing the structural causes above. They are real and they are not the largest term.
- **Screens.** The global entry above is US-framed (CDC YRBS: 77% of high schoolers under the recommended hours). The Indian cross-sectional evidence (BMC Public Health, 2022) reports sleep problems in the preceding 15 days among **15.6% of adolescent males and 23.5% of adolescent females, rising to 18.4% and 33.2% among young adults**, with **more than two hours of smartphone use** raising the odds of sleep problems. The sex gradient runs opposite to the rest of this section, where the exposed populations — gig riders, night-shift agents, irrigating farmers — are overwhelmingly male. Screen-mediated sleep loss is the one route in this file that falls harder on women, and the mechanism is likely compounded by the fact that unpaid domestic work leaves the late night as the only unclaimed private time.
- **Caffeine.** A Delhi study of 300 school-going adolescents found **97% consuming caffeine at a mean 121 mg/day**, with tea and coffee supplying over half and the remainder from colas, chocolate and energy drinks. FSSAI has a standard for energy drinks, which makes this one of very few entries in this file with a regulator already holding the object. Recorded for completeness rather than as a frontier: individually held, already regulated, and small relative to the roster and the roof.

**Measurement: better than this file previously claimed, and still not adequate**
- An earlier version of this section stated that India has no national sleep data at all. That is too strong and is corrected here. The **Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI)** is nationally representative and carries sleep items; published work uses it to estimate sleep-disorder prevalence by occupation among adults **45 and over**. A 2023 systematic review gives pooled Indian prevalence figures: **insomnia 25.7%, obstructive sleep apnoea 37.4%, restless leg syndrome 10.6%**, with insomnia at **15.1% in otherwise healthy populations against 32.3% in patient populations**, and excessive daytime sleepiness at **19.6% among the healthy**.
- What is actually missing is narrower and worth stating precisely. **There is no population-representative sleep-duration measurement for Indians below 45** — NFHS covers nutrition, anaemia and sanitation but not sleep, and there is no Indian equivalent of the CDC's BRFSS or YRBS sleep items — which is to say the working-age population that this entire section is about is the one age band with no denominator. Every claim above therefore still rests on occupational studies, global datasets with Indian subsamples, or inference from housing and labour data.
- **The second gap is diagnostic capacity rather than epidemiology.** Against an estimated **~104 million Indians with OSA**, the literature attributes systematic underdiagnosis to absent infrastructure, too few trained sleep-medicine specialists, low detection by primary-care clinicians, and cost — sleep medicine being a recent subspecialty in India with few training fellowships. This is the same shape as the *Legionella* entry in `05-sanitation-disease.md`: prevalence estimable in principle, diagnosis unavailable in practice, so the case count reflects testing capacity rather than disease. It is a distinct failure from the absence-of-measurement pattern in `03-air.md` (mesothelioma), `02-water.md` (aquifer storage) and `04-shelter-thermoregulation.md` (houselessness), and this file contains both.
- The original conclusion survives the correction: sleep remains the least-measured tier-1 need in India, and it is measured least in exactly the populations losing the most of it.

**Who is already commercially working on this, and who pays them**
- Required by the commercial-landscape pass, and here it tests the structural claim at the top of this file directly. If sleep truly has nothing to procure, there should be no industry. There is a large one.
- **The market is real and sizeable.** Mattresses and sleep products run about **₹15,000 cr (~$2.57B, 2026) growing to ~$3.89B by 2031**, with over 60% still unorganised; **Wakefit posted ₹1,310 cr FY25 revenue (+24%), raised $154.76M, and listed in December 2025 at roughly $734M** — the first Indian sleep company to IPO. Alongside: Sheela Foam (Sleepwell/Kurlon), Duroflex, Peps; Ultrahuman selling sleep-tracking rings; melatonin sold freely as an unregulated wellness SKU (HK Vitals, Wellbeing Nutrition, Setu, Carbamide Forte); **113 India-based meditation-app startups**, the third-largest such market globally.
- **The structural claim survives, refined rather than refuted.** What the industry sells is a *surface to lie on* and an *aid to falling asleep* — downstream artifacts — not the removal of interference. It sells to households whose interference is already low: fixed hours, quiet housing, some cooling. **Sleep commerce serves the unexposed with adjacent goods; it does not serve the exposed with remedies.** So the claim becomes sharper, not weaker: there is nothing to procure *for the people losing sleep*, and the existence of a $2.5B market alongside them is the evidence, not the counter-evidence.
- **Not one product in the market is bought by a party that imposes sleep loss.** No gig platform, BPO or GCC employer, discom or landlord appears anywhere as a customer. Every rupee is spent by a consumer who is not the person the India section above is about.
- **The one position that would invert this does not exist in India.** Fatigue-risk management systems — sold to airlines and mining operators elsewhere, where the employer buys rest because the employer carries the liability — have no confirmed Indian equivalent. What exists is AIS-140 telematics and driver-behaviour monitoring (FleetX, Intangles) sold to logistics operators on a harsh-braking and asset-protection framing, **not a fatigue framing**. The distinction matters: the data being collected is largely the data a fatigue product would need, and nobody is selling the interpretation, because no Indian operator carries priced liability for a tired driver. **That is the file's clearest single opening**, and it is a business-model gap rather than a technology gap.
- **OSA diagnostics is a genuine market and a poor proxy for capacity.** ResMed India sells direct-to-consumer home sleep tests (~₹2,680, 24–48 hour report) and CPAP retail; the sleep-apnoea device market runs **$209.35M (2025) → $515.18M (2033)**. It is concentrated in Delhi-NCR and metro private hospitals against the **~104 million** national estimate. Device SKUs and awareness campaigns are proliferating while trained sleep-medicine specialists and labs outside metros are not — entry density mistaken for diagnostic coverage, exactly as flagged in the measurement note above.
- **Air conditioning belongs in this list and is covered in `04-shelter-thermoregulation.md`**: a market heading toward $21.6B at ~8–10% household penetration, with the rural and Tier-3 households that carry the thermal sleep loss gated out by electricity cost and grid reliability rather than by appliance price alone. Penetration is projected to reach only ~40% over two decades, which sets the timescale on the single largest lever in this file.

**Noise as an exposure in its own right.** Treated above as one input among several, but Indian urban noise is a standalone environmental exposure: ambient night-time levels in metros routinely exceed the CPCB residential limit of 45 dB(A), with construction, traffic, generator sets and amplified religious and festival sound as the main contributors. Enforcement is complaint-driven, so exposure is highest exactly where residents have least standing to complain. India has continuous ambient noise monitoring in only a handful of cities — the same detection asymmetry as air, and with even less attention.
