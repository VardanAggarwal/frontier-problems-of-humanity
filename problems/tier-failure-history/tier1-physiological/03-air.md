# Air / Breathable Environment (Tier 1 — Physiological)

Tier definition: short-horizon cellular/bodily survival.

## How this need has been threatened

**Volcanic and natural asphyxiation**
- **79 CE, Pompeii/Herculaneum**: Vesuvius's pyroclastic surges killed most victims via thermal shock and asphyxiation on superheated ash and gas, not just burial. Pliny the Younger's letters describe choking ash clouds at Misenum.
- **1783–84, Laki eruption, Iceland**: SO2 and fluorine haze ("mist hardship," *móðuharðindin*) killed ~50% of livestock and ~20–25% of Iceland's population via poisoning and famine; the sulfurous haze reached across Europe, implicated in tens of thousands of excess deaths in Britain and France.
- **21 August 1986, Lake Nyos, Cameroon**: a limnic eruption released ~1.2 million tonnes of CO2 that rolled downhill as an invisible, denser-than-air cloud, asphyxiating ~1,746 people and thousands of livestock in nearby valleys within hours (Kling et al., 1987, *Science*).

**Chemical and gas warfare**
- **22 April 1915, Second Battle of Ypres**: Germany's first large-scale chlorine gas attack on the Western Front; ~1,100 Allied deaths outright, thousands more casualties, opening industrial chemical warfare.
- **1917 onward, WWI mustard gas**: sulfur mustard caused blistering and pulmonary damage rather than instant death, but produced ~120,000+ Allied gas casualties by war's end; total WWI gas casualties across all sides estimated over 1.3 million, ~90,000 fatal (per various post-war Allied tallies).
- **16 March 1988, Halabja, Iraq**: Iraqi forces under Saddam Hussein dropped sarin, tabun, VX, and mustard gas on the Kurdish town, killing an estimated 3,200–5,000 civilians within hours and injuring 7,000–10,000, per Human Rights Watch documentation.
- **1994–95, Aum Shinrikyo sarin attacks, Japan**: Matsumoto (1994, 8 dead) and the Tokyo subway (20 March 1995, 13 dead, ~6,000 injured) marked non-state chemical terrorism using nerve agent aerosolized in enclosed public transit.

**Industrial and urban smog**
- **1930s–1940s, Meuse Valley fog, Belgium (1930)** and **Donora, Pennsylvania smog (26–31 October 1948)**: temperature inversions trapped sulfur dioxide and particulates from steel/zinc works; Donora killed ~20 and sickened ~7,000 of its 14,000 residents in five days — the first US industrial smog death event to draw federal attention.
- **5–9 December 1952, Great Smog of London**: a windless anticyclone trapped coal-smoke sulfur dioxide and soot over the city; contemporary estimates put deaths at ~4,000, but later epidemiological reanalysis (Bell & Davis, 2001, *Environmental Health Perspectives*) raised the toll to ~12,000 from respiratory and cardiovascular causes.
- **1943–present, Los Angeles photochemical smog**: unlike coal-smoke smog, LA's haze (first formally studied by Arie Haagen-Smit, 1952) came from vehicle exhaust and sunlight reacting to form ozone — a distinct chemistry requiring separate regulatory response (catalytic converters, not just scrubbers).
- **2013–present, Beijing "Airpocalypse" and Delhi winter smog**: January 2013 Beijing PM2.5 readings exceeded 800–900 µg/m³ (WHO safe guideline ~5 µg/m³ annual); Delhi's post-Diwali/crop-stubble-burning smog regularly pushes AQI past 450 each November.

**Mining hazards**
- **Firedamp (methane) explosions**: recurrent through industrial coal mining — e.g., **Courrières mine disaster, France, 10 March 1906**, killed 1,099 miners, Europe's worst mining disaster, triggered by coal dust/gas ignition.
- **Black lung (coal workers' pneumoconiosis)**: chronic inhalation of coal dust causing progressive fibrosis; despite regulation, a documented US resurgence in Appalachia was reported by NIOSH/NPR investigations (2018) finding the largest cluster of advanced black lung ever recorded, tied to thinner coal seams requiring cutting through silica-rich rock.
- **Asphyxiation/oxygen depletion**: routine cause of death in unventilated shafts and wells throughout mining history, addressed only gradually by forced-air ventilation engineering.

**Occupational and indoor air**
- **Asbestosis and mesothelioma**: mass industrial use of asbestos (insulation, shipbuilding, construction) from the late 1800s through the 1970s caused a wave of mesothelioma deaths peaking decades after exposure; the UK alone still records ~2,500 asbestos-related deaths/year (HSE data).
- **Silicosis**: historically devastating in hard-rock mining and sandblasting — the **Hawk's Nest Tunnel disaster, West Virginia, 1930–31**, killed an estimated 764+ workers (many Black migrant laborers) from acute silicosis after unprotected drilling through silica-rich rock, one of America's worst industrial disasters.
- **Indoor cooking-fire smoke**: the largest ongoing air-related death toll globally and historically underrated — WHO (2023) attributes ~3.2 million deaths/year to household air pollution from solid-fuel cooking (wood, dung, coal) on open fires, disproportionately killing women and children in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa; this mechanism has operated at scale for millennia, essentially unrecorded until 20th-century epidemiology caught up.
- **Wildfire smoke**: escalating with climate-driven fire seasons — **2019–20 Australian "Black Summer"** and **2023 Canadian wildfire smoke over the US Northeast** (NYC AQI exceeding 400 in June 2023) exposed tens of millions to hazardous PM2.5 spikes in modern, previously "clean-air" cities.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

**Personal protective technology**
- Primitive wet-cloth face coverings against WWI chlorine gas gave way to the **box respirator (1916)** and eventually standardized **gas masks**, driving the founding of dedicated chemical-defense corps and, post-war, the **1925 Geneva Protocol** banning chemical/biological weapons in warfare (though not their production or stockpiling).
- Industrial respirators and later NIOSH-rated N95/P100 masks became mandatory PPE in mining, sandblasting, and asbestos work following silicosis and asbestosis litigation and regulation.

**Mine ventilation engineering**
- Forced-air ventilation shafts, methane detection (canaries, then Davy safety lamps from **1815**), and later electronic gas sensors directly targeted firedamp explosions and asphyxiation, becoming standard mine design after disasters like Courrières.
- Modern mine safety regulation (US **Federal Mine Safety and Health Act, 1977**, post-Farmington disaster) mandates dust suspension limits and ventilation rates tied directly to black lung incidence.

**Smokestack and fuel regulation**
- **UK Clean Air Act, 1956**: passed directly in response to the 1952 Great Smog, created smoke-control areas, banned dark smoke emissions, and pushed households from coal to smokeless fuel/gas heating.
- **US Clean Air Act, 1963**, strengthened in **1970** (creating the EPA) and **1990**: set national ambient air quality standards, mandated catalytic converters for vehicle exhaust (targeting LA-style photochemical smog), and established a permitting/enforcement regime for industrial emitters.
- China's post-2013 "war on pollution" and India's National Clean Air Programme (2019) represent later, developing-world analogues, using vehicle emission standards, coal-plant retrofits, and seasonal construction/burning bans.

**Air quality monitoring**
- The **Air Quality Index (AQI)**, formalized by the US EPA in the 1970s–1999 and adopted with local variants worldwide, translated invisible pollutant concentrations into a public, actionable daily signal, enabling health advisories and school/outdoor-activity closures during smog spikes.

**Indoor air and cookstove initiatives**
- Recognition of household air pollution as a top global killer drove the **Global Alliance for Clean Cookstoves (2010, later Clean Cooking Alliance)** and national LPG-subsidy programs like India's **Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (2016)**, aiming to shift hundreds of millions of households off open biomass fires.
- Building codes evolved separately to address asbestos abatement, radon mitigation, and mechanical ventilation standards (ASHRAE 62.1) for enclosed occupied spaces.

**International treaties on atmospheric threats**
- The **Montreal Protocol, 1987** — prompted by discovery of the Antarctic ozone hole (1985) — phased out CFCs globally and is the most successful environmental treaty in history, with the ozone layer now on a recovery trajectory (UNEP assessments, 2018–2023). It stands as the clearest precedent that coordinated global regulation can reverse an atmospheric-scale threat, informing later climate-treaty design (Kyoto, Paris).
- Chemical weapons control matured from the toothless 1925 Geneva Protocol into the **Chemical Weapons Convention (1993, in force 1997)**, backed by the OPCW's verification and destruction inspections regime.

### Where it worked

- **India's leaded-petrol phase-out, completed 2000 — the largest measured public-health win in this file, and the reason the remaining lead problem is invisible.** Mean childhood blood-lead across Mumbai, Chennai, Bengaluru, Amritsar and Lucknow fell from **18.1 µg/dL in the leaded phase to 12.1 µg/dL after**. Urban air lead in Lucknow dropped from 1.6 µg/m³ (1994) to 0.2 µg/m³ (2002); lead in Ganga water from 18.0 µg/L (1988) to 3.1 µg/L (2001). One regulatory act, one substitution, measurable across three environmental media.
- **The lesson is in what it did not fix.** Removing the single largest source produced a large decline and then the effort stopped — the non-petrol sources (spice adulteration, informal battery smelting, paint, cookware) were never addressed, and **275 million Indian children still carry elevated blood lead**, the largest such population of any country. A win against the most visible source became a reason to stop looking, and the residual is now larger than most active entries in this tier. See `00-summary.md` on the toxic-exposure class.
- **Montreal Protocol compliance.** India phased out CFCs and met its HCFC obligations on schedule under a treaty with financing attached — evidence that Indian industrial compliance follows when a phase-out is globally coordinated and funded, which is precisely the structure asbestos lacks.

## Where this fails today

**Wildfire smoke reaching previously "clean" cities**
- June 2023: smoke from Canadian wildfires pushed New York City's AQI above 400 ("hazardous"), the worst air quality NYC had recorded in decades, visible as an orange haze and detected as far as Europe.
- 2023 was Canada's worst wildfire season on record (~18 million hectares burned, per Natural Resources Canada), and smoke plumes recurred across the US Midwest and Northeast in summer 2024 and 2025. California and Australia continue to see episodic hazardous-smoke events tied to lengthening, climate-driven fire seasons (Australia's 2019–20 "Black Summer" remains the reference case; similar though smaller flare-ups recurred in 2023–2025).

**Indoor air pollution from cooking fires**
- WHO's 2024 update states household air pollution from burning wood, dung, charcoal, and kerosene for cooking still causes an estimated ~3.2 million premature deaths per year, concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, and disproportionately affecting women and children exposed daily to indoor smoke. Roughly one-third of the world's population still lacks access to clean cooking fuels or technology (WHO/IEA "Access to Clean Cooking" tracking, 2023–24 data).

**Climate-driven structural worsening**
- The EPA and WHO both note that climate change is lengthening wildfire seasons and increasing ground-level ozone formation (heat accelerates the photochemical reactions that produce smog), meaning some of the 20th century's regulatory gains (US Clean Air Act reductions) are being partially offset by climate-driven increases in wildfire PM2.5 and heat-driven ozone spikes, per EPA's own air-quality trend reporting through 2023–2024.
- Overall, WHO (2024) estimates ~7 million deaths per year globally from combined ambient and household air pollution — making air quality one of the largest unresolved physiological-tier threats, despite a century of regulatory and technological response.

**Vegetation as a remedy: real mechanism, wrong order of magnitude**
- The intuition that vegetation at continental scale can restore atmospheric balance is mechanically correct and quantitatively insufficient, and the two halves have to be held apart from each other. Trees do absorb carbon at scale; they do not meaningfully clear the pollutant that does the killing.
- **On climate, the sink is real but it is a dependent variable of the thing it is supposed to fix.** The land biosphere has historically absorbed roughly 30% of fossil emissions. In 2023 it absorbed almost nothing: the net land CO2 sink fell to **0.44 ± 0.21 GtC/yr, the weakest since 2003**, driven by Amazon drought and record Canadian wildfire emissions, and atmospheric CO2 at Mauna Loa rose **3.37 ppm — 86% above the previous year** while fossil emissions grew only 0.6% (Ke et al., *National Science Review*, 2024). 2024 did not rebound; CO2 rose a record **3.73 ppm**. The sink weakens under exactly the heat, drought and fire that emissions produce, so it cannot be treated as a stable reservoir to plan against.
- **On planted area, the arithmetic does not close.** Bastin et al. (*Science*, 2019) put global tree-restoration potential at 205 GtC and were contested at roughly **five times too large** (Veldman et al., *Science*, 2019): the estimate assumed restorable land held no carbon already, counted savannas and grasslands as degraded forest, and ignored the albedo warming that makes high-latitude planting net-warming. Even at ~100 GtC, the whole global restoration programme buys about **ten years of current emissions**, once — a stock, against a flow that continues.
- **Forests are not chemically neutral either.** Forestation raises biogenic VOC emissions, which increase tropospheric ozone and methane, and lowers surface albedo; chemistry-albedo feedbacks offset **up to a third** of the CO2-removal benefit and in some scenarios flip the sign entirely (Weber et al., *Science*, 2024).
- **On PM2.5 specifically the effect is near-zero at city scale.** Nowak et al. (2013), modelling ten US cities, found annual average air-quality improvement from the entire urban tree canopy of **0.05% (San Francisco) to 0.24% (Atlanta)**; later scenario work reaches ~2.5% of PM2.5 emissions under deliberately optimised planting. At street level dense canopy can make it worse — trees in enclosed street canyons reduce ventilation and have measured **PM2.5 increases of up to 30%** near leeward walls, with concentration enhanced at pedestrian height.
- The mechanism to carry forward is the one this file has already named twice: **greening is a remedy with high visibility and no identifiable loser**, which is what selects it over source control, not its measured effect. It belongs in the mitigation column alongside emission reduction, not as a substitute for it.

### India: smog, small-town industry, and misdirected spend

**North Indian winter smog, ongoing**
- Delhi has ranked the world's most polluted capital city every year since 2018 — eight consecutive years as of IQAir's 2025 report. Annual average PM2.5 has sat in a narrow band across that period, ~92–100 µg/m³ over 2021–2025 (2025: 99.6 µg/m³, roughly 20x the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³). Winter peaks are driven by crop-stubble burning in Punjab/Haryana, vehicle emissions, construction dust, and temperature inversions trapping pollutants near ground level.
- This recurs annually: November 2023, 2024 and 2025 each produced multi-day "severe" AQI episodes (readings above 450) in Delhi-NCR, prompting school closures, construction bans (GRAP Stage IV), and odd-even vehicle rationing — the same emergency-response playbook repeated each winter rather than a structural fix. Delhi's 2025 annual average fell ~8% year over year, the first meaningful move in the series, but off a base so high that the city remained the world's worst capital; see the NCAP funding section below for why the decline is probably meteorological rather than structural.
- The Lancet Planetary Health (2021) estimate of ~1.67 million annual Indian deaths attributable to air pollution remains the standard figure; no subsequent national assessment has revised it materially downward.

**Small industrial towns, not megacities, are the actual worst-air places on Earth**
- Delhi absorbs the attention, but it is not the worst case even within its own country. IQAir's 2025 report puts **66 of the world's 100 most polluted cities in India**, and five of the global top ten: Loni, Byrnihat, Delhi, Ghaziabad and Ula. The world's 25 most polluted cities are all in India, Pakistan or China. Delhi ranked only third in India.
- **Loni (Ghaziabad district, UP) is the world's most polluted city** — annual PM2.5 of 112.5 µg/m³ in 2025, over 22x the WHO guideline, and up ~23% year over year while the national average edged down. Loni is not a residential suburb that caught someone else's smog: it hosts 1,000+ small manufacturing units (garments, leather, plastics, e-waste, auto parts), most running diesel gensets against grid gaps, and functions as a truck transit corridor between Delhi and western UP. The pollution is locally produced by informal-sector industry operating below the threshold where emission regulation is enforced.
- **Byrnihat (Meghalaya/Assam border, PM2.5 101.1 µg/m³) exposes an airshed-jurisdiction failure.** Meghalaya's own pollution control board attributes the pollution to roughly 20 red-category industries on the *Assam* side of the border, while Byrnihat itself (in Meghalaya) has two. The regulator with authority over the affected residents has no authority over the emitters. Compounding this, the Meghalaya government publicly disputed the IQAir ranking, describing local air quality as "satisfactory" — the state simultaneously blames another state and denies the measurement. Byrnihat received its first government continuous monitoring station only in **December 2025**, *after* being ranked the most polluted town on Earth for 2024 on the basis of non-governmental sensors.
- **These rankings are a floor, not a ceiling.** Per the 2026 State of India's Environment report, only ~15% of India's population (~200 million) lives within 10 km of a continuous air quality monitor; the other ~1.2 billion live outside any measurable range. India operates 562 real-time monitors across 294 cities and 966 manual stations across 419 towns — against roughly 7,900 census towns and cities nationally. Small towns entering the rankings therefore reflect *newly acquired visibility* at least as much as newly acquired pollution — and the unmonitored tail is unknown by construction. The list of worst cities is drawn from the minority of India that is measured.
- **The same resolution problem operates within cities — the Mumbai case.** Coastal metros were assumed to be self-cleaning via sea breeze; Mumbai's 2023 winter season broke that assumption, briefly running worse than Delhi as construction dust load rose and winter winds slackened. But the trend did not hold: per CSE's winter 2024-25 analysis, Mumbai's peak daily PM2.5 was 81 µg/m³ (28 December 2024), a **44% decrease** from the average of the previous three winter peaks. What persists is hyperlocal — Deonar averaged 80 µg/m³ across the winter and Malad W spiked to 194 µg/m³, alongside a distinct NO2 rise from November to January. Mumbai is thus not a city-wide failure but a set of ward-level ones concealed by a city-wide average. The mechanism is identical to the national one: aggregate statistics at too coarse a spatial resolution make the worst-exposed populations disappear.
- Internationally the same pattern holds: Lahore, Pakistan and Dhaka, Bangladesh recur among the most polluted major cities year over year (Lahore's November 2023 smog crisis pushed AQI past 1,900 on local scales, prompting school closures and cross-border smog diplomacy with India), while sub-Saharan African cities such as N'Djamena, Chad rank high on thin monitoring infrastructure that has historically undercounted the true toll.

**The response is funded, and aimed at the wrong sources**
- India's National Clean Air Programme is not an unfunded aspiration — it is a funded programme misallocated at its core. Per CREA's *Tracing the Hazy Air 2026*, of ₹13,415 crore released, ₹9,929 crore was spent (74% utilisation), and **68% of that went to road dust management**, 14% to transport, and negligible amounts to industry and domestic fuel.
- Road dust is predominantly coarse PM10; the mortality burden is driven by fine combustion-derived PM2.5 from industry, vehicles and biomass. Spending is therefore concentrated on the most *visible* fraction of the problem rather than the most lethal one. The political economy explains the choice: mechanical sweepers and water sprinklers are procurement decisions with contractors and no identifiable loser, whereas closing or retrofitting an industrial cluster creates one. Loni and Byrnihat are exactly the cases this allocation cannot touch.
- The spending often precedes the diagnosis: only 90 of 130 NCAP cities have completed source apportionment studies, meaning roughly 40 cities have been allocating abatement budgets without established knowledge of what is polluting them.
- Outcomes match the allocation. Of 130 NCAP cities, only 51 achieved even the original 20–30% PM10 reduction target and just 23 met the revised 40% target — while a separate 23 cities **recorded PM10 increases** over the programme period (the two counts of 23 are coincidental, not the same cities). In 2025, 190 of 229 cities with adequate PM10 data exceeded India's own NAAQS. On PM2.5, 103 of 231 cities exceeded the standard — but India's annual PM2.5 NAAQS is 40 µg/m³, eight times the WHO guideline of 5, so "compliant" cities are largely still at levels the WHO considers hazardous. Compliance is being measured against a lenient benchmark.
- The measurement layer is underfunded alongside: 28 NCAP cities still have no continuous monitoring station, and India remains 465 stations short of its own 1,500 manual-station target. A programme cannot be held to outcomes it has not instrumented itself to observe.
- Net effect at national level: India's average PM2.5 moved from 50.6 to 48.9 µg/m³ (2024→2025) and Delhi's fell ~8%, but IQAir's own lead scientist characterises the national decline as not statistically significant and likely weather-driven. Seven years into NCAP, the observable change is within meteorological noise, while the emergency playbook (GRAP stages, school closures, odd-even rationing) continues to substitute for structural source control each winter.
- **The same selection pressure produces the visible-remedy class.** Delhi's ₹25 crore Connaught Place smog tower, inaugurated August 2021, was measured to cut pollution by at most **17% within a 100 metre radius**; the DPCC told the National Green Tribunal that smog towers are not a practical solution and that on that performance the city would need on the order of **40,000 of them**. The tower has spent long stretches non-functional through the same winters it was built for, and October 2025 cloud seeding over Delhi produced no measurable change. Tree-planting drives fall in the same class on the evidence in the global section above. Each shares the road-dust property: procurable, photographable, and directed at no emitter. India separately lost **2.33 million hectares of tree cover between 2001 and 2023** (Lancet Countdown 2025) — the plantation counts and the canopy-loss figure are not reconciled anywhere in official reporting, which is itself the finding.
- *Data caution for anyone citing these numbers*: the 2025 IQAir report gives Delhi both 82.2 µg/m³ (as "New Delhi", in the capital-cities ranking) and 99.6 µg/m³ (as "Delhi", in the cities ranking), reflecting different administrative boundaries. Always specify which.

**Silicosis: the same disease as the 1930 case above, still running at national scale**
- The historical entry for silicosis in this file is Hawk's Nest Tunnel, 1930. The present-day version is Indian and larger. Researchers project **~52 million Indian workers exposed to silica dust** — stone quarrying and cutting, sandstone, agate grinding, construction, foundries. Measured prevalence in surveyed Rajasthan blocks runs as high as **79%**; of roughly 8,000 stone-carving workers screened in Sirohi district, close to half were certified silicotic.
- **Rajasthan built the compensation machinery and it works — the detection layer is what is missing.** The state's 2019 Pneumoconiosis Policy runs screening camps, medical boards and a grant-disbursement portal; **22,000+ certifications and over ₹250 crore disbursed**. But against 3 million-plus mining workers in the state, certified cases represent under 1%. The scheme pays reliably for the cases it finds and has no mechanism obliging it to find more.
- This is the sharper form of the measurement failure that recurs across this tier: **paying for a harm substitutes for counting it**, and leaves the liability bounded by the size of the detection effort. The same shape appears in manual scavenging (`05-sanitation-disease.md`) — Supreme Court compensation mandated since 1993, districts self-declaring themselves free — and in asbestos, where no registry exists at all.
- Silicosis is 100% preventable with wet drilling, ventilation and respirators. It is the purest "solution at hand" case in this file after asbestos, and unlike asbestos it does not even require a ban — only enforcement of controls that already exist on paper.

### India: who is already commercially working on this, and who pays them

Required by the commercial-landscape pass.

- **Consumer air purifiers are the densest market and serve close to the least-exposed population.** The category runs **$507M (2024) toward a projected $1.8B (2033)** — Airveda (₹4.31 cr FY25 revenue, largely B2B monitors for corporates and construction sites), Atlanta Healthcare, Nirvana Being, Atovio (bootstrapped, ~18,000 units at ₹3,500), Praan (which failed to raise in India and raised in the US instead). The buyer is an affluent urban household or a corporate office. Against that: **52 million silica-exposed workers with under 1% detection**, and outdoor workers, informal-industry labourers and slum residents who buy nothing. Problem specification followed ability to pay, not exposure.
- **Silicosis and lead have no commercial actor of scale, and the missing element is a payer rather than a technology.** ICMR-NIOH developed a **point-of-care CC16 finger-prick silicosis screening test** — a public research output, not a product line anyone sells. Wet drilling, ventilation and respirators are settled controls with no company selling them into informal quarrying at scale. Blood-lead testing is likewise absent as a market despite **275 million children with elevated blood lead**. Quarry workers cannot pay, unorganised employers will not, and no insurer carries the risk.
- **Rajasthan's silicosis scheme shows why no detection market formed.** **Over 22,000 certifications and roughly ₹250 cr disbursed** — a compensation layer that pays *after* disease is certified rather than funding the finding of it. That caps liability at the number of people who manage to get certified and leaves the denominator unknown, which is `00-summary.md` mechanism 7 (*compensation substitutes for counting*) reappearing as a reason no diagnostics industry exists. A payout ceiling is not a market signal.
- **Ambient sensing sells to governments and platforms, not to the exposed.** **Respirer Living Sciences** has deployed 2,000+ monitors including ~700 across UP and Bihar; **Ambee** sells hyperlocal air-quality data as a service. Revenue comes from government contracts, corporate ESG and platform partnerships. The detection layer this tier keeps finding absent is, in air specifically, commercially viable — but only because a non-exposed third party buys the data.
- **CEMS is a functioning compliance market.** CPCB mandated continuous emissions monitoring across **17 industry categories (2014)**, with CSIR-NPL certifying instruments since 2019. The payer is the emitter under mandate. Together with ZLD in `02-water.md`, this is the second instance in the tier of regulation constituting an industry — and both attach to a countable set of permit-holding facilities.
- **Stubble burning is being solved by a mandate written for a different reason.** Punjab had **16 operational biomass pelletisation plants with 21 more expected by late 2024**, against a **₹50 cr CPCB subsidy (₹12.37 cr disbursed)** — but the demand actually comes from the **7% biomass co-firing obligation on thermal plants within 300 km of Delhi (FY25–26)**. Happy Seeder and the PUSA bio-decomposer remain subsidised equipment rather than businesses, because at farm-gate prices the farmer's cost-benefit does not clear. The pellet market works because a power utility must buy; the seeder does not because only the farmer would.
- **Clean cooking is the tier's best-documented capital graveyard, and the reason is instrument design.** Ujjwala delivered **70M+ LPG connections**; refill rates run at **less than half those of general rural consumers**, **25% of beneficiaries took zero or one refill in 2022–23 (CAG)**, and no household cohort sustained LPG above 55% of cooking time. The subsidy covered **capex (the connection) and not opex (the refills)** — cost, not access or awareness, is the binding constraint (SEI 2024; *Nature Energy* 2019). Commercial actors selling *improved biomass* stoves — Greenway (~550K units), Envirofit, Prakti — survive precisely by not attempting fuel switching. This is *instrument keyed to the wrong object* with an unusually clean measurement of the miss.
- **Construction dust is the most commercially served failure by rupee volume and among the least well-matched to harm.** CREA's *Tracing the Hazy Air* (2026) finds **68% of ₹9,929 cr in NCAP spending** went to mechanical sweepers, sprinklers and anti-smog guns. A procurement market exists, the only payer is a municipal tender, and the spend is not aimed at the sources carrying the mortality burden — the same *spend mismatched to source* finding recorded earlier in this file, now visible from the vendor side.

### India: stored risk, not yet realised

**Asbestos still legally imported and used at scale in India**
- Unlike the smog problems above, this is a *solved* problem being actively un-solved. Roughly 68 countries have banned all forms of asbestos; India banned only *mining* (phased out from 1986) while leaving import and use of chrysotile ("white asbestos") fully legal. India has been among the world's largest importers for over a decade, taking in roughly **360,000–485,000 tonnes a year** across recent reporting years (379,043 t in 2014; 361,164 t in 2019–20; ~485,000 t cited for 2023) — sourced ~85% from Russia, with the remainder from Brazil, Kazakhstan, Hungary, Poland and South Africa (IBAS / British Safety Council India). Treat the year-on-year series with caution rather than as a clean trend: figures differ by source depending on whether they count chrysotile only or all asbestos-containing imports, so the reliable claim is sustained scale, not a specific growth rate. Most of it goes into asbestos-cement roofing sheets, explicitly marketed as an affordable building material for poor households — the hazard is being installed into low-income housing stock as a *development* product.
- The disease burden is structurally invisible. India has no central mesothelioma registry, no systematic occupational-disease surveillance, few pathologists trained to diagnose asbestos-related disease, and low general awareness that chrysotile causes asbestosis, lung cancer and mesothelioma. Officially reported case counts are therefore near-zero in a country consuming hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year — absence of data is being read as absence of harm.
- Latency compounds the invisibility: mesothelioma typically presents 20–40 years after exposure. The UK's continuing asbestos death toll (noted above) is the delayed bill for use that peaked in the 1960s–70s, decades after the material was banned there. India's exposure is peaking *now*, meaning the mortality wave arrives in the 2040s–2060s, after the political window for prevention has closed.
- **Open question — ambient vs. occupational exposure.** The literature frames asbestos as an occupational hazard (miners, shipbuilders, insulation workers). India's situation may add a second route: construction and demolition dust as an *ambient* urban exposure, affecting people who never worked with the material. Indian cities already run high PM10 loads dominated by construction dust; if fibre release from asbestos-cement demolition and cutting is a meaningful fraction, the exposed population is orders of magnitude larger than the occupational cohort. This would also offer a partial explanation for lung cancer in non-smokers. Speculative — ambient asbestos fibre measurement in Indian cities is thin to nonexistent, which is itself the finding. The mechanism generalises: other mineral dusts released by mass construction may carry undiscovered hazards on the same detection lag.
