# Shelter & Thermoregulation (Tier 1 — Physiological)

> Enclosure that holds body temperature inside survivable bounds against heat and cold.

Short-horizon survival need: a body loses to hypothermia in hours, to hyperthermia in hours-to-days, and to exposure-driven infection within days. Shelter is the buffer. History is a record of that buffer being destroyed faster than it can be rebuilt.

## How this need has been threatened

- **1666 — Great Fire of London.** Destroyed ~13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, made ~70,000-100,000 of London's 80,000 residents homeless overnight. Wood-and-thatch construction was the failure mode.
- **1871 — Great Chicago Fire.** ~17,500 structures destroyed, ~100,000 left homeless (a third of the city's population) in a city built almost entirely of wood.
- **1900s-1930s — Industrial Revolution slums and tenements.** Rapid urbanization (Manchester, Glasgow, New York's Lower East Side) packed workers into overcrowded, unheated, poorly ventilated tenements. Friedrich Engels' *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) documented Manchester cellar-dwellings; NYC's 1900 census found ~2.3 million people in ~82,000 tenements, many windowless.
- **1947 — Partition of India.** Roughly 14-16 million people displaced between India and Pakistan within months; refugee camps (Kurukshetra held ~300,000) formed under monsoon and winter conditions with no permanent shelter; hundreds of thousands died en route, many from exposure alongside violence.
- **1939-1945 — WWII strategic bombing of housing.** Dresden firebombing (Feb 1945) destroyed ~1,600 acres of the city center, killed an estimated 25,000, left the city functionally shelterless. The London Blitz (1940-41) destroyed or damaged over a million houses; ~1.4 million Londoners were made homeless at some point. Post-war Europe had an estimated 40 million displaced persons, many without housing through the winter of 1945-46.
- **1948-1951 — Post-WWII European housing shortage.** UK alone needed an estimated 750,000+ new dwellings after the Blitz; families lived in prefab "temporary housing" units (over 150,000 built 1945-49) meant to last 10 years, some occupied into the 1970s.
- **1830s-1900s — Native American forced displacement.** The Indian Removal Act (1830) and Trail of Tears (1838-39) forcibly relocated ~60,000 people from ancestral homelands to reservations; an estimated 4,000+ Cherokee died en route, largely from exposure and disease during winter marches. Reservation housing policy through the 20th century left chronic overcrowding and substandard shelter (Pine Ridge, Navajo Nation still show some of the worst housing-quality statistics in the US).
- **1948-1994 — South African apartheid townships and forced removals.** The Group Areas Act (1950) and Bantu Resettlement Act forcibly relocated over 3.5 million non-white South Africans, notably Sophiatown (1955, ~65,000 people) and District Six, Cape Town (1966-1982, ~60,000 people), into townships with substandard, state-built shelter.
- **1976 — Tangshan earthquake, China.** Magnitude 7.6, killed an estimated 240,000-655,000, destroyed roughly 90% of buildings in a city of over 1 million, leaving hundreds of thousands without shelter through winter.
- **2003 — European heatwave.** Killed an estimated 70,000 people across Europe, disproportionately elderly people in un-air-conditioned apartments; France alone recorded ~14,800 deaths. Exposed the lethality of thermoregulation failure indoors, not just outdoors.
- **2005 — Hurricane Katrina, US Gulf Coast.** Destroyed or severely damaged over 300,000 homes in Louisiana and Mississippi; displaced over 1 million people, with tens of thousands sheltering in the Superdome and Convention Center under acute sanitation and thermal-stress conditions.
- **2011-present — Syrian civil war displacement.** Over 6.7 million internally displaced and 5.5+ million refugees (UNHCR figures, sustained through the 2010s-2020s); informal tented settlements in Lebanon's Bekaa Valley and Jordan's Zaatari camp (peaked near 80,000 residents) exposed populations to winter cold (2013-14 winter storm Alexa killed refugee children in Lebanon/Syria camps from hypothermia) and summer heat.
- **2023 — Turkey-Syria earthquake.** Magnitude 7.8, destroyed or damaged an estimated 230,000+ buildings, left ~1.5-2 million people needing emergency shelter in near-freezing February temperatures; WHO and UNHCR flagged hypothermia as an active secondary death risk in the days following.
- **2023-2025 — Gaza.** UN OCHA and satellite damage assessments estimate 60-70%+ of structures in Gaza damaged or destroyed by mid-2024; over 1.9 million people internally displaced, many in tent encampments through winter rains and summer heat, with UNRWA and WHO reporting hypothermia deaths among displaced infants in winter 2023-24.
- **Ongoing — urban unhoused cold/heat deaths.** US National Weather Service and CDC data attribute hundreds of hypothermia deaths annually among unhoused populations (e.g., Cook County, Chicago: dozens per severe winter); Phoenix, Arizona recorded record heat-associated deaths among the unhoused in summer 2023 (Maricopa County: 645 heat-associated deaths countywide, with unhoused people a disproportionate share).

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **Vernacular climate-adapted architecture, prehistoric-present.** Igloos (Inuit, thermal mass of packed snow, interior can reach 16°C against -45°C outside), adobe/thick-mass walls in the arid Southwest US and Middle East (thermal lag smooths day-night swings), stilt houses in Southeast Asia and West Africa (flood and heat/ventilation adaptation), Yakha/Central Asian yurts (portable insulated felt structures for nomadic pastoralists), and Scandinavian log construction — each a localized, empirically-tuned solution predating any formal engineering.
- **1666-1667 — London Building Act / rebuilding codes post-Great Fire.** First major legal code mandating brick/stone construction, street widths, and party walls, directly triggered by the fire; template for later municipal fire codes worldwide.
- **1871-1893 — Post-Chicago Fire building codes.** Chicago adopted fireproofing ordinances and building height/material regulation that shaped the US building-code tradition and, indirectly, the steel-frame skyscraper (fire-resistant structural steel + terra cotta cladding).
- **1935 onward — US Social Security Act and later public housing.** US Housing Act of 1937 created federally-funded public housing; expanded post-WWII (Housing Act of 1949, "a decent home for every American family").
- **1945-1980s — UK council housing program.** Post-Blitz reconstruction plus the 1949 Housing Act built roughly 1 million council homes in the first postwar decade; by 1979 nearly a third of UK households lived in council housing — the largest state-run shelter-guarantee program in peacetime history.
- **1950 (established) — UNHCR and refugee shelter standards.** Formalized the tent/shelter kit system, later codified in the *Sphere Handbook* (1997, revised regularly) setting minimum standards for covered floor space (3.5m² per person), thermal comfort, and camp planning — the direct institutional answer to Partition-, WWII-, and Syria-scale displacement.
- **1976 Tangshan → Chinese seismic building code overhaul.** Led to a national seismic design code (GB50011) and, decades later, informed the 2008 Sichuan post-earthquake reconstruction, which rehoused millions within 3 years using prefabricated and reinforced techniques.
- **1995 — Kobe earthquake reconstruction, Japan.** Killed 6,434, destroyed ~200,000 buildings; response created Japan's modern seismic retrofit subsidy system and temporary-housing-to-permanent-housing pipeline, later exported globally as a reconstruction-sequencing model.
- **2003 European heatwave → heat-health action plans.** France created the *Plan Canicule* (2004) with mandated cooling centers, wellness checks on elderly residents, and heatwave alert levels; adopted in modified form across the EU and by the WHO as a template for heat-mortality prevention.
- **1979-present — FEMA and US disaster shelter/rebuilding pipeline.** Post-Katrina reforms (Post-Katrina Emergency Management Reform Act, 2006) restructured federal disaster housing assistance, trailer/manufactured-home deployment, and rebuilding grants.
- **Insulation and heating technology diffusion, 20th century.** Fiberglass insulation (commercialized 1938), central heating and later heat pumps, and building-envelope standards (US energy codes from the 1970s oil shocks onward) cut the lethality of cold exposure for housed populations to near zero in wealthy countries.
- **Tenant protection and rent law, 20th century.** UK Rent Acts (1915 onward), US eviction-moratorium and right-to-shelter statutes (e.g., New York City's 1981 right-to-shelter consent decree, *Callahan v. Carey*) formalized a legal floor against involuntary homelessness, distinct from physical construction remedies.
- **Cooling-center and extreme-heat shelter networks, 2000s-present.** US cities (Phoenix, Chicago) built formal cooling-center networks and heat-emergency declarations after 1995 Chicago heatwave (739 deaths) and repeated Phoenix summers, extending sheltering infrastructure to the unhoused specifically.
- **1999-2018 — Odisha cyclone shelters, the clearest demonstration that shelter mortality is a policy variable.** The 1999 Odisha super cyclone affected ~18 million people and killed roughly 10,000. In response the state created OSDMA and expanded permanent cyclone shelters from **23 (1999) to 879 (2018)**, with capacity for over 500,000 people, paired with a mandatory pre-landfall evacuation protocol. When Cyclone Phailin made landfall in October 2013 at ~250 km/h — the strongest storm to hit India in 14 years — close to 1 million people were evacuated in advance and the death toll was **44**. Fatalities per million affected fell from **779.3 (1999) to 3.55 (2013)**, a ~200x reduction, and Odisha was featured as a success case in the UN's 2015 Global Assessment Report on Disaster Risk Reduction. This is the strongest available evidence in this file that tier-1 shelter mortality responds to deliberate state capacity rather than to income growth — useful as a positive control against which the failures below should be read.
- **2013 — Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan, South Asia's first.** Integrated heat forecasting with early warning, municipal coordination, health-worker risk communication, and land-cover interventions including reflective cool roofs; subsequently replicated in modified form across Indian cities and states. The direct counterpart to France's *Plan Canicule* above, in a far lower-AC-penetration context.

### Where it worked

The Odisha and Ahmedabad entries immediately above are this file's positive controls, and the strongest in Tier 1. Restated here so they are not lost inside the chronology:

- **Odisha cyclone shelters, 1999–2018.** 23 shelters to 879; fatalities per million affected from **779.3 to 3.55**, a ~200-fold reduction. The 1999 super cyclone killed ~10,000; Phailin in 2013 killed 44 with roughly a million people evacuated. Income did not change over this period; state capacity did.
- **Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan, 2013.** South Asia's first, since replicated in 20+ Indian cities. Establishes that heat mortality is a forecast-and-coordination problem before it is a cooling-technology problem.
- Together these are why the failures below should be read as *neglected* rather than intractable — the interventions exist, are cheap, and have measured before/after numbers attached.

## Where this fails today

- **US homelessness, record high.** HUD's January 2024 Point-in-Time Count found 771,480 people homeless — an 18% jump over 2023, the largest one-year rise on record. Families with children rose 39% to over 259,000. Drivers: rising rents, expiry of pandemic rental aid, migration pressure on shelter systems (HUD; Bipartisan Policy Center, 2024/2025).
- **UK housing crisis.** England recorded ~15,000+ people sleeping rough in 2024; 299,100 households experienced acute homelessness, up 21% since 2022. By September 2025, 175,990 children were in emergency accommodation — the 11th consecutive record month — with 134,760 households in temporary housing overall (Crisis; UK government TA statistics).
- **Wealthy-country affordability squeeze.** Canada's OECD house-price-to-income index hit 136.8 in Q3 2024, up ~41 points since 2015 — the steepest deterioration among major economies after Portugal. The US, UK, and Australia all show smaller but still-widening price-to-income gaps over the same period (OECD; Demographia 2024).
- **Sudan — world's largest displacement crisis.** As of late 2025, Sudan's civil war has displaced over 11.7 million people (7.26 million internally, 4.25 million across borders). By January 2025 nearly 5 million displaced people were in camps or informal settlements below WHO minimum shelter standards; humanitarian aid cuts in 2025 left response plans in neighboring Chad under 5% funded (UNHCR; OCHA).
- **Global internal displacement, record high.** IDMC's 2025 Global Report found 83.4 million people internally displaced at end of 2024 — the highest ever recorded. Disasters alone triggered 45.8 million displacements in 2024, more than double the prior decade's annual average; 29 countries logged their worst disaster-displacement year on record.
- **Pakistan 2022 floods, unresolved.** Floods damaged or destroyed 2.3 million homes and displaced 8 million of the 33 million people affected, with reconstruction needs estimated above $16 billion (World Bank). Much of that housing stock remained unrebuilt years later.
- **Gaza.** By January 2025, UN OCHA data showed 92% of housing units destroyed or damaged (160,000 destroyed, 276,000 damaged out of 436,000). Through winter 2024-25, roughly 110,000 of 135,000 displacement tents were themselves damaged, destroyed, or washed away by storms — a shelter failure nested inside a shelter failure.
- **Turkey-Syria earthquake, still unresolved.** The February 2023 quake displaced over 3.3 million people and damaged or destroyed 1.9 million housing units. As of 2025, more than 400,000 people in Turkey remained in temporary container cities; reconstruction progress is contested, with opposition figures claiming only ~30% of pledged rebuilding complete (Hatay province at 18%) against the government's higher figures.
- **Rohingya camps, Cox's Bazar.** Over 1 million refugees remain packed into roughly 10 square miles of bamboo-and-tarpaulin shelter exposed to monsoons, floods, and cyclones. ~150,000 more fled Myanmar into the camps from 2024 onward even as funding collapsed — only 35% of the 2025 response plan's $934.5 million was funded, halving food rations and closing health facilities.
- **Extreme heat, thermoregulation failure indoors and out.** Europe's 2023 and 2024 summers produced an estimated 47,000 and 62,000+ heat-related deaths respectively (mostly elderly people in un-cooled housing), echoing the 2003 heatwave at larger scale. India's 2024 heat wave hit 50.5°C, with independent counts (733 heatstroke deaths) running far above the official toll (219); researchers estimate thousands of excess deaths per day nationally during peak heat, disproportionately among the urban poor and unhoused.

### India: land, affordability, and urban carrying capacity

**Land acquisition without rehabilitation**
- India has displaced an estimated **60–65 million people for development projects since 1947** — the largest such figure of any country. Dams alone account for roughly 16.4 million of 21.3 million project displacements (Fernandes and subsequent multi-state surveys).
- **Fewer than 25% were even partially resettled**; roughly 75% were never rehabilitated. The burden is sharply caste- and tribe-skewed: Adivasis are ~40% of the displaced against ~8.6% of the population, and Dalits a further ~20% — so about 60% of displacement lands on roughly a quarter of the population.
- The structural defect is that **compensation is keyed to land title while shelter loss is not.** The 1894 Land Acquisition Act, and the R&R provisions that replaced it, compensate registered owners. Tenants, landless labourers, forest-dwellers without recorded rights, and informal settlers lose the dwelling and receive nothing, because the instrument recognises property but the harm is to shelter. This is why high compensation outlays coexist with near-total rehabilitation failure — they are not measuring the same population.
- The LARR Act, 2013 introduced consent requirements, social impact assessment, and mandatory rehabilitation, but was diluted during passage under industry and state counter-lobbying; a 2014–15 ordinance sought to remove consent and SIA for five project categories and lapsed after resistance, after which several states legislated their own exemptions. The reform exists and has been substantially unwound at state level.
- Related but distinct entries elsewhere: the food-source dimension of Adivasi habitat loss is in [food](/need/food) (§India), and the Sardar Sarovar displacement and dam-ecology costs are in [water](/need/water) (§India perspective). This entry covers the loss of dwelling specifically.

**Housing priced out of reach, for buying and renting alike**
- India's average **house price-to-income ratio is ~11** — eleven years of a household's entire income, spending nothing on food, transport or rent, to buy a home.
- Supply has moved upmarket rather than expanding at the bottom: homes launched below ₹50 lakh fell from **52.4% of new launches (2018) to 17% (2025)**. The urban affordable-housing deficit stands at ~9.4 million units and is projected to reach ~30 million by 2030, against a total housing shortage estimated at 50–70 million units.
- **Both policy instruments miss the migrant.** PMAY-U is ownership-based, so it structurally excludes migrants and informal workers who hold no land and have no stable documented income. The Affordable Rental Housing Complexes scheme (2020), created specifically for migrant and low-income workers, had allocated only **7% of its targeted units to migrant labourers** as of late 2024. The population most exposed to shelter failure is the one both schemes are least able to reach.

**Construction as a climate driver — a tier-1 need degrading tier 1**
- Buildings and construction account for **~37% of global emissions** (UNEP Global Status Report 2024/25). Cement alone is ~8% of global CO2; cement and steel together ~18%.
- India is the **world's second-largest cement producer** (>8% of global installed capacity, ~500+ Mt/yr) and the second-largest producer of sand, gravel and aggregate (~5 billion tonnes/yr), with each tonne of cement requiring roughly six to seven tonnes of sand and gravel — and pervasive illegal riverbed sand mining as the extraction mode.
- This creates a **within-tier loop rather than a tier conflict**: building the ~30 million urban units India needs, at current material intensity, is a large emissions commitment, and those emissions return as the extreme heat that is already killing the urban poor (see the heat entry above). Shelter provision at scale currently degrades thermoregulation at scale. Neither side of that loop can be dismissed as a lower-priority need — they are the same tier.

**Cities built past their resource carrying capacity**
- Bengaluru is the best-documented case: **paved surface rose 1,005% between 1973 and 2016**, urban area ~1,028% between 1973 and 2017, while **vegetation cover fell 88% and water bodies 79%** over the same period (Ramachandra et al., IISc Energy & Wetlands Research Group).
- The physical consequence is direct: impervious surface raises surface runoff and suppresses groundwater recharge simultaneously, producing flood and scarcity in the same city — Bengaluru's 2022 floods and its 2023–24 borewell crisis (in [water](/need/water)) are the same land-cover change expressed twice. Chennai's 2015 floods follow the same pattern.
- The governance defect: **no Indian city has a statutory resource carrying-capacity assessment that gates development approval.** Traffic, water, waste and air load are managed as separate downstream crises rather than as a single permitting constraint — structurally the same failure as NCAP funding abatement without source apportionment (see [air](/need/air)).

**Shelter built on the land that prevented flooding — with the state as encroacher**
- Bengaluru held **1,452 water bodies and ~80% green cover in the early 1800s**, ~760 lakes by the 1970s, and roughly **216 today**. About **54% of surviving lakes have been encroached** by illegal building; a BBMP survey found encroachment on 80% of lakes in its jurisdiction, across ~4,238 hectares.
- **Government agencies are themselves responsible for over 30% of the total encroached area** — and in Chennai, experts identify government buildings among the principal encroachers on lake land. Chennai's Pallikaranai marsh, a primary flood sink, now carries an MRTS line, a landfill, a railway shed, roads, housing and multiple institutions.
- This is a sharper variant of the authority-mismatch pattern seen at Byrnihat (see [air](/need/air)): there, regulator and polluter sat in different states. Here **the enforcement authority and the violator are the same body**, which removes the possibility of enforcement rather than merely complicating it. Housing built in drained lakebeds and flood zones then converts every heavy monsoon into a shelter-destruction event, closing the loop from land-use decision to tier-1 failure.

**Slope failure — the hazard is mapped in detail and the map binds nothing**
- **Wayanad, 30 July 2024, is India's deadliest landslide.** It destroyed **352 houses and damaged 122 more**; peer-reviewed post-event analysis puts the toll at **373 dead with 218 missing**, above the contemporaneous official counts, and the two have never been reconciled — the same unreconciled-toll pattern as the 2024 heat deaths above.
- **The susceptibility was already known and published.** GSI had long classified the Wayanad–Kozhikode belt as highly susceptible. GSI's National Landslide Susceptibility Mapping, run 2013–2020, covers **4.3 lakh km² at 1:50,000 across 19 states and UTs**. ISRO's *Landslide Atlas of India* (2023) is built on **80,000 recorded landslides between 1998 and 2022 across 147 districts**, and identifies **Rudraprayag and Tehri Garhwal as the highest-density districts — Rudraprayag also carrying the highest exposure by number of houses**. India's detection layer for this hazard is unusually good.
- **It is not connected to any permitting instrument.** The Gadgil committee (2011) classified much of Wayanad as ESZ-1 and recommended a complete ban on quarrying, mining and large-scale construction there; the Centre set it aside for the weaker Kasturirangan report, and the Western Ghats ESA notification has been redrafted six times without ever being notified — the sixth call issued after Wayanad. Wayanad district holds **at least 48 stone quarries**. NDMA's Landslide Risk Mitigation Scheme (2019) funds project-level works and its land-use zoning framework is a *model techno-legal framework* offered to states, not a binding gate. The **Landslide Early Warning System is a prototype in 21 districts across 8 states**, issuing experimental bulletins only from the 2024 monsoon.
- **Joshimath is the slow version of the same failure.** In January 2023 subsidence cracked **849 houses across a town where ~65% of the housing stock was affected and 4 of 9 wards were declared unsafe**; **355 families were relocated**, parts of the town sank **more than 30 cm between December 2022 and December 2024**, and residents have moved back into houses declared unsafe because no rehabilitation package reached them. Attribution between hydropower tunnelling, road widening and the town's own moraine foundation is contested and should stay contested; what is not contested is that a town was built on a known moraine deposit and no authority held the question of whether it should be.
- **This is `Cities built past their resource carrying capacity` restated in the hills.** There, the missing instrument is a statutory carrying-capacity assessment gating approval. Here, the hazard map is far better than the carrying-capacity data ever was, and still gates nothing. That makes slope failure the file's cleanest case of **solution at hand, blocked**: the detection layer is built and paid for, and the step of making it binding on construction approval has not been taken. Where it *was* taken — the Kullu 2023 slide that destroyed eight buildings with no casualties because residents had already been moved out of ground declared unsafe — it worked.
- Rehabilitation performs no better than elsewhere in this file. The Wayanad model township's first phase of **178 houses was inaugurated on 1 March 2026 with 128 of them still incomplete at the end of that month**, twenty months after the slide.

**Forced eviction and demolition as active state practice**
- Distinct from the land-acquisition failure above: acquisition operates through a compensation and rehabilitation machinery, however badly it performs. Punitive and arbitrary demolition operates through none.
- **Over 1.68 million people were evicted from their homes between 2017 and 2023** (Housing and Land Rights Network annual reporting). In 2023 alone, **515,752 people were evicted and 107,449 homes demolished** — roughly 294 houses destroyed per day and 58 people evicted per hour. 2022 and 2023 combined account for ~740,000 people losing their homes to state-driven demolition. An estimated **17 million people live under active threat of eviction**.
- On **13 November 2024** the Supreme Court held punitive bulldozer demolition unconstitutional — a violation of the rights to shelter, life and liberty, and fair trial — and issued binding procedural guidelines, with non-compliance amounting to contempt of court. The instrument is new and its compliance record is untested; this is the entry in this file most likely to change materially within a year, in either direction.

**Conflict displacement inside India — three years of relief camps in Manipur**
- This file records war as a shelter destroyer through Partition, the Blitz, Syria, Gaza and Sudan, all of them historical or foreign. The live Indian instance is Manipur. Government data reports **58,821 people displaced since May 2023 and 217 dead**, with **roughly 4,700–5,000 houses burnt** and 386 religious structures destroyed; independent counts put displacement nearer **67,000**, and the gap is unresolved because no agency counts displaced people in India by statute.
- **More than 58,000 remain in 281 relief camps in 2026.** Three monsoons and three winters in, the camps have become settlements: the conditions reported inside them are overcrowding, no stable income, rationed food, and sexual violence against women and children — a protection failure nested inside a shelter failure, structurally the same as Gaza's tents being destroyed by the weather they were issued against.
- **The rehabilitation package exists and is well funded.** A **₹523 crore Resettlement and Rehabilitation package** in the 2025–26 state budget, ~**7,000 houses sanctioned**, ~**₹124 crore released**, ~**3,000 prefabricated houses built**, 16,500+ resettled against a target of 40,000 by March 2026.
- **The instrument is a housing scheme and the blocker is not housing.** Rehabilitation is routed through a special **PMAY-G** package — a rural ownership-housing programme — while the reason people cannot go back is that their villages are unsafe and, in many cases, their houses are occupied by armed groups. Return requires a security guarantee that no housing scheme can issue. This is **instrument keyed to the wrong object** in its purest form in this file: the state is building the thing it knows how to build, and the missing input is one it is not attempting to supply. Note the symmetry with PMAY-U above, which also delivers ownership to a population whose problem is not ownership.
- Amnesty International's May 2025 assessment, at the two-year mark, was that rehabilitation was urgent and had not happened. The three-year mark passed in May 2026 with the camps still open.

**The shelter that exists amplifies heat — the thermoregulation failure**
- The lethal exposure is often indoors, not outdoors. Measured indoor conditions in Indian slum housing show **tin and asbestos-cement sheet roofs running 4–6°C hotter inside than the outdoor air**. Under identical outdoor conditions, indoor heat stress in Kolkata slum dwellings was greater and more prolonged than in rural village housing — density and roofing material, not climate, being the differentiating variables. During pre-monsoon weeks indoor temperatures routinely exceed safe physiological limits **even at night**, removing the recovery window that makes daytime heat survivable.
- **Only ~8% of Indian households have air conditioning.** Heat mortality therefore concentrates almost entirely in the un-cooled, and within that, in the households whose roof is the amplifier. The same asbestos-cement sheeting marketed as affordable roofing (see [air](/need/air), asbestos entry) is doing double duty as a carcinogen and a heat trap.
- **The fix is cheap, proven, and unadopted at scale.** Field trials across four Indian cities found a single lime-based or acrylic reflective coat cools interiors by **1–4°C**; adding a thin thermocol or bamboo-jute layer roughly doubles that, delivering **6–13°C lower peaks** than bare tin. Against ~8% AC penetration and a national heat-death toll already in the thousands, this is a solution at hand held back by neither cost nor science — the clearest instance in this file of an implementation rather than knowledge gap.

**Overcrowding, and the disease route out of it**
- Everything above treats bad shelter as a *thermal* or *toxic* harm. It is also an *infectious* one, and density is the variable. **Census 2011 counted 65.5 million people in slums, ~17% of the urban population**, and there has been no slum enumeration since. India's own Technical Group on Urban Housing Shortage defines overcrowding as **more than two persons per room**; UN-Habitat uses three. Slum dwelling units typically hold **five to six or more people in a single room used for cooking, sleeping and living**.
- **TB is the clearest route.** India carries **25% of the global TB burden**, with incidence falling from 237 to **187 per lakh between 2015 and 2024** (WHO Global TB Report 2025) — real progress, roughly twice the global rate of decline, driven by detection and treatment. But decomposition of NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 finds **overcrowding accounts for ~10% and 8.2% respectively of the wealth-based disparity in TB prevalence**, with house type a further 8.9% and 9.7%. Among the poorest quintile the population-attributable fractions are **34.2% for low BMI and 28.5% for indoor air pollution** (DHS-based analysis); estimates of the share of Indian TB attributable to undernutrition run considerably higher in some studies and the spread is not settled.
- **Read across the tier, that is one problem with three carriers.** Undernutrition is [food](/need/food), household solid fuel is [air](/need/air), and the room they share is this file. Each is treated as a separate programme with a separate ministry, and TB control is treated as a fourth, run on drugs and diagnostics. The housing determinant is not an input to any TB target, so the fastest-falling component of TB risk is the one being measured and the slowest is the one nobody owns. This is the tier's strongest candidate for a **cross-need node that is not a policy object** — no single instrument controls it, which is precisely why it persists.
- **The measurement is worse than for any other harm in this file.** The NSS 76th round (2018) reports average dwelling floor area of **~46.1 m² urban and 46.6 m² rural** — a mean that tells you nothing about the tail this section is about, since it averages a slum room together with a bungalow. There is no current published persons-per-room distribution for urban India, no slum count since 2011, and therefore no denominator against which any overcrowding intervention could be assessed. **The absence is the finding**, and it is the same absence as the missing homeless count immediately below, the missing mesothelioma registry (see [air](/need/air)) and the unpublished aquifer-storage assessment (see [water](/need/water)).

**No current data on the population this section is about**
- India last counted its homeless population in **Census 2011: 1.77 million houseless people, 938,000 of them urban** — figures widely held to undercount pavement dwellers, transient migrants, and anyone not present at an enumerable address.
- The census due in 2021 was deferred and is now scheduled to conclude in **March 2027**. India has therefore run the world's largest housing programme for over a decade against a 15-year-old baseline, with no current national count of either homelessness or slum population. Progress against a target cannot be assessed when the denominator is a decade and a half stale — the same absence-of-measurement pattern as the missing mesothelioma registry (see [air](/need/air)) and the unpublished aquifer-storage assessment (see [water](/need/water)).

**Rental exclusion by identity — an access failure independent of price**
- **No Indian law prohibits discrimination in the rental housing market.** Documented exclusion falls hardest on Muslims and Dalits, and extends to single women, unmarried men, meat-eaters, and Northeastern migrants; those who do secure tenancies frequently accept restrictive conditions (curfews, visitor bans) to do so. Maharashtra added an anti-discrimination clause under its RERA implementation, but it does not extend to the private rental market where the exclusion occurs.
- Structural significance: this means the affordability failures above cannot be fixed by price alone. A household can clear every income and credit threshold and still be refused shelter on identity — so subsidy, rent control, and supply expansion all leave a residual population unhoused by design. Any shelter intervention measured purely in units or rupees will not detect it.
- Related: the 2020 lockdown exodus exposed the same gap from the employer side — migrant workers held no shelter claim at their destination city, and roughly ten million left on foot when work stopped. The Model Tenancy Act (2021) offered a template for formalising tenure but has seen limited state adoption.

### India: thermoregulation failure at work, not only at home

- The heat entries above concern dwellings. The larger exposure is occupational, and it is absent from most shelter framing because the failure happens where people earn rather than where they sleep. India lost an estimated **247 billion potential labour-hours to heat in 2024** — about **419 hours per working person** — with **agriculture (66%) and construction (20%)** carrying nearly all of it (Lancet Countdown India). The 2023 figure was 181 billion hours, with income loss estimated at ~$141 billion.
- The ILO projects India could account for **34 million job-equivalents lost to heat-driven productivity decline by 2030**, and roughly 5.8% of working hours; heat-related losses put up to 4.5% of GDP at risk on the same horizon. Field measurement in southern Indian workplaces finds wet-bulb globe temperature routinely above the 30°C threshold, associated with a **1.4× increased risk of productivity loss**.
- **The mechanism is the same one this file describes for housing, moved outdoors.** Thermoregulation fails for people who cannot control their thermal environment; a construction worker on an open site and a family under a tin roof are the same failure in two locations, and India's poorest households supply both. The intervention set is also known — work-rest cycling, shaded rest areas, hydration protocol, shifting hours — and is standard in Gulf construction under enforced heat rules, so the gap here is enforcement rather than knowledge.
- **Cold is the neglected half.** This file records US hypothermia deaths among the unhoused but not the North Indian equivalent: cold-wave deaths concentrated among people sleeping rough in the Gangetic plain each December–January, where shelter capacity is a fraction of the houseless population and night temperatures fall near freezing. The mortality is real, seasonal, and even less measured than the heat toll.

### India: lead paint in the housing stock

- The **Regulation on Lead Contents in Household and Decorative Paints Rules, 2016** caps lead in household and decorative paint at 90 ppm — India has the rule. Enforcement testing remains thin, and the rule is prospective: it does not touch the existing painted stock, where deteriorating pre-2016 paint remains a dust-borne exposure route for children in exactly the housing this file describes as otherwise substandard.
- Filed here because the exposure is a property of the dwelling. The residual-lead failure as a whole is a food leaf (paint is one of its carriers); see [food](/need/food) for the dietary route, [air](/need/air) → Where it worked for the leaded-petrol win, and the [tier-1 summary](/tier/1) for why these are one problem.

### India: who is already commercially working on this, and who pays them

Required by the commercial-landscape pass.

- **Affordable housing finance is dense, durable and structurally excludes the population this file is about.** **Aavas Financiers, Aptus Value Housing Finance and Home First Finance** are all listed, all growing AUM in the 15–25% range, and all lending against documentable income and a credit score. Aptus runs only ~52% pure home loans, the rest business and loan-against-property. The segment is well-served *because* it is bankable. The households in the entries above — informal income, no title, migrant — are not underserved by oversight; they are outside the underwriting model by construction.
- **Co-living built a large market for the wrong migrant.** **Stanza Living ($31.9M Series E in 2025, at a reduced valuation), Zolo (450+ properties), Colive ($20M Series B)** serve the salaried white-collar renter, often billed to an employer HR budget. Sector funding went **$143M (2021) → $30.4M (2023) → $8.4M (2024) → $81.7M (2025)** — a 94% collapse and a partial recovery at lower valuations, which is capital testing the bankable-renter thesis and finding the unit economics thinner than expected. For the informal migrant worker, essentially nothing: **Aarusha Homes** is the only named low-cost operator, at unclear scale, and everything else in that space is a government scheme (ARHC, 2020) rather than a company.
- **Cool roofs are the sharpest "solution at hand" in this file and have almost no market.** Vendors exist — Coolroof® Paint (SRI 122), LuminX, Heat Cure — and sell to commercial clients like warehouse and dark-store operators, not to slum households. Deployment at population scale has been government-led (Ahmedabad 2017 onward, Hyderabad, Telangana and Tamil Nadu state cool-roof policies). The blocker is a **split incentive**: the landlord buys the roof and the tenant gets the cooling, and no subsidy or mandate bridges the two. That is a business-model problem, not a technology or a knowledge problem — the third path named in `CLAUDE.md`, with a specific missing instrument.
- **Air conditioning is the same gap in mirror image.** **Voltas (~18% share, 2M+ units FY24), Blue Star, Daikin (₹1,000 cr R&D investment), LG, Hitachi** operate in a market heading from **$6.15B toward $21.6B by 2034** — at **~8–10% household penetration**. The industry is healthy and growing precisely within the buffered decile. Cooling is commercially solved for the households that were never dying of heat.
- **Property catastrophe risk is essentially uninsured, which forecloses the private route to slope-failure zoning.** Home insurance penetration is around **1% in India against ~95% in the US and 70–75% in the UK**, and no commercial parametric residential catastrophe product operates at household scale. I suggested earlier in this project that priced risk transfer could bind the GSI hazard maps where the Western Ghats ESA notification has not — that route is not available at present, because there is no residential insurance market to price into. Where parametric cover exists it is state-bought (Nagaland rainfall and flood cover, 2024; a SEEDS–Howden–New India Assurance cyclone pilot on the east coast).
- **The one genuine instance of capital creating a payer where none existed is heat insurance for informal workers.** **SEWA, with the Arsht-Rock Climate Resilience Center and Blue Marble, Ahmedabad 2023: 21,000 women across seven informal trades, with a parametric payout triggered at two consecutive days at or above 43.72°C.** It is philanthropically backed rather than a standalone insurer product, and 21,000 against **400–500 million informal workers** is a pilot. But it is proof that the exposed population can be made a beneficiary of a priced instrument, which nothing else in this file demonstrates.
- **Developer-side capital destruction is the largest in Indian real estate and is not about affordability.** The Economic Survey (2024) records that **one in five insolvent Indian companies is a real-estate firm** — Amrapali (~42,000 homebuyers, funds diverted), Ansal Housing, Radius. Capital in Indian shelter has been destroyed at scale by governance failure in the formal segment, which is worth separating from the question of whether the informal segment is servable at all. The two are routinely conflated as "housing is hard."
- **No commercial actor exists against rental discrimination or eviction**, and for discrimination the reason is structural rather than economic: there is no legal instrument to build a business around — no enforceable prohibition means no compliance obligation, no audit market, no remedy to sell. Compare ZLD in [water](/need/water) and CEMS in [air](/need/air), where a mandate constituted an industry. Here the mandate does not exist, so neither does the industry.

### India: stored risk, not yet realised

**Seismic exposure of the existing housing stock**
- Roughly **60% of India's landmass lies in earthquake-prone zones** (Seismic Zones III–V), including Delhi in Zone IV, while the dominant residential stock — particularly informal and rural construction — is unreinforced masonry in brick with mud or weak mortar, the failure mode that killed most victims in the 1991 Uttarkashi, 1999 Chamoli and 2001 Bhuj earthquakes.
- Codes exist (**IS 4326**, and **IS 13828** specifically for low-strength masonry). Compliance is the variable: enforced rigorously in Gujarat after Bhuj, weakly or not at all elsewhere, and structurally unenforceable in owner-built informal construction that never enters a permitting process.
- Listed separately because it is a *stored* rather than active failure — near-zero present death toll, high irreversibility once realised. It is the shelter analogue of asbestos latency (see [air](/need/air)): the exposure is being built now and the bill arrives on a schedule nobody controls.
