# Food / Caloric Intake (Tier 1 — Physiological)

Tier definition: short-horizon bodily survival. Without adequate calories and nutrients, death follows within weeks (starvation) or is accelerated within months (deficiency disease).

## How this need has been threatened

### Sudden shock: siege, blockade, war
- **1315–1317, Great Famine of Northern Europe**: successive crop failures from extreme wet weather; chroniclers (Ian Kershaw, "The Great Famine and Agrarian Crisis in England 1315–1322") record cannibalism reports and mortality of 10–15% in some regions.
- **1601–1603, Russian famine**: Tambora-unrelated volcanic winter (likely Huaynaputina, Peru, 1600 eruption) cut growing seasons; ~2 million dead, roughly a third of the population in affected areas, per R.E.F. Smith and David Christian's "Bread and Salt."
- **1944–45, Siege of Leningrad (1941–44)**: German blockade cut the city off; official Soviet figures and historian Richard Bidlack estimate 800,000–1.1 million civilian deaths, mostly starvation, documented in ration-card records.
- **1944–45, Dutch Hongerwinger**: German blockade of the western Netherlands after Market Garden; ~20,000 deaths documented by the Dutch famine studies later used in epidemiology (Lumey et al., prenatal famine cohort studies).
- **Siege of Sarajevo, 1992–95**: Bosnian Serb forces blocked food convoys; UNHCR and humanitarian reports document sustained caloric deficits city-wide for ~46 months.

### Forced/deliberate: weaponized famine, requisition, policy
- **1932–33, Soviet Holodomor**: forced grain requisition in Ukraine and Kazakhstan under collectivization; historian Robert Conquest ("The Harvest of Sorrow") and later Timothy Snyder estimate 3.5–5 million deaths in Ukraine; recognized as genocide by multiple parliaments.
- **1943, Bengal Famine**: British wartime policy — grain export continued, denial policy (removing boats/rice stocks to deny the Japanese), diversion of supplies to military and Calcutta — under Amartya Sen's entitlement-failure analysis ("Poverty and Famines," 1981); ~3 million deaths in a year of adequate aggregate harvest.
- **1959–61, Great Chinese Famine**: Great Leap Forward grain-procurement quotas set against local officials' inflated production reports; Frank Dikötter ("Mao's Great Famine," 2010) and demographer Judith Banister estimate 15–45 million excess deaths, the deadliest famine in recorded history.
- **1967–70, Nigerian Civil War / Biafra blockade**: federal government blockaded Biafra; images of starving children triggered the modern humanitarian-NGO movement (MSF's founding is directly tied to this); estimated 1–2 million deaths, mostly children, per John de St. Jorre's history of the war.
- **2017–present, Yemen**: Saudi-led coalition blockade of Houthi-held ports (Hodeidah) combined with civil war; UN OCHA and IPC classifications documented famine-risk conditions affecting millions, cited as the worst humanitarian crisis of the 2010s.

### Gradual scarcity: soil depletion, population pressure, ecological collapse
- **c. 1200 BCE, Late Bronze Age collapse**: drought across the eastern Mediterranean (documented via Dead Sea and Cyprus paleoclimate cores, Kaniewski et al. 2013) contributed to the collapse of Hittite, Mycenaean, and Ugaritic food systems.
- **9th–10th century, Classic Maya collapse**: soil exhaustion from intensive maize monoculture combined with multi-decade droughts (Richardson Gill, "The Great Maya Droughts," 2000); abandonment of lowland cities in the Yucatán.
- **1845–1852, Irish Great Famine**: gradual dependency on a single potato cultivar (Lumper) collapsed under *Phytophthora infestans* blight; combined with British laissez-faire policy and continued food exports, ~1 million died and 1–2 million emigrated (Cormac Ó Gráda, "Black '47 and Beyond").
- **1930s, US Dust Bowl**: decades of over-plowed prairie topsoil combined with drought produced regional agricultural collapse and malnutrition among displaced farm families (Donald Worster, "Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s").

### Distributional failure: food exists but access is blocked (Sen's entitlement failures)
- **1943 Bengal** (above) is the canonical case Sen used to show famine without absolute food-supply shortfall.
- **1974, Bangladesh famine**: flooding destroyed rural wage-labor demand, not the aggregate rice supply; landless laborers lost purchasing power while food stocks existed — again analyzed by Sen as an entitlement collapse.
- **2011, Horn of Africa famine (Somalia)**: UN-declared famine; humanitarian access blocked by al-Shabaab in famine-affected regions even as food aid existed in-country; FEWS NET and UN estimates of ~260,000 deaths, half children under five (Checchi & Robinson, 2013 mortality study).

### Quality failure: contamination, monoculture fragility, nutrient deficiency
- **16th–18th century, European voyages of exploration**: scurvy (Vitamin C deficiency) killed an estimated 2 million sailors across the Age of Sail per historian Stephen Bown ("Scurvy," 2003); James Lind's 1747 citrus trial aboard HMS Salisbury was the first controlled clinical nutrition trial.
- **Late 19th–early 20th century, colonial and plantation Asia**: beriberi (thiamine deficiency) from polished-rice monodiets caused mass mortality in Japan, Indonesia, and the Philippines; Christiaan Eijkman's research (Nobel Prize 1929) traced it to rice-milling practices.
- **Early 20th century, US South**: pellagra (niacin deficiency) from corn-dependent sharecropper diets caused over 100,000 deaths between 1907–1940 per Joseph Goldberger's US Public Health Service studies (1914–1926).
- **1967–70, Biafra and other 20th-century famines**: kwashiorkor and marasmus (protein-energy malnutrition) became clinically defined during this period, extensively documented by relief physicians and driving the founding of therapeutic-feeding protocols.
- **2008, Chinese melamine milk-powder scandal**: industrial contamination of infant formula killed at least 6 infants and sickened ~300,000, a quality/adulteration failure rather than scarcity (Chinese government and WHO reports).

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

### Agricultural innovation
- **c. 8000 BCE onward**: domestication of wheat, barley, rice, and maize independently in the Fertile Crescent, China, and Mesoamerica — the base technology converting foraging risk into stored surplus.
- **Medieval Europe, 8th–10th century**: the three-field crop rotation system spread, raising yields ~50% over two-field fallow systems and reducing famine frequency (Lynn White, "Medieval Technology and Social Change").
- **1730s–1800s, British Agricultural Revolution**: Charles Townshend's four-field Norfolk rotation (wheat–turnips–barley–clover) eliminated fallow years and enabled sustained population growth.
- **1909, Haber-Bosch process**: synthetic nitrogen fixation; Vaclav Smil estimates it now sustains roughly half of global food production, the single largest input behind 20th-century population growth.
- **1940s–1970s, Green Revolution**: Norman Borlaug's semi-dwarf high-yield wheat (Mexico, from 1944) and IRRI's rice varieties (Philippines, from 1960) roughly doubled cereal yields across Asia and Latin America; credited with preventing famine deaths in India and Pakistan post-1965, though later criticized for water and fertilizer dependency.

### Storage, preservation, and logistics
- **Ancient Egypt, c. 2300 BCE onward**: state granaries under pharaonic administration, documented in the Joseph narrative and Old Kingdom records, used to smooth Nile flood-cycle variability.
- **Ancient China, Han Dynasty (2nd century BCE)**: "ever-normal granary" (changping cang) system — state buys grain in surplus years, sells in lean years to stabilize price and supply; revived repeatedly through Chinese dynastic history and referenced directly in 1930s–40s US New Deal agricultural policy debates.
- **1809, Nicolas Appert's canning process**: developed for Napoleon's armies; industrialized through the 19th century, decoupling food availability from harvest seasonality and geography.
- **1870s onward, refrigerated shipping**: first successful refrigerated meat shipment (Frigorifique, 1876) enabled Argentine and Australian meat exports to Europe, globalizing caloric supply chains.
- **20th century, strategic grain reserves**: US Commodity Credit Corporation (1933) and later national reserves in India (Food Corporation of India, 1965) and China buffer against harvest shocks.

### Governance and policy responses
- **1880s, British India Famine Codes**: following the 1876–78 Great Famine (5.5 million dead), the 1880 and 1883 Famine Codes established early-warning triggers, public-works relief, and grain-price monitoring — one of the first codified state famine-response bureaucracies.
- **1917 and 1939–45, wartime rationing**: UK Ministry of Food rationing (from 1940) and US Office of Price Administration rationing (from 1942) used coupon systems to guarantee minimum caloric access under wartime scarcity, and are credited with improving average nutrition among Britain's poorest.
- **1945–52, Marshall Plan food aid**: direct US grain and food shipments to famine-vulnerable postwar Europe.
- **1961, US Food for Peace / PL 480**: institutionalized surplus-grain food aid as foreign policy tool.
- **1978–2008, Chinese land reform sequence**: decollectivization under Deng Xiaoping's Household Responsibility System (from 1978) reversed the structural cause of the 1959–61 famine and roughly tripled grain output per capita by the mid-1980s.

### International and humanitarian systems
- **1961, founding of the World Food Programme (UN)**: first multilateral standing famine-response institution.
- **1985, Live Aid and the Ethiopian famine response**: mobilized modern mass humanitarian fundraising following the 1983–85 famine (~1 million dead, drought plus civil-war blockade under the Derg regime).
- **1985, FEWS NET (Famine Early Warning Systems Network)**: established by USAID after the Ethiopian famine, using satellite rainfall and crop data to forecast famine risk months in advance.
- **2004, IPC (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification)**: standardized international famine-severity scale, now used by the UN to formally "declare" famine (used in Somalia 2011, South Sudan 2017, Yemen).
- **2008, opening of the Svalbard Global Seed Vault**: international seed-bank backup against agricultural collapse, monoculture failure, or catastrophic crop loss.

### Cultural and religious practices
- **Biblical/Talmudic gleaning laws (Leviticus 19:9–10) and Islamic zakat**: mandated food-sharing obligations to the poor, functioning as pre-state redistributive mechanisms against distributional famine.
- **Ramadan fasting and Lenten fasting**: structured voluntary caloric restriction embedding resilience and communal food-solidarity practices, distinct from famine but culturally adjacent risk-buffering norms.
- **Traditional grain-sharing and seed-saving customs** across Andean, West African, and South Asian smallholder communities: informal seed banks and communal storage predating formal genebanks, documented extensively in agrarian anthropology (e.g., work on Andean potato diversity as a blight-resilience strategy after the Irish precedent).

### Where it worked

- **India's PDS, from the leakiest to a working entitlement in the states that reformed.** Bihar's PDS leakage fell from **68.7% to 19.2%** after end-to-end computerisation, doorstep delivery and Aadhaar-linked portability, against a national residual of ~29%. Chhattisgarh and Tamil Nadu show the same trajectory. This is the tier's cleanest demonstration that a *distributional* failure — the Sen category — responds to plumbing rather than to production, and it is the reason India has famine-proofed itself while remaining nutritionally poor.
- **Salt iodisation, 1962 onward.** Universal salt iodisation moved household coverage above 90% and effectively ended endemic goitre and iodine-deficiency cretinism as mass conditions. A single fortification vehicle, one cheap additive, no behaviour change required — the reference case for what micronutrient policy can do when the carrier is universal.

## Where this fails today

### Active famine and near-famine (2023–2026)
- **Sudan**: IPC confirmed Famine (Phase 5) in parts of the country in 2025, with famine-risk conditions projected into 2026 for Sudan and South Sudan; the FAO/WFP 2025 "Hunger Hotspots" report names Sudan among the highest-concern countries for 2026 alongside South Sudan, Yemen, Palestine, Nigeria, and Somalia (FAO/WFP, "Hunger Hotspots," 2025–26).
- **Gaza (occupied Palestinian territory)**: IPC confirmed Famine in parts of the Gaza Strip in 2025, with continued famine risk in other areas heading into 2026 — the first IPC-confirmed famine determination in the Middle East (IPC Famine Review Committee, 2025).
- **Yemen**: 41,200 people classified in Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) in 2025, the ongoing legacy of coalition blockade and civil war conditions first flagged in 2017 (Global Report on Food Crises, 2025 edition, GNAFC/FAO/WFP/EU JRC).
- **Haiti**: 8,400 people in Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) in 2025, driven by gang blockades of Port-au-Prince supply routes rather than harvest failure — a modern entitlement-collapse case structurally similar to 1943 Bengal (Global Report on Food Crises, 2025).
- **Aggregate scale**: across 47 food-crisis countries and territories, roughly 266 million people (22.9% of their combined population) faced acute food insecurity in 2025, and 1.2–1.4 million people were in Catastrophe/Phase 5 conditions during 2025 — conflict was the primary driver for 147.4 million of them (EU Joint Research Centre / Global Report on Food Crises, Sept. 2025 and April 2026 updates).

### Supply-chain fragility: the Ukraine war
- Ukraine's 2025 exports of corn, barley, wheat, and meslin remained ~35% below 2020 (pre-invasion) volumes; Black Sea port and grain-infrastructure attacks (including strikes on grain-carrying vessels from September 2024) continue to disrupt shipments, with global grain prices roughly doubling from ~$270 to ~$500/ton at points in the disruption (CSIS, "The Russia-Ukraine War and Global Food Security: Impacts Four Years Later," 2025–26; INFORMS/phys.org, Jan. 2025). Net global welfare losses from the disruption are estimated at $5–20 billion depending on alternative-route (EU "Solidarity Lanes") throughput.

### Climate-driven agricultural disruption
- Recurrent drought and flood shocks across the Horn of Africa, the Sahel, and South Asia continue to erode harvests underlying the hotspot countries above; FEWS NET's ongoing early-warning bulletins (successor to its role after the 2011 Somalia famine) track these shocks in near-real time as a standing, not episodic, feature of the current food system.
- **The 2023-24 El Niño — a global rice-price shock triggered pre-emptively, not after a realized shortfall.** India banned non-basmati white rice exports (July 2023) and restricted basmati exports (August 2023), citing El Niño risk to domestic production and food inflation ahead of any confirmed harvest failure. The Thai benchmark price rose over 20% and stayed roughly $100/tonne above pre-ban levels; India's own rice exports fell 21% (4.2 million tonnes). Sub-Saharan African importers, the most exposed to the global rice market, absorbed the bulk of the resulting price shock (IFPRI/Food Security Portal, 2023-24; MDPI, 2024). The domestic mechanism behind the ban is developed below; the water-side manifestation of the same monsoon deficit is in `02-water.md`.

### Cost-of-living food insecurity in wealthy countries
- **United States**: 47.9 million people in 18.3 million households (13.7% of households, nearly 1 in 7) were food insecure in 2024, including nearly 1 in 5 households with children; 16.5 million people experienced severe food hardship (USDA ERS, "Household Food Security in the United States, 2024," released Dec. 2025 — the report's final edition before the administration ended the 30-year survey series). Roughly 41–42 million Americans relied on food banks or food assistance in 2025, with Feeding America network food banks reporting a demand surge tied to 2025 government-shutdown SNAP disruptions.
- **Global inflation gap**: food price inflation in high-income countries ran ~5.1% (April–May 2026) versus low-income countries, which saw inflation peak above 50% in August 2024 and remain around 25%+ from early 2023 onward — a purchasing-power erosion concentrated in the countries least able to absorb it (FAO, "State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2025," food-price-inflation brief).
- Severe food insecurity globally is estimated at 993 million people for 2025, projected to exceed 1 billion by 2028 and reach ~1.05 billion by 2030 (World Bank Food Security Update, March 2026).

### Childhood malnutrition and stunting
- 150.2 million children under five were stunted and 42.8 million were wasted globally per the 2025 Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates (UNICEF/WHO/World Bank Group, JME 2025 edition); only 28% of countries are on track to halve stunting by the 2030 target, and severe wasting affects 1.9% of children under five worldwide.

### Food deserts (access failure without absolute scarcity)
- An estimated 12.8% of the US population lived in USDA-designated low-income, low-access (LILA) census tracts in 2025, concentrated in the South — Mississippi (30%), New Mexico (29%), and Arkansas (26%) rank highest (USDA Economic Research Service, Food Access Research Atlas, 2025). This is a distributional failure structurally continuous with Sen's entitlement-failure framework: food supply is not short, but proximity and cost of access are.

### Ultra-processed food: a quality failure distinct from scarcity
- US youth derived 61.9% and adults 53.0% of daily calories from ultra-processed foods in the 2021–23 NHANES cycle (CDC/NCHS Data Brief No. 536, Aug. 2025); in some countries ultra-processed foods reach 42–60% of total dietary energy intake. Cohort studies (UK Biobank and others) link high ultra-processed consumption to materially higher BMI and obesity odds, plus elevated cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and all-cause mortality risk — a caloric-sufficiency-but-nutrient-and-health-quality failure mode with no historical precedent at this population scale, structurally analogous to but mechanistically distinct from the beriberi/pellagra monodiet failures above.

### Self-administered intake: tobacco, alcohol, and substance use

- **Tobacco kills more than 800,000 Indians a year** (Tobacco Atlas), a toll on the same order as ambient air pollution, and India carries roughly a fifth of the world's smokeless-tobacco burden — khaini, gutka and zarda, which are cheaper, less regulated and more rural than cigarettes. The annual cost of smoking-attributable illness runs to ~₹1.97 lakh crore in direct and indirect costs.
- **The category is included deliberately, and it is the one tier-1 failure that is transacted rather than imposed.** Every other failure in this tier arrives without the consent of the harmed. This one is purchased. That difference matters for what a solution can look like — the intervention history here is taxation, packaging, advertising bans and cessation support, not infrastructure — but it does *not* exempt the category from the tier's central finding: consumption sorts steeply by class and education, with bidi and smokeless use concentrated among the poorest, so the mortality lands exactly where every other tier-1 failure lands.
- **Alcohol and drug dependence extend the same logic downward into the tiers above.** The physiological harm (liver disease, injury, dependence) is tier 1; the household consequences — income diversion, domestic violence, child neglect — are tier 2 and tier 3 failures produced by a tier 1 cause. This is the clearest downward cascade in the tier, and it runs opposite to the direction the rest of the file describes.
- *Data gap*: India lacks a routinely published national estimate of alcohol-attributable mortality comparable to the tobacco figures. The 2019 National Survey on Extent and Pattern of Substance Use gives prevalence but not attributable deaths.


### India: regulatory, distributional, and infrastructural failures
- **Adulteration — FSSAI enforcement gap**: ~18% of food samples tested nationally failed safety standards in FY26 (40,023 of 223,808), with extreme state variance — Uttar Pradesh 52.8% (2024), Rajasthan 28.4%, Maharashtra 18.7%. Common adulterants: detergents in milk, lead chromate in turmeric, calcium carbide for fruit ripening. Enforcement is thin relative to failure volume: 520,566 inspections in 2025–26 yielded only 1,918 criminal convictions (FSSAI/Ministry of Health parliamentary data, 2024–26; Down to Earth, "Food Adulteration in India," 2025).
- **PDS leakage — distributional failure, unevenly improving**: an estimated 29% of FCI/state PDS grain fails to reach beneficiaries nationally, a ₹69,108 crore annual loss (ICRIER, 2022–23 data), consistent with CAG's 2013–14 estimate of ₹30,981 crore lost to diversion. Trend is not uniform: Bihar's leakage fell from 68.7% (2011–12) to 19.2% (2022–23), West Bengal from 69.4% to 9% — a partial-recovery pattern rather than ongoing blanket collapse.
- **Aadhaar-linked PDS exclusion**: a distinct tech-exclusion failure mode, separate from corruption-driven leakage. The Right to Food Campaign documented 20+ starvation deaths (2017) tied to biometric authentication failures at ration shops across 10+ states. The State of Aadhaar Report (2017–18) found 0.3–1.2 million beneficiaries/month excluded from PDS due to Aadhaar issues in just three states, with over 30% of authentication failures resulting in zero rations received.
- **Tribal (Adivasi) habitat loss and land acquisition**: development-induced displacement via dams, mines, and industrial projects — under the legacy of the 1894 Land Acquisition Act — has displaced tens of millions since independence, per range estimates (a precise national figure is not well established). 47% of the rural tribal population lives below the poverty line vs. 28% nationally. In Attapadi, Kerala, Adivasi hamlets lost 3,000+ acres over three generations to settlers, directly eroding traditional food and nutrition sources (The Conversation/phys.org, Oct. 2025; IDR, "Adivasi livelihoods in Central India").
- **Child and maternal anemia — a live quality/deficiency failure (NFHS-5)**: child anemia (ages 6–59 months) rose from 58.6% to 67.1% between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5; women's anemia rose from 50.1% (2012) to 53.7% (2023) — worsening even as stunting (35.5% of under-5s) slowly improves. This tracks with the historical beriberi/pellagra pattern of deficiency disease persisting alongside caloric sufficiency.
- **Soil health and climate-driven yield risk**: the March 2022 heat wave cut wheat yields 10–15%, dropping the national production forecast from 110 to 99 million metric tons (USDA FAS). India lost 33.9 million hectares to floods and 35 million hectares to drought over 2015–21. Decades of urea over-subsidy (>80% subsidy rate vs. other fertilizers) skewed the NPK application ratio to 7.7:3.1:1 against an ideal of 4:2:1; roughly 50% of Indian soils are now zinc-deficient, linked to a 30% maize yield reduction in Madhya Pradesh (Nature Scientific Reports), and nitrate groundwater contamination exceeds WHO limits in many regions.
- **Urea access failure**: subsidized urea is routinely diverted to industrial uses (UF resin, plywood glue), producing real farmer-facing shortages, distress sales, and black-market pricing. The government's own enforcement response confirms this is a substantive, not fringe, problem: 370+ surprise inspections and 30 FIRs filed for urea diversion in 2025. Urea imports nearly doubled (24.76 to 58.62 lakh tonnes, Apr–Oct year-over-year) to cover domestic supply gaps.
- **Grain storage concentration risk — anti-monopoly safeguard removed**: FCI's "Hub and Spoke" silo-modernization tender originally capped how much storage capacity any single company could win — a standard safeguard against concentration risk in strategic food infrastructure (a single operator's financial distress, contract dispute, or renegotiating leverage becomes a supply-chain single point of failure). In 2022, following NITI Aayog and Department of Economic Affairs opposition, the cap was dropped. Across both tender phases, two firms (Adani Agri Logistics and Leap India Food & Logistics) won 110 of 134 silo contracts, worth over ₹16,500 crore combined. FCI disputes any favoritism, noting the winning firms' Phase II share fell and calling the removal a collective decision "in view of the sector's growth potential." The underlying risk — concentrated control over strategic grain storage — is real regardless of the contested causation (Newslaundry, May 2026; IANS/FCI rebuttal, June 2026; SabrangIndia; FACTLY).
- **State-level fragility masked by national self-sufficiency**: India's aggregate grain surplus does not guarantee state-level access. Punjab, Haryana, UP (wheat), and Telangana/Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal, Bihar (rice) are structural surplus producers; Kerala is a total deficit state, and Northeastern/hill states and union territories mostly produce below 10 lakh tonnes, structurally dependent on inter-state and central grain movement. Center-State allocation disputes are real and ongoing: Punjab's food minister accused the Centre of delaying NFSA pulse supplies (one cluster received 900 MT against a 2,189 MT allocation), and a Punjab & Haryana High Court case alleges an unauthorized 10% cut in wheat allocation. **Manipur, 2025–26 (live case)**: landslides cut the Imphal–Silchar highway and an ongoing blockade closed the Imphal–Dimapur route; freight operators abandoned the routes, and traders reported climbing prices and looming shortages — a real-time example of a logistically isolated state facing acute food-access failure while India overall runs a national grain surplus (EastMojo, July 2026; NENow).
- **Ethanol: the buffer stock and the feed crop routed into fuel.** This is a water entry primarily (`02-water.md`, §India) but it lands on food through two channels, and both run through instruments this section already describes. **Grain.** FCI has opened roughly **12.7 million tonnes of rice to distilleries** — 5.5 Mt of 100% broken rice via e-auction plus 7.2 Mt from buffer stocks — after an additional 2.8 Mt sanction took the 2024–25 allocation to 5.2 Mt; through June 2026, **3.9 Mt of FCI rice and 6.8 Mt of maize** had been consumed for ethanol. The buffer stock is the instrument that answers the distributional failure described in the PDS entries above, and a share of it is now committed to a fuel target. **Feed.** With ~7 Mt of maize diverted in 2024–25 — ethanol from maize reached 2.86 billion litres in ESY 2023–24, ~42% of total ethanol — **India became a net maize importer in 2024**, exports falling from ~1.9 Mt to ~0.5 Mt, and maize prices moved from roughly **₹15,000 to ₹25,000 per tonne**. The poultry and cattle-feed industry consumes 60–70% of Indian maize and absorbed that shift; demand is projected to rise from ~50 Mt (2025–26) to ~72 Mt by 2030–31 (FICCI–YES Bank).
- The mechanism is not "fuel versus food" as a moral trade-off, which is how it is usually argued. It is narrower and more checkable: **a demand created by an energy-policy target is being met out of the two stocks that exist to absorb food shocks** — the buffer, and the feed supply that converts to protein. Neither the FCI allocation SOP nor the E20/E27 schedule carries a food-security or water condition. Note also that the diverted crops are rice and maize specifically, so the diversion runs *with* the MSP-driven cereal bias described in the next entry rather than against it: ethanol gives paddy and maize a second guaranteed buyer at the exact moment the stated nutrition policy is trying to move land toward pulses and millets. Two arms of policy are paying for opposite cropping outcomes.
- **Nutrient-density loss from cereal-over-pulse/millet cropping shift**: policy-driven crop substitution, not breeding itself, is the well-documented mechanism. MSP (minimum support price) and procurement policy have favored rice/wheat expansion since the Green Revolution, at the expense of pulses and millets — millets area fell from ~36–40 million hectares (1960s) to ~14–15 million hectares in recent years, displaced from semi-arid tracts by rice/wheat. Millets carry roughly 3–5x the iron and 2–3x the calcium of polished rice per 100g (ICAR/NIN figures). The government's own 2023 International Year of Millets push and "Shree Anna" initiative cite this exact nutrient gap and cropping-shift history as rationale — an official, acknowledged policy narrative, not speculation. A separate, additive mechanism is rising atmospheric CO2: Myers et al. (2014, *Nature*) found elevated CO2 reduces zinc ~9–10%, iron ~5–8%, and protein ~6–8% in wheat and rice grain, with India specifically named as high-risk given its rice/wheat dietary dependence. Neither mechanism has been directly shown to cause a specific share of the NFHS-5 anemia rise documented above — the link is plausible and consistent, not a demonstrated causal chain.
- **Compounding demand-side fragility (directional, not fully proven)**: urban India shows a fast-growing shift from home-stored wheat-grain-plus-chakki-milling toward branded packaged atta (market growing 12–15% CAGR, ₹95.1bn in 2025 to a projected ₹286.4bn by 2034), driven by hygiene, shelf-life, and adulteration concerns — packaged atta spoils faster and is bought in smaller, more frequent quantities than stored whole grain (IMARC, India Packaged Atta Market Report). This is still a minority, urban-concentrated pattern — 75–80% of Indians still buy loose atta or mill locally — and no direct survey confirms a national decline in household stock-holding (a 2020 COVID-lockdown study found tribal households held a median 240kg, ~5 months' supply). But directionally, less household-level buffering combined with concentrated upstream storage means a supply shock has fewer absorption layers before it reaches consumer prices — the same entitlement-failure mechanism Sen identified in Bengal 1943 and Bangladesh 1974 (above): food need not disappear for access to collapse, only its price relative to income.

### India: El Niño/ENSO-driven crop and price shocks

- **The mechanism is distinct from water's.** El Niño weakens or delays the southwest monsoon, cutting rainfed kharif sowing area and shifting sowing dates. Pulses, oilseeds, and coarse cereals take the largest hit; rice and wheat are comparatively buffered by irrigation. See `02-water.md` for the same monsoon deficit's reservoir/groundwater consequences (Chennai 2019, Bengaluru 2023-24) — this section covers what it does to acreage, yield mix, and price instead. The Indian Ocean Dipole is a real confounder, not a footnote: a positive IOD offset El Niño's drying effect in both 1997 and 2015, both strong El Niño years with above-average rice yield, so "El Niño year = bad monsoon" does not hold as a rule (Agronomy, 2021; "Monsoon under Siege," 2024).
- **2002 is the clean case.** A confirmed strong El Niño year: 19-21% rainfall deficit, foodgrain output down 18% (38 million tonnes), agri-GDP down roughly 7% (~$8 billion loss) (ICRIER Working Paper 276).
- **2009 is a genuine, unresolved disagreement, written as one rather than smoothed over.** One line of research describes 2009 as a moderate El Niño year in which above-average kharif rice yield was still achieved. 2009 is also separately and widely documented as one of India's worst droughts, with a rainfall deficit near 23%. No source found reconciles the two. The likely explanation is a metric mismatch — irrigated rice yield-per-hectare holding up while total foodgrain output and rainfed crops collapsed — but this is not demonstrated in any source consulted, and the disagreement is left standing rather than resolved by assumption.
- **2018-19 is the sharpest rainfed-crop-specific case.** Pulses acreage fell 43% year-over-year (arhar, moong, urad) and oilseed acreage fell roughly 40% in early kharif sowing — directly undercutting the pulses/millet self-sufficiency push described in the nutrient-density entry above.
- **The instrument gap is narrower than the tier's usual "absence of measurement" finding, and more checkable.** This is not a case of no forecast existing: IMD runs a live ENSO-IOD bulletin, and the government maintains District Agriculture Contingency Plans for 150-200 high-risk districts, prescribing crop substitution (drought-tolerant pulses/millets over paddy) ahead of El Niño-risk seasons — active again for 2026. What is missing is narrower: no evidence that buffer-stock release or import contracting is triggered by the months-ahead ENSO forecast itself. The 2023-24 rice-export ban above shows the state *can* act pre-emptively on an ENSO signal; the buffer-stock and import side of the same policy stays reactive to realized shortfall rather than anticipatory, unlike the forecast-to-response pipeline this file already credits FEWS NET with building for famine risk (above, International and humanitarian systems).
- See the commercial-landscape section below for the ENSO-linked crop insurance layer and who actually pays for it.

### India: ingested toxicants — lead in the spice supply

- **Lead chromate adulteration of turmeric is a food-borne lead exposure route of national scale.** The pigment — an industrial yellow used in paint, rubber and plastics — began being added to turmeric in the 1980s after flood-damaged rhizomes came to market dull, and consumer preference for bright yellow made brightness worth faking. Samples from Patna have measured lead above **1,000 µg/g**, more than 200 times the permitted limit. This is not a hygiene failure or a spoilage failure; it is a cosmetic-quality failure with a neurotoxin as the instrument.
- It belongs in this file because it enters through food, but the harm is neurological rather than nutritional, and the exposed population is the entire consuming public rather than the food-insecure minority. See the toxic-exposure class in `00-summary.md` — lead, asbestos and silica share one mechanism, and only one of them has a home in a single file.

### India: who is already commercially working on this, and who pays them

Required by the commercial-landscape pass. A failure is not neglected because the state is failing at it — establish the private layer before calling anything a frontier.

- **The best-capitalised category in Indian food is the failure this file names.** Ultra-processed and snacking D2C is where the money went: **Rebel Foods (~$773M valuation), Licious (~$489M), Epigamia ($67.3M raised), Farmley (₹394 cr FY25 revenue, +71% YoY, still loss-making at ₹22.6 cr), Open Secret (~₹226 cr valuation), Slurrp Farm (₹73.2 cr FY24 revenue, $18.1M raised)**, against a "healthy snacking" market sized at **$3.13B (2025) rising to $4.77B (2034)**. The customer is the urban discretionary buyer. The commercial system has re-specified *nutrition* as an urban premiumisation problem rather than a deficiency-disease one — and the population carrying the NFHS-5 anaemia and stunting burden is not in the addressable market.
- **The adulteration hypothesis does not survive contact with the data, and the correction matters.** The intuitive fix — better brands, in-house quality control — assumes brands are the solvers. In the largest recent case they were the subject: **MDH and Everest faced ethylene-oxide recalls in Hong Kong, Singapore and the UK in 2024**, and both were inspected under FSSAI's own nationwide spice-enforcement drive. What exists commercially is *testing infrastructure* — NABL-accredited labs (Eurofins, SGS, TÜV, Envirocare) selling to businesses — and no venture-funded consumer-facing adulteration-detection company at all. Spectroscopic and traceability approaches (FTIR/NIR, ~93% detection accuracy) remain at academic-pilot stage. **The buyer of purity assurance is a brand protecting itself, not a household protecting itself**, which is why the instrument exists and the exposure continues.
- **Lead chromate in turmeric has no commercial actor whatsoever.** No detection product, no remediation service, no brand built against it. The harmed party is every consumer of turmeric and none of them knows, so there is no demand signal to serve — the purest instance in this file of an exposure with no wallet attached.
- **Farmer-facing agritech is a graveyard, and the cause is specific.** Sector funding fell from **$802M (2022) to $178M (2023) to roughly $182M in 2025**. Named shutdowns include **BharatAgri (2025), Greenikk (2024), Fraazo, Otipy, Deep Rooted and ReshaMandi**, with a consistent stated cause: customer-acquisition cost against low willingness-to-pay and weak repeat rates. This is the strongest available evidence for the *payer-identity* hypothesis — the farmer is the beneficiary and a poor customer simultaneously, and a decade of capital did not resolve that.
- **Where the state is the customer, a stable industry exists and the household is not served by it.** PDS ePOS terminals reached **99.8% of 5.51 lakh fair-price shops** under government tender; fortification runs through Anaemia Mukt Bharat and mandated fortified rice, supplied by premix manufacturers and Fortify Health. These are functioning businesses whose buyer is a ministry. The failure documented above — exclusion errors, and a buffer stock now partly committed to fuel — is not something a supplier to that buyer has any incentive to surface.
- **Ethanol shows a mandate manufacturing a payer.** **Praj Industries has built roughly 65% of India's ethanol plants**, with EID Parry and Dalmia Bharat Sugar moving into maize feedstock, on the back of a **guaranteed ₹66.17/litre procurement price for grain ethanol**. That is not a market price; it is a state-created revenue floor. Capital arrived promptly and at scale for the node this file documents as a *harm* — which is the clearest demonstration available that capital follows the mandate rather than the harm.
- **The counter-example is a cooperative, not a startup.** **Amul/GCMMF** returns roughly **80% of the consumer rupee to producers** (against 35–40% in Western dairy models), procures above private dairies, and covers on the order of **80–100 million farmer families** — genuinely serving a bottom-quintile producer *and* a price-sensitive consumer without ongoing state offtake. It is the only entry here that reaches the harmed population at scale, and its ownership structure rather than its technology is what makes that possible. Worth holding against the assumption that "cannot pay" is settled: Amul suggests the binding constraint is often who captures the margin, not whether one exists.
- **Density of entry is not durability.** Millet and healthy-snack D2C shows dozens of entrants and continued funding but few profitable ones — Farmley loses money at ₹394 cr of revenue. Agritech shows the inverse: dense entry in 2021–22, near-total capital withdrawal by 2025. Both are recorded above as activity; only one of them is a business.
- **ENSO-linked crop risk has an insurance instrument, and it is state-subsidized, not farmer-paid.** PMFBY (multi-peril, one of the world's largest crop insurance programs by enrolment) and RWBCIS (parametric weather-index insurance) are the main instruments covering El Niño-linked yield risk, with government-subsidized premium. Private parametric products are emerging through agri-fintech distribution (DeHaat, Ninjacart, Gramophone, AgriBazaar), but target export-grade horticulture in Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, and Punjab — not the smallholder rainfed pulses and oilseed growers who actually lost acreage in 2018-19 above. Same pattern as the ethanol entry: the mandate manufactures the payer, and it is not the exposed farmer.
