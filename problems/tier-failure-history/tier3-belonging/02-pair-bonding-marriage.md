# Pair-Bonding / Marriage — Bond Maintenance (Tier 3 — Belonging)

> Maintenance of the formed bond, as distinct from forming it.

Tier definition: bonding as a precondition for this species' reproductive/care strategy.

## How the need has been threatened

**1. Legal denial of marriage to enslaved people, US slave codes from 1662.** Virginia's 1662 statute and later colony- and state-level slave codes withheld legal marriage from enslaved people entirely; unions were informal ("jumping the broom") and had zero legal standing, so spouses and children could be sold apart with no recognized bond to protect. After emancipation, the Freedmen's Bureau (1865–66) processed tens of thousands of retroactive marriage registrations because so many couples had no legal record of decades-long unions (Tera Hunter, *Bound in Wedlock*, 2017).

**2. Anti-miscegenation law, US 1691–1967.** Virginia's 1691 act banning interracial marriage began a legal tradition that spread to most US states; by the mid-20th century 16 states still criminalized interracial marriage, some with prison terms. Richard and Mildred Loving were arrested in their Virginia bedroom in 1958 for marrying across the color line (Peter Wallenstein, *Tell the Court I Love My Wife*, 2002).

**3. Apartheid-era migrant labor system, South Africa 1910s–1990.** Mine labor policy confined Black male workers to single-sex compounds under pass laws and the 1913 Natives Land Act's homeland system, barring wives and children from residing near workplaces. Migrant husbands saw spouses for weeks a year across careers of decades, producing generations of de facto separated marriages (Francis Wilson, *Labour in the South African Gold Mines 1911–1969*, 1972).

**4. Indentured servitude and separated spouses, British Empire 1834–1917.** Post-abolition indenture contracts shipped roughly 1.3 million Indian laborers to Caribbean, Mauritian, and Fijian plantations under terms that made joint spousal emigration difficult; colonial recruitment skewed heavily male (often 3:1 or worse), leaving married women in India separated from husbands for the 5–10 year contract term or permanently (Hugh Tinker, *A New System of Slavery*, 1974).

**5. WWI war widowhood, France 1914–1918.** France suffered roughly 1.4 million military deaths, producing an estimated 600,000 widows, many in their twenties, with no state precedent for supporting mass spousal loss at this scale (Jay Winter, *The Great War and the British People*, 1985; INSEE demographic retrospectives).

**6. Nazi Lebensborn program, Germany 1935–1945.** The SS-run Lebensborn network encouraged extramarital pregnancies among women deemed racially valuable and, in occupied Norway and Poland, facilitated relationships between German soldiers and local women outside marriage bonds while separating existing families through deportation and forced labor — state engineering of reproduction that bypassed and undermined marital bonding entirely (Georg Lilienthal, *Der "Lebensborn e.V."*, 1985).

**7. Khmer Rouge forced marriages, Cambodia 1975–1979.** The Khmer Rouge organized mass wedding ceremonies assigning spouses to each other with no consent, sometimes hundreds of couples at once, to sever pre-revolutionary family loyalty and subordinate the family unit to the collective (Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia, Case 002/02 judgment, 2018, which found forced marriage a crime against humanity).

**8. Great Depression divorce and desertion, US 1930–1933.** Formal divorce rates dipped as filing fees became unaffordable, but informal marital dissolution — desertion, men "riding the rails" to find work and never returning — surged; contemporary relief agencies reported spousal desertion as a leading cause of families needing aid (Glen Elder, *Children of the Great Depression*, 1974).

**9. Guest-worker programs separating spouses, West Germany 1955–1973.** The Gastarbeiter recruitment agreements (with Italy 1955, Turkey 1961, Yugoslavia 1968) brought over 2.6 million workers, overwhelmingly on single-status contracts with no family accompaniment rights for years, leaving spouses in home countries for extended periods before family reunification policy loosened in the 1970s (Ulrich Herbert, *A History of Foreign Labor in Germany*, 1990).

**10. Same-sex marriage denial, global, until 2001–present.** Until the Netherlands became the first country to legalize same-sex marriage in 2001, no jurisdiction anywhere recognized same-sex pair-bonds as legal marriage; as of 2024 same-sex marriage remains illegal or unrecognized in over 120 countries, and in some (parts of the Middle East, sub-Saharan Africa) same-sex relationships are separately criminalized, foreclosing legal bond-maintenance entirely.

**11. Post-Soviet marriage collapse, Russia/Eastern Europe 1991–1999.** The USSR's dissolution triggered economic shock that collapsed marriage rates and spiked divorce; Russia's marriage rate fell alongside a divorce rate that reached roughly 4.5 per 1,000 population by the late 1990s, among the highest in the world, driven by the loss of Soviet-era housing allocation and job security that had underwritten marital formation (UN Demographic Yearbook data, 1990s series).

**12. Forced separation through migrant domestic and labor programs, Gulf states 1970s–present.** Sponsorship (kafala) systems across Gulf Cooperation Council states typically bar low-wage migrant workers — millions of South and Southeast Asian laborers — from bringing spouses, producing years-long involuntary separation as a structural feature of the labor contract (Human Rights Watch, *Slow Reform: Protection of Migrant Domestic Workers*, 2010).

**13. Chinese hukou-driven "left-behind" spouses, China 1990s–present.** Internal migration under the hukou household registration system, which restricts urban social benefits to registered residents, has driven an estimated 280 million rural migrant workers to cities without their families; spouses often remain apart for months or years at a stretch (All-China Women's Federation surveys, 2013).

**14. WWII wartime separation and war widowhood, global 1939–1945.** Beyond direct combat deaths (an estimated war widow population running into the millions across the USSR, Germany, Japan, and elsewhere), tens of millions of marriages were interrupted for years by conscription, internment (Japanese American incarceration, 1942–46, split families across camps), and occupation, with divorce rates spiking in the US and UK immediately postwar as reunited couples found the bond unsustainable (Andrew Cherlin, *Marriage, Divorce, Remarriage*, 1992).

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **Marriage as a legal institution with property/inheritance protection.** From Roman law's *matrimonium* through English common law and the Napoleonic Code (1804), states codified marriage to confer inheritance rights, property regimes (dower, dowry, community property), and legitimacy of offspring — converting a social bond into an enforceable legal claim.
- **Loving v. Virginia, 1967.** The US Supreme Court struck down all remaining anti-miscegenation statutes, ruling unanimously that marriage is a fundamental right that cannot be restricted by race, ending the last explicit racial bar to marriage formation in the US.
- **Obergefell v. Hodges, 2015, and global same-sex marriage expansion.** The US Supreme Court legalized same-sex marriage nationwide in 2015; by 2024, over 35 countries had followed the Netherlands' 2001 lead (Belgium 2003, Canada 2005, Argentina 2010, France 2013, Ireland by referendum 2015, Taiwan 2019 as the first in Asia), progressively closing the legal-recognition gap.
- **UN Convention on Consent to Marriage, Minimum Age, and Registration, 1962.** This UN convention obligated ratifying states to require free consent of both parties and formal registration, directly targeting forced-marriage practices like those later prosecuted at the ECCC against the Khmer Rouge.
- **Migrant labor family reunification policy.** West Germany's 1973 Anwerbestopp shifted Gastarbeiter policy toward family reunification rather than rotation, and the EU's Family Reunification Directive (2003) formalized a right for legal migrants to bring spouses — a direct institutional response to decades of guest-worker spousal separation.
- **War widow pension systems.** France's 1919 pension law for war widows, expanded across combatant nations after both world wars, and the US's Dependency and Indemnity Compensation program, institutionalized state support for spouses whose bond was severed by war rather than leaving mass widowhood as unmanaged private loss.
- **No-fault divorce law.** California's Family Law Act of 1969, the first no-fault divorce statute, spread across nearly all US states and much of the world (England and Wales, 2020) by the 2020s, converting marriage dissolution from an adversarial fault-finding process into an administrative exit — a pressure-release valve against bonds sustained only by legal difficulty of leaving.
- **Marriage counseling and family therapy as a field.** The founding of the American Association of Marriage Counselors in 1942 and the growth of family systems therapy from the 1950s (Nathan Ackerman, Salvador Minuchin) professionalized bond-repair as an alternative to unmanaged dissolution.
- **Freedmen's Bureau marriage registration, 1865–1866.** As a direct remedy to slavery's legal marriage denial, the Bureau formally registered tens of thousands of existing enslaved unions, retroactively converting informal bonds into legally recognized marriages with inheritance and custody standing.
- **Kafala reform pressure and bilateral labor agreements.** Beginning with Qatar's 2020 labor reforms and Philippine and Indonesian bilateral agreements mandating minimum contract terms, sending and receiving states have begun negotiating family-visit and reunification provisions into migrant labor contracts, though coverage remains partial.

## Where this fails today

**Declining marriage formation, global, 2022–2026.** China's Ministry of Civil Affairs recorded 6.83 million marriage registrations in 2022, the lowest since records began in 1986, a rebound to 7.68 million in 2023, then a fresh drop to roughly 6.1 million in 2024 — a near-halving from the 2013 peak of 13.5 million. South Korea's marriage rate fell to about 3.7 per 1,000 population in 2023, among the lowest recorded anywhere. In the US, the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics provisional data put the 2022 marriage rate at 6.2 per 1,000, roughly half the 1970 rate, while the divorce rate held near 2.4 per 1,000 — meaning the institution is both less-entered and, proportionally, no more stable.

**Forced marriage, global, 2022–present.** The ILO, Walk Free Foundation, and IOM's *Global Estimates of Modern Slavery* (2022) found 22 million people living in forced marriage on any given day, up from 15.4 million in the 2017 estimate — a rise, not a decline, over five years; two-thirds of those in forced marriage are women and girls, and most cases occur within family structures rather than by traffickers. UNICEF's 2023 update estimates 12 million girls are still married before age 18 every year worldwide, with roughly 1 in 5 young women globally married as children; conflict-affected regions (Yemen, Afghanistan under Taliban rule, parts of the Sahel) show child-marriage rates rising, not falling, as families use marriage as an economic or protective response to displacement and poverty.

**Migrant labor separating spouses, Gulf states and Philippines, ongoing.** The kafala sponsorship system across Gulf Cooperation Council states still structurally bars most low-wage migrant workers from family accompaniment; Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International reporting through 2022–2023 found reforms in Qatar and elsewhere unevenly enforced, leaving millions of South and Southeast Asian workers separated from spouses for the duration of multi-year contracts. The Philippine Statistics Authority's 2022 Survey on Overseas Filipinos counted roughly 1.77 million Overseas Filipino Workers, a large share married with spouses and children remaining in the Philippines — a labor-export model built on sustained spousal separation as a national economic strategy.

**Same-sex marriage still illegal or criminalized, global, 2024–2026.** ILGA World's 2023 report found consensual same-sex relations still criminalized in 64 countries, with legal marriage recognized in roughly 35–38 countries as of 2025 and unrecognized in over 150 — meaning same-sex couples in the large majority of the world's jurisdictions still have no legal bond-maintenance mechanism at all, and in a subset (parts of the Middle East, several African states, and Brunei's 2019 sharia penal code) face criminal penalty for the relationship itself.

**War-driven spousal separation, Ukraine and Gaza, 2022–2026.** Ukraine's martial law, in force since February 2022, bars most men aged 18–60 from leaving the country, separating hundreds of thousands of military-age husbands from spouses who fled abroad as refugees — UNHCR recorded over 6.3 million Ukrainian refugees as of 2024, disproportionately women and children, split from male partners bound by conscription law. Israel's 2023–2024 military campaign in Gaza and associated displacement of over 1.9 million Gazans (UNOCHA, 2024) has similarly fractured spousal cohabitation through death, detention, and forced movement, with no functioning family-reunification mechanism operating inside the active conflict zone.
