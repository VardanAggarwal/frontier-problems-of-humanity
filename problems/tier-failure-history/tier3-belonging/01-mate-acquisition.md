# Mate Acquisition / Courtship (Tier 3 — Belonging)

> The ability to find and form a reproductive/intimate partnership at all.

Distinct from bond *maintenance*: this is the competing-for phase, where population-level mate-market structure (sex ratios, endogamy rules, price systems) determines whether an individual can find a mate at all. Offspring altriciality forced pair-bonding into the human reproductive strategy; when the market that produces those bonds is distorted, the failure shows up as bachelorhood, coerced marriage, or violence between competitors. This topic is thinner-documented than food or violence — much of the evidence is demographic (sex-ratio data) rather than narrative history, so some entries below lean on statistics more than events.

## How the need has been threatened

- **1848-1860 — California Gold Rush bachelor society.** 1850 census recorded a sex ratio of roughly 12 men per woman in California; ~115,000 male "Argonauts" arrived pre-1850 versus ~3,500 women. Men filled domestic roles (cooking, laundering) normally allocated by pair-bonding; the ratio fell to 7.2:1 by 1852 and 2.4:1 by 1860 as migration normalized (Aguilar-Gomez, "Norms Formation: The California Gold Rush and Women's Roles"; FoundSF, "Men : Women in Early San Francisco").
- **1914-1918 — WWI male-deficit Britain and France.** Britain's adult (15-50) sex ratio fell from 98 men per 100 women at war's onset to 88 by 1918; ~700,000-900,000 British servicemen died, concentrated in the prime marriageable cohort. The 1921 UK census recorded 1.72 million more women than men — the press-coined "Surplus Two Million" — with 1,209 single women aged 25-29 per 1,000 men in that bracket; by 1931 half of that cohort remained unmarried. France lost ~11% of its population to death or wounding (ww1centenary.oucs.ox.ac.uk, "Surplus Women: a legacy of World War One?").
- **1939-1945 — WWII compounded the same deficit** across the USSR, Germany, and Japan on a larger scale; postwar Soviet censuses show tens of millions more women than men in the war-affected cohorts, a demographic scar still visible decades later in Russian sex-ratio-by-age data.
- **1961-present — India: dowry as institutionalized courtship, and dowry death as its pathology.** The Dowry Prohibition Act (assented 20 May 1961, effective 1 July 1961) criminalized dowry exchange but was widely judged ineffective; it was amended in 1985 after failing to curb the practice. As of recent NCRB data, ~6,450 dowry deaths were recorded in 2022, and India sees an estimated 20 women die daily from dowry-related harassment, murder, or induced suicide — a direct pathology of a mate-market where the bride's family bears an escalating price to secure a match (indiacode.nic.in; IMPRI, "Dowry Prohibition Act 1961 Completes 60 Years").
- **1979-2015 — China's one-child policy and sex-selective abortion: "bare branches" (guanggun).** Son preference plus the one-child policy drove widespread sex-selective abortion from the 1980s onward; China's sex ratio reached 106.3 men per 100 women overall and 117 boys per 100 girls under 15. The policy generated an estimated surplus of 32.5 million men aged 5-39; the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences projected 24 million men unable to find wives by 2020. A 2010 Xi'an Jiaotong University survey of 256 villages across 20 provinces found average village sex ratios of 122, with ~9 unmarried older men per village — rural "bare branch" bachelors disproportionately low-status, since better-off men out-compete them in the thinned market (theconversation.com; ifstudies.org, "China's One-Child Policy: Effects on the Sex Ratio and Crime").
- **1991-2011 — India's child sex ratio decline as a parallel, less state-driven version.** Child sex ratio (age 0-6) fell from 927 girls per 1,000 boys (2001) to 919 (2011), an unbroken decline since 1961, hitting a low of 914 nationally; Haryana recorded 834, among the lowest in the country. Same mechanism as China (son preference + prenatal sex determination technology) without a one-child policy forcing it (pib.gov.in; ijme.in, "India's child sex ratio: worsening imbalance").
- **17th-20th century — legal endogamy restricting mate choice: anti-miscegenation laws in the US.** Statutes banning interracial marriage existed across most US states at some point; by 1967, 16 states (concentrated in the former Confederacy plus Texas, Oklahoma, West Virginia, Delaware, Kentucky, Missouri) still enforced bans, while 14 states had repealed theirs in the preceding 15 years (Arizona, California, Colorado, Idaho, Indiana, Maryland, Montana, Nebraska, Nevada, North Dakota, Oregon, South Dakota, Utah, Wyoming). These laws mechanically shrank the eligible-mate pool along racial lines regardless of individual preference (getlegal.com; constitutioncenter.org).
- **Historical, cross-cultural — bride-price systems and their inflation crises.** In many bride-price societies (parts of sub-Saharan Africa, historical China, parts of Central Asia), the price a groom's family must pay functions as the market-clearing mechanism for mate acquisition; anthropological literature on bride-price inflation (e.g., cattle-price escalation among pastoralist groups) documents episodes where rising prices delayed male marriage age by years and pushed poorer men out of the marriage market entirely — a mirror-image pathology to dowry inflation.
- **Colonial and frontier settlements generally — imported gender imbalance.** Beyond the California case, colonial Australia (convict transportation, 1788-1868) and various 19th-century frontier and mining territories (Klondike, Australian goldfields) show the same pattern: male-dominated migration streams produce local mate-market scarcity, documented via colonial census records showing male:female ratios often exceeding 3:1 in frontier decades before family migration caught up.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **Professional matchmaking, pre-modern to modern.** Marriage brokers (Chinese *meiren*, South Asian family-network matchmakers, Jewish *shadchan*) formalized mate search as a service industry, substituting institutional search for individual competition and reducing the violence/uncertainty of open courtship contests.
- **Arranged marriage as market-clearing institution.** Family-negotiated marriage, historically dominant across South Asia, East Asia, and much of the pre-modern world, replaced individual mate competition with household-level negotiation — reducing (though not eliminating) the coordination failure of thin or skewed local mate markets, at the cost of individual choice.
- **1961, amended 1985 — India's Dowry Prohibition Act** and subsequent Section 304B (dowry death) and Section 498A (cruelty) additions to the Indian Penal Code represent direct legal intervention into a pathological mate-price system, though enforcement gaps persist.
- **1918-1919 — UK Oversea Emigration Committee and Society for the Oversea Settlement of British Women.** Direct state response to the WWI "surplus women" crisis: government-funded emigration programs sent British women to colonies (Canada, Australia) with more balanced or male-skewed sex ratios, treating the mate-market imbalance as solvable via geographic redistribution.
- **1967 — Loving v. Virginia.** US Supreme Court struck down anti-miscegenation statutes in the remaining 16 states, eliminating the legal endogamy floor and expanding the eligible-mate pool across race nationwide; followed a 15-year wave of 14 state-level repeals that preceded it.
- **1979-2015 → ongoing — China's policy reversal and sex-ratio interventions.** The one-child policy's phase-out (two-child policy 2015, three-child 2021) plus provincial bans on prenatal sex-determination test disclosure (in force since the 1990s, unevenly enforced) directly target the mechanism generating the bare-branches surplus; "Care for Girls" campaigns (from 2000s) offered subsidies to families with daughters to counter son preference.
- **Sex ratio-driven internal migration as an informal correction.** China's guanggun population disproportionately migrates to cities for work, partly a self-organized response to thinned rural mate markets — an unplanned but real adaptation.
- **Courtship ritual formalization, pre-modern to modern.** Chaperonage systems, formal engagement periods, and codified courtship stages (from Victorian calling-card etiquette to modern dating "stages") reduced the ambiguity and violence risk of unregulated mate competition by giving it agreed rules.
- **Late 20th-21st century — online dating platforms as structural fix for thin local markets.** Match.com (1995) onward, then algorithmic apps (Tinder 2012, Bumble 2014), scaled the search pool far beyond geographic or social-network limits — a direct technological answer to the same problem gold-rush bachelors and post-war "surplus women" faced: too few eligible mates within physical reach. Rural and demographically skewed populations (including parts of rural China) are documented adopters specifically because local pools are thin.
- **Bride-price and dowry regulation elsewhere.** Several African nations (e.g., statutory caps or registration requirements on bride price in parts of Kenya, Uganda, South Sudan under customary-law reform efforts) attempted price ceilings analogous to India's dowry law, aiming to prevent price inflation from excluding poorer men from the marriage market.

## Where this fails today

- **2020s — global decline in marriage and partnership formation among young adults.** US marriage rates fell to a record low of 6.0 per 1,000 population in 2022 (Bowling Green State University National Center for Family & Marriage Research, using CDC/NCHS provisional data), continuing a decades-long slide; the median age at first marriage reached 30.2 for men and 28.6 for women in 2023 (US Census Bureau), the oldest on record. Pew Research (2023) found 44% of US adults ages 25-54 without a spouse or partner report not being interested in a relationship or dating.
- **China and India — bare-branch bachelor surplus persists.** China's 2020 census still showed ~34.9 million more men than women nationally, and demographers (e.g., Wei Chen, Nicholas Eberstadt) project 30-35 million Chinese men will be unable to marry by 2030 given the cohort already born. India's Sample Registration System data (released 2021-2023) shows sex ratio at birth still skewed male in several northern states (Haryana, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh), with Haryana around 917-920 girls per 1,000 boys as of 2022-23 reporting — the "bare branches" mechanism from the 1980s-2010s has not resolved, only slowed.
- **2022-2024 — South Korea and Japan's marriage-birth collapse tied to mate-market dysfunction.** South Korea's total fertility rate hit 0.72 in 2023 (Statistics Korea) and 0.75 in 2024, the world's lowest recorded rate; marriages fell to roughly 193,000 in 2023, less than half the 2012 figure. Surveys (Korean Institute for Health and Social Affairs, 2022-2023) cite housing costs, job insecurity, and a widely reported gender-conflict backlash (the "4B movement" — bihon, bichulsan, biyeonae, bisekseu: no marriage, no childbirth, no dating, no sex) as drivers among young Korean women disengaging from the marriage market entirely. Japan's births fell below 730,000 in 2023 (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare), with the National Institute of Population and Social Security Research's 2021 survey finding record shares of never-married adults reporting no interest in marriage.
- **2019-2024 — dating-app fatigue and algorithm-driven dissatisfaction.** Match Group (parent of Tinder, Hinge, Match.com) reported declining paying-user counts through 2023-2024, with Tinder's paying subscribers falling for multiple consecutive quarters; Pew Research (2023) found 46% of US online-dating users described their overall experience as negative, and a Stanford/Michigan academic literature review (Bumble-funded and independent studies, 2022-2023) documented "swipe fatigue," decision paralysis from oversized choice sets, and algorithmic sorting by attractiveness/response-rate proxies that concentrates matches on a thin top tier of users — a digitally reinstated version of the same skewed-market problem, now sorted by app design rather than geography.
- **2023 — Pew Research documents rising involuntary singlehood.** Pew's "The Modern American Family" and related 2023 releases found single US adults ages 18-49 who are looking increasingly report bad experiences: 75% say dating has gotten harder in the last ten years; roughly half of single adults not looking for a relationship cite "just not interested," but a substantial share of those looking report frustration finding compatible partners, and loneliness researchers (Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness) tie rising social disconnection partly to declining partnership rates.
- **2020s — housing costs as an economic barrier to marriage formation.** US and UK research (Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies reports, 2022-2024; UK Office for National Statistics analyses) links rising home prices and rents to delayed marriage and cohabitation, since many couples still treat homeownership or stable housing as a marriage precondition; China's property-price surge through the 2010s-early 2020s is separately cited (Chinese Academy of Social Sciences commentary, 2022-2023) as compounding the bare-branches problem, since bride families in many regions expect the groom's family to own an apartment before marriage — pricing out lower-income men on top of the existing sex-ratio deficit.
