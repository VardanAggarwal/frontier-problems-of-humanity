# In-Group Identity/Cohesion (Tier 3 — Belonging)

> The we-feeling that lets strangers act as kin at scale.

Tier definition: group cohesion as a bonding mechanism scaled from blood-kin to symbolic/national in-groups — the "we-feeling" that lets strangers act as kin.

## How the need has been threatened

**1. Berlin Conference borders, 1884–85.** European powers partitioned Africa into colonial territories using straight lines on maps, with no reference to existing ethnic, linguistic, or kingdom boundaries. The result split single ethnic groups (Ewe between Ghana and Togo; Somali across five states) across new borders while forcing rival groups into shared colonial units, wrecking pre-existing in-group cohesion at both ends (Ali Mazrui, "The Bondage of Boundaries," 1994; Rene Lemarchand on colonial boundary-making).

**2. Sykes-Picot Agreement, 1916.** British and French diplomats Mark Sykes and François Georges-Picot secretly drew Middle East spheres of influence that became the borders of Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Jordan, ignoring Kurdish, Sunni-Shia, and tribal lines. James Barr, *A Line in the Sand* (2011), documents how the resulting states each fused mutually hostile in-groups under one flag, a structural fault line still visible in Iraqi and Syrian civil conflict.

**3. Belgian ethnic engineering in Rwanda, 1926–1959.** Belgian colonial administrators formalized fluid Hutu/Tutsi social categories into fixed racial identity cards (from 1933) and favored Tutsi elites for administrative roles, then reversed favoritism toward Hutu after 1959 — manufacturing a rigid ethnic cleavage where a more fluid clan-based cohesion had existed (Mahmood Mamdani, *When Victims Become Killers*, 2001).

**4. Sovietization and suppression of national identities, USSR, 1920s–1980s.** Soviet policy alternated between formal "national in form" republics and brutal suppression of actual national cohesion — Stalin's deportations of entire peoples (Chechens and Crimean Tatars, 1944), the Ukrainian Holodomor (1932–33, killing an estimated 3.5–5 million and functioning partly to break Ukrainian national cohesion), and russification campaigns replacing local languages in schools (Terry Martin, *The Affirmative Action Empire*, 2001).

**5. Sinicization of Tibet and Xinjiang, 1950s–present.** Following the 1950 invasion of Tibet and 1949 incorporation of Xinjiang, Chinese state policy has promoted Han migration, restricted religious and linguistic practice, and since 2017 detained an estimated 1+ million Uyghurs in "re-education" camps aimed explicitly at severing cultural and religious in-group transmission (Adrian Zenz, "Thoroughly Reforming Them," 2019; UN OHCHR Xinjiang report, 2022).

**6. Yugoslav breakup, 1991–2001.** Josip Broz Tito's postwar state spent decades constructing a supranational "Yugoslav" identity over Serb, Croat, Bosniak, Slovene, and Macedonian in-groups. After Tito's death (1980) and the 1991 secession wave, this cohesion fractured violently along the older ethnic lines, culminating in the Bosnian War (1992–95) and Srebrenica genocide (1995) (Misha Glenny, *The Fall of Yugoslavia*, 1992).

**7. Korean division, 1945.** The US-Soviet agreement to divide Korea at the 38th parallel for postwar occupation split a single, ethnically and linguistically cohesive nation with a millennium-plus shared identity into two states that fought a war (1950–53) and remain separated, severing families and regional in-group continuity for three generations (Bruce Cumings, *The Korean War*, 2010).

**8. Cyprus partition, 1974.** Following a Greek-backed coup and Turkish invasion, Cyprus split into a Greek Cypriot south and Turkish Cypriot north along the "Green Line," with roughly 150,000–200,000 people displaced along ethnic lines and the island's mixed communities forcibly resegregated (UN Peacekeeping Force in Cyprus records; Christopher Hitchens, *Hostage to History*, 1997).

**9. German division, 1945–1990.** Post-WWII occupation split Germany into East and West along Cold War ideological lines, formalized by the Berlin Wall (1961–89), severing a single national in-group into two states with divergent institutions, propaganda, and generational identity for 45 years (Mary Fulbrook, *A History of Germany 1918–2008*, 2009).

**10. American Indian boarding schools, 1879–1970s.** Founded by Richard Henry Pratt with the explicit motto "kill the Indian, save the man," the Carlisle Indian Industrial School (1879) and roughly 350 subsequent US and Canadian institutions removed Native children from families, banned native languages, and cut hair and dress markers of tribal identity, systematically severing intergenerational transmission of in-group cohesion (US Department of the Interior, Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative Report, 2022).

**11. Forced assimilation of Indigenous Australians ("Stolen Generations"), 1905–1970s.** Australian state and federal policies removed an estimated 1 in 3 Indigenous children from families between roughly 1910 and 1970 for placement in institutions or with white families, aiming explicitly at cultural absorption (Australian Human Rights Commission, *Bringing Them Home* report, 1997).

**12. Armenian and Assyrian genocide and deportation, 1915–1923.** Beyond mass killing, the Ottoman deportations dispersed Armenian and Assyrian survivors across the Levant, Europe, and the Americas, severing the territorial anchor for in-group cohesion and forcing identity onto a permanently diasporic footing (Taner Akçam, *A Shameful Act*, 2006).

**13. Palestinian dispersal, 1948 (Nakba).** The 1948 war displaced an estimated 700,000 Palestinians from what became Israel, dispersing a previously territorially concentrated in-group across refugee camps in Lebanon, Jordan, Syria, and the West Bank/Gaza, fracturing direct territorial cohesion into exile-sustained identity (Benny Morris, *The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem*, 1987/2004 revised).

**14. Rohingya expulsion from Myanmar, 2016–2017.** Myanmar military "clearance operations" drove over 700,000 Rohingya into Bangladesh, following decades of statelessness imposed by the 1982 Citizenship Law that had already stripped the group's recognized legal in-group status within the state (UN Fact-Finding Mission on Myanmar report, 2018).

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **Nationalism as deliberate cohesion engineering.** Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (1983) documented how print capitalism (vernacular newspapers, novels) let people who would never meet feel part of one national "we" — nationalism is not a natural residue of ethnicity but a constructed technology of cohesion, built through standardized language education (French Third Republic school laws, 1880s) and mass media.
- **National symbols as engineered cohesion tools.** Flags, anthems, and holidays are deliberately designed bonding technology: the US Pledge of Allegiance (1892), Bastille Day as republican civic ritual (formalized 1880), and post-independence African and Asian states' rapid adoption of new flags and anthems (India, 1947; dozens of African states, 1957–1975) to manufacture a national in-group where colonial rule had left fragments.
- **Federalism and devolution as identity accommodation.** Rather than forcing assimilation, federal structures let sub-national in-groups retain cohesion within a larger state: Switzerland's cantonal system (formalized 1848) accommodates German, French, Italian, and Romansh in-groups; Spain's 1978 Constitution created autonomous communities (Catalonia, Basque Country) after Franco-era Castilian-only suppression; the UK's devolution acts (Scotland and Wales, 1998) restored recognized sub-national political identity.
- **Minority rights and language protection law.** The Framework Convention for the Protection of National Minorities (Council of Europe, 1995) and language-protection statutes such as Canada's Official Languages Act (1969, protecting Francophone Quebec identity within the federation) formalized state obligations to sustain minority in-group cohesion rather than erase it.
- **Diaspora nationalism sustaining identity across distance.** Zionism, formalized at the First Zionist Congress (Basel, 1897) under Theodor Herzl, organized a stateless, dispersed in-group around a shared restorative national project, culminating in the 1948 establishment of Israel. Irish diaspora nationalism (Clan na Gael in the US, funding the 1916 Easter Rising) and Kurdish transnational political identity (sustained across Iraq, Turkey, Syria, and Iran diasporas since the mid-20th century) show the same pattern: shared narrative and institutions substituting for territorial contiguity.
- **Reunification and reconciliation processes.** German reunification (October 3, 1990) formally restored a single national in-group after 45 years of division, though "inner unification" of divergent identities remained a decades-long social project (studies documenting persistent East-West attitudinal gaps into the 2010s). South Africa's post-apartheid "Rainbow Nation" project, launched under Nelson Mandela (1994) with Desmond Tutu's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–2003), explicitly sought to construct a shared multiracial national in-group atop apartheid's engineered divisions.
- **International indigenous and minority rights frameworks.** The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007), adopted after over two decades of negotiation, formally recognizes indigenous peoples' rights to maintain and strengthen distinct cultural, linguistic, and political in-group identity, and has been invoked in subsequent redress efforts including Canada's and Australia's formal apologies for boarding-school and Stolen Generations policies (Canadian apology, 2008; Australian apology, 2008).

## Where this fails today

**Sudan's ethnicized civil war, 2023–present.** The Rwandan Armed Forces/Sudanese Armed Forces conflict since April 2023 has an explicit ethnic dimension in Darfur, where the RSF and allied Arab militias have targeted the Masalit and other non-Arab groups. The UN and US State Department both determined in 2024–2025 that the RSF committed genocide against the Masalit in West Darfur, following the November 2023 massacre in El Geneina that killed an estimated 800–15,000 people (US State Department genocide determination, January 2025; UN Panel of Experts on Sudan, 2024). Over 12 million people have been displaced (UNHCR, 2025), fracturing in-group territorial continuity on top of the killing.

**Myanmar's Rohingya, continued statelessness, 2021–present.** Roughly 1 million Rohingya remain in Cox's Bazar camps in Bangladesh nearly a decade after the 2017 expulsion, with no citizenship pathway restored; Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law still excludes them. Since the 2021 military coup, fighting between the Arakan Army and the junta in Rakhine State has produced fresh Rohingya displacement — the UN reported over 45,000 newly displaced in 2024 alone (UNHCR, 2024; Human Rights Watch, "An Island of Nowhere," 2024), with Rohingya accused by both sides of being an unrecognized in-group with no state protector.

**Ethiopia's Tigray-Amhara-federal tensions, post-2022.** The Pretoria Agreement (November 2022) ended the Tigray War (2020–2022, an estimated 600,000+ dead) but left Western Tigray under Amhara regional control and did not resolve competing territorial and identity claims. Amhara Fano militia, once allied with the federal government against Tigray, launched its own insurgency from mid-2023 after federal disarmament efforts, and Human Rights Watch documented ongoing ethnic-targeted killings in Amhara region through 2024–2025 (HRW, "Ethiopia: Amhara Militia, Civilians Killed," 2024). The ceasefire stopped one war without restoring a shared national "we-feeling" across Tigrayan, Amhara, and Oromo in-groups.

**Democratic polarization fracturing national cohesion, US.** Pew Research's polarization tracking shows affective polarization — dislike and distrust of the opposing party's supporters — at or near record highs through 2024, with majorities in both parties describing the other side as more immoral, dishonest, and closed-minded than a decade earlier (Pew Research Center, "Views of the Parties," 2024). The American National Election Studies and Stanford's Polarization Research Lab both documented continued growth in "negative partisanship" through the 2024 election cycle, with cross-party marriage rates and geographic self-sorting cited as compounding indicators of eroding shared in-group identity within a single nation-state.

**Uyghur cultural erasure, ongoing.** The leaked "Xinjiang Police Files" (2022) and subsequent reporting confirmed the scale of the detention system the 2022 UN OHCHR report had already found "may constitute crimes against humanity." Independent monitors (Uyghur Human Rights Project, 2024–2025 reporting) document continued forced labor transfers, demolition of mosques and shrines, and Mandarin-only schooling replacing Uyghur-language instruction, sustaining the severing of intergenerational cultural transmission first documented in 2019.

**Active separatism, Catalonia and Kurdistan.** Spain's 2024 amnesty law pardoned Catalan independence leaders from the 2017 referendum crisis, but polling (CEO Catalonia, 2024–2025) continues to show Catalan society roughly split on independence, with the underlying identity cleavage unresolved rather than reconciled. The PKK's May 2025 announcement of dissolution and disarmament, following imprisoned leader Abdullah Öcalan's call, marked a rare de-escalation, but Kurdish political status in Syria (SDF-controlled northeast) and Iraq remains contested and unresolved amid Syria's post-Assad transition (2024–2025).
