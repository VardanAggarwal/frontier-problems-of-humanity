# Hereditary/Caste Status — Tier 4 (Esteem)

> Rank assigned by birth into a hereditary order, gating resource and mating access.

Rank gates resource and mating access within a group. When hereditary status closes, esteem stops being convertible into access — provoking either forced leveling or entrenched backlash. History shows four distinct failure modes, not one: sudden legal abolition, slow economic erosion, violent reassertion when loss is threatened, and quiet persistence/adaptation under formal abolition. Colonial administration adds a fifth: outside powers hardening indigenous hierarchies for their own convenience.

## Dated episodes

1. **France, 4 August 1789** — the National Assembly's "Night of August 4th" abolished feudal privilege, tithes, seigneurial dues, and (by June 1790) hereditary noble titles outright. This is the paradigm case of *sudden legal abolition* — status revoked by decree rather than eroded (William Doyle, *Origins of the French Revolution*).

2. **Russia, 1917-1918** — the Bolsheviks abolished the Petrine Table of Ranks (1722-1917), estates (dvorianstvo, meshchanstvo, krestianstvo), and hereditary titles by decree of the Central Executive Committee, November 1917. Unlike France, this abolition was paired with physical liquidation of the class (dekulakization, Red Terror), not just legal delisting (Orlando Figes, *A People's Tragedy*).

3. **Japan, 1876 Haitōrei Edict** — banned samurai from carrying swords in public, following the 1871 abolition of the han system and 1873 conscription law that ended the samurai's military monopoly. Status didn't vanish overnight; ex-samurai retained "shizoku" registry status into the 20th century even after real privilege had gone — an example of *legal status outliving functional status* (Eiko Ikegami, *The Taming of the Samurai*).

4. **Britain, 1870s-1914** — the "Great Depression of British Agriculture" and 1894 death duties (Finance Act) began taxing landed estates directly, forcing sales; by 1914 roughly a quarter of England had changed hands out of aristocratic ownership since 1880. This is *gradual economic erosion* of hereditary status without legal abolition — titles persisted, power didn't (David Cannadine, *The Decline and Fall of the British Aristocracy*).

5. **Central Europe, 1918-1919** — the Habsburg, German, and lesser noble houses lost formal legal privilege when Austria's 1919 law abolished titles of nobility outright (Adelsaufhebungsgesetz) and Germany's Weimar Constitution (Article 109, 1919) ended noble legal privileges, even though many noble families retained land and informal social status for decades — abolition-in-law without abolition-in-practice.

6. **India, 1950 constitutional abolition of untouchability (Article 17)** coexisting with **ongoing caste violence** — the 2006 Khairlanji massacre (Maharashtra), where a Dalit family was murdered after asserting land rights, and the National Crime Records Bureau's routine annual count of tens of thousands of registered crimes against Scheduled Castes, show *formal abolition without informal enforcement collapse*. The Prevention of Atrocities Act 1989 exists precisely because 1950 abolition didn't stop caste-based violence (Anand Teltumbde, *The Khairlanji Murders*).

7. **India, backlash against caste assertion** — the 2006 anti-reservation protests against extending OBC quotas to elite institutions, and repeated upper-caste mobilizations (e.g., the 2016 Jat reservation-quota riots in Haryana demanding *inclusion* in reserved categories) show status groups reacting to *relative* not just absolute status loss — when a lower group's floor rises, a threatened group riots to protect its own gate, sometimes by demanding entry into the protected category itself.

8. **India, British colonial census, 1871-1931** — the decennial Census of India, especially under H.H. Risley (1901), classified and ranked jatis by "ethnographic" criteria, converting a regionally variable, often negotiable caste order into a fixed, all-India hierarchy for administrative legibility. Nicholas Dirks (*Castes of Mind*, 2001) and Susan Bayly (*Caste, Society and Politics in India*, 1999) argue this colonial codification hardened boundaries that were previously more fluid — the paradigm case of *external reinforcement of local hereditary hierarchy for administrative convenience*.

9. **Korea, Baekjeong** — the outcaste butcher/leatherworker group was formally freed from legal caste status under the 1894 Gabo Reforms, yet faced organized discrimination into the 1920s-30s, prompting the Hyeongpyeongsa movement (founded 1923) — a Baekjeong-led civil rights organization, showing formal 1894 abolition did not end social exclusion for decades (Joong-Seok Seo, *Korea's Modernization*).

10. **Japan, Burakumin** — the 1871 Emancipation Edict abolished the legal "eta/hinin" outcaste designation, yet residential segregation, employment discrimination, and family-registry (koseki) tracing of Burakumin ancestry persisted into the postwar era, prompting the 1922 Suiheisha ("Levelers Society") movement and later the 1965 Dowa (assimilation) policy — a century-plus gap between legal and social abolition (George De Vos & Hiroshi Wagatsuma, *Japan's Invisible Race*).

11. **Sub-Saharan West Africa, "casted" occupational groups** — Mande-speaking societies (Mali, Senegal, Guinea) retain hereditary artisan/griot (jeli) endogamous strata alongside "freeborn" (horon) status, documented as functioning into the 21st century in marriage restrictions, not from any single colonial-era law but from precolonial social structure that survived both colonial rule and post-independence nation-building (Tal Tamari, "The Development of Caste Systems in West Africa," *Journal of African History*, 1991).

12. **China, imperial exam system decline and 1905 abolition** — the keju examination system (established ~7th century, formalized under the Sui/Tang) had already partially substituted merit credentialing for hereditary aristocracy over a millennium; its 1905 abolition (Qing edict) under reformist pressure removed the last major *pre-modern* meritocratic counterweight to hereditary elite reproduction just before the dynasty's 1911 collapse (Benjamin Elman, *A Cultural History of Civil Examinations in Late Imperial China*).

13. **South Africa, apartheid pass laws and their 1986/1994 unwinding** — while race-based rather than caste-based in the Indian sense, the apartheid system (1948-1994) functioned as a state-enforced hereditary status hierarchy gating land, marriage, and labor access; the Population Registration Act was repealed 1991, full legal equality arrived with the 1994 constitution — another case of sudden top-down legal abolition following decades of entrenchment (Leonard Thompson, *A History of South Africa*).

14. **Rwanda, Hutu/Tutsi hierarchy hardening under Belgian rule, 1930s** — Belgian colonial administrators issued identity cards from 1933 fixing "Hutu," "Tutsi," "Twa" as hereditary, exclusive categories based on cattle-ownership thresholds, converting a more fluid precolonial social-economic distinction into a rigid administrative caste, with catastrophic downstream consequences in 1994 — a second major case (alongside #8) of *colonial administrative fixing of a prior fluid hierarchy* (Mahmood Mamdani, *When Victims Become Killers*, 2001).

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **Sudden legal abolition of hereditary privilege.** France's Night of August 4th 1789 and the subsequent June 1790 decree abolishing hereditary nobility is the template: strip legal recognition of birth-based rank in one legislative act, rather than negotiate it down gradually. Russia (1917) and Austria/Germany (1919) followed the same abolition-by-decree logic after their own revolutions/collapses.

- **Meritocratic credentialing as an alternative allocator of rank.** China's imperial examination system (institutionalized by the Tang dynasty, lasting to 1905) is the oldest large-scale substitute: status via tested merit rather than birth, open in principle to commoners for over a millennium before Europe had an equivalent. Britain's Northcote-Trevelyan Report (1854) and subsequent Civil Service reform (Orders in Council, 1870) replaced patronage appointment with open competitive examination, deliberately importing the meritocratic principle to break aristocratic capture of state office (Peter Hennessy, *Whitehall*).

- **Affirmative action / reservation systems compensating for caste disadvantage.** India's Constitution (1950) reserved seats in legislatures, government jobs, and educational institutions for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Articles 15(4), 16(4), 330, 332), later extended to Other Backward Classes following the Mandal Commission report (1980, implemented 1990). This is a direct institutional attempt to convert formal legal equality into substantive access where centuries of exclusion left gaps that abolition alone didn't close.

- **Statutory anti-discrimination law targeting caste-based violence specifically.** India's Prevention of Atrocities Act 1989 (SC/ST Act) criminalizes caste-based violence and humiliation with enhanced penalties and special courts — a recognition that general equality law (Article 17, 1950) was insufficient against continuing informal enforcement of caste hierarchy. The UK's 2010 Equality Act included a clause (Section 9(5)) enabling caste to be added as a protected characteristic, following lobbying after documented caste discrimination in the UK's South Asian diaspora; the enabling clause was never formally activated by ministerial order, showing this mechanism is contested and incomplete even in a jurisdiction with no indigenous caste system of its own (House of Commons Library briefing, "Caste Discrimination," 2018).

- **International human-rights framing of caste (or "descent-based discrimination") as a treaty-monitored category.** The UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (CERD) issued General Recommendation No. 29 (2002) explicitly interpreting "descent" in Article 1 of the ICERD Convention to cover caste, obliging state parties (including India) to report on and remedy caste discrimination under an existing human-rights treaty rather than requiring a caste-specific new convention.

- **Occupational and geographic mobility diluting caste enforceability.** Urbanization and labor migration (India's post-1991 economic liberalization is the largest recent instance) weaken the village-level social surveillance that caste enforcement historically depended on — anonymity in cities makes inter-caste transactions harder to police, a structural rather than legal mechanism working alongside statutory reform (Surinder Jodhka, *Caste in Contemporary India*, 2015).

## Where this fails today

- **India, ongoing Dalit atrocity statistics.** NCRB's *Crime in India* reports recorded 57,582 registered cases of crimes against Scheduled Castes and 10,064 against Scheduled Tribes in 2022 — up from roughly 50,900 SC cases in 2021 — a rising, not falling, count 72 years after Article 17's abolition of untouchability. Convictions under the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act remain low relative to filings, with pendency rates over 90% in several states per National Crime Records Bureau and Ministry of Home Affairs data cited in Parliament (2023 Lok Sabha replies). High-profile 2020s cases — the July 2023 killing of a nine-year-old Dalit boy in Rajasthan's Surana village allegedly for drinking from a teacher's water pot, and repeated "manual scavenging" deaths (Safai Karamchari Andolan counted over 400 sewer/septic-tank deaths 2019-2023) — show occupational caste stigma persisting in fatal form.

- **Indian diaspora, caste in tech/corporate contexts.** California's Civil Rights Department (formerly DFEH) sued Cisco Systems in 2020 alleging a Dalit engineer was discriminated against by upper-caste managers; the case proceeded through 2023-2024 in state court. This prompted legislative action: California SB 403 (2023), which would have added caste as a protected category under the state's Fair Employment and Housing Act, passed both chambers but was vetoed by Governor Newsom in October 2023 on grounds existing "ancestry" protections already covered it. Seattle's city council passed a caste-discrimination ordinance in February 2023 — the first such law by a US city — and Fresno, California followed in January 2024. Surveys by Equality Labs (2018, cited repeatedly through 2023-24 advocacy) reported roughly 1 in 4 Dalit respondents in the US had faced workplace caste discrimination, though the sample and methodology remain contested by opposing Hindu-American groups.

- **Nepal, caste system.** The Muluki Ain caste hierarchy was legally abolished in 1963 and caste-based discrimination criminalized further by the Caste-Based Discrimination and Untouchability (Offence and Punishment) Act, 2011. Enforcement remains weak: the May 2020 lynching of Nawaraj BK and five companions in Rukum district, killed by a mob after BK (a Dalit) eloped with an upper-caste woman, led to convictions only in 2023 — three years later — illustrating the same abolition/enforcement gap as India. Nepal's National Dalit Commission continues to report caste-based "untouchability" practices (temple entry bans, inter-caste marriage violence) into 2023-2024.

- **Hereditary privilege in monarchies/aristocracies.** The UK's House of Lords retained 92 hereditary peers as of 2024; a Labour government bill introduced in 2024 (Second Reading September 2024) proposes removing all remaining hereditary peers' right to sit and vote — debate ongoing into 2025-2026, showing hereditary legislative status persisting over a century after the 1911 Parliament Act first curbed the Lords.

- **West Africa, occupational caste-like hierarchies.** Mauritania criminalized slavery a fourth time in 2007 and again strengthened penalties in 2015, yet the 2023 US State Department Trafficking in Persons report and SOS-Esclaves (a Mauritanian anti-slavery NGO) continue documenting descent-based slavery among Haratine communities. Niger's courts have heard cases into the 2020s involving "wahaya" (hereditary fifth-wife/concubine status tied to descent) and caste-based marriage bans among Tuareg and Zarma communities, per Anti-Slavery International reporting (2022-2023).
