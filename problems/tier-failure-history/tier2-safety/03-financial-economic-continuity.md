# Financial/Economic Continuity (Tier 2 — Safety)

Tier definition: a buffer against unpredictable future threats to resources. Money, savings, and banks are the storage medium for that buffer; this need is about the medium itself failing — through inflation, bank collapse, default, confiscation, or fraud — independent of any single threat to the resources it stores.

## How this need has been threatened

**1. Unconstrained monetary financing of deficits — hyperinflation.** When a state funds spending by printing money rather than taxing or borrowing at market rates, the currency itself is the thing destroyed, wiping out savings, wages, and fixed-income contracts held in that currency. Weimar Germany's reparations-plus-deficit spending drove prices up roughly a billionfold between 1922–23; by November 1923, 1 USD equaled 4.2 trillion marks, and wages were paid daily or twice daily because money lost value between shifts. Hungary's 1945–46 episode is the worst ever recorded — prices doubled every 15 hours at the July 1946 peak, and the pengő was replaced by the forint at 4×10^29 pengő to 1. The mechanism recurred with local triggers but an identical monetary cause: Yugoslavia's 1993–94 breakup/sanctions inflation (monthly rate ~313 million percent, January 1994, prices doubling every ~34 hours); Zimbabwe's 2008 collapse (peak monthly inflation 79.6 billion percent, November 2008, per Hanke–Krus, the most-cited estimate — a separate, differently-scoped *annual* figure of 89.7 sextillion percent for the same episode also circulates and is not the same measurement, see numbers-hygiene note below); Bolivia's 1984–85 crisis (annual inflation near 24,000%, some estimates to ~60,000% at peak); and Venezuela's episode (IMF-estimated annual inflation over 1,000,000% by 2018, cooling to double/low-triple digits by 2023 under de facto dollarization, before pressures reportedly resumed in 2024–25 — a genuine non-monotonic trend, not a straight-line collapse).

**2. Bank runs and deposit freezes in the absence of a credible backstop.** A bank holds illiquid assets against liquid, callable deposits; if depositors doubt solvency, a doubt-triggered run can fail an otherwise-solvent bank, and the loss lands on whoever could not withdraw first. Roughly 9,000 US banks failed 1930–33 — about a third of the ~25,000-bank system — with no deposit insurance and no reliable lender of last resort, deepening the Depression-era money-supply contraction by roughly a third (Friedman & Schwartz). The same mechanism, not merely the same name, recurs wherever the backstop is absent or is itself withdrawn: Northern Rock (UK, Sept 2007) suffered the UK's first bank run in over a century after losing wholesale funding access; Cyprus's 2013 bail-in converted ~47.5% of large deposits above €100,000 at Bank of Cyprus into equity, the first time in the Eurozone crisis that depositors rather than taxpayers absorbed bank losses directly; Argentina's 2001–02 "corralito" froze withdrawals to ~$250/week by decree, then forcibly converted dollar deposits to devalued pesos; Lebanon's banks have frozen depositors out of their own dollar accounts since 2019 via an informal "lollar" system with no bank-restructuring law passed as of 2025, and the lira has lost over 98% of its value since 2019. Silicon Valley Bank ($209bn assets) and Signature Bank ($110bn) failed within days of each other in March 2023 on uninsured-deposit runs after unrealized bond losses became public — First Republic ($229bn) followed within weeks — demonstrating that deposit insurance calibrated to a 1930s withdrawal speed does not fully cover a digital, same-day run; the FDIC's invocation of a "systemic risk exception" to guarantee uninsured deposits was an implicit admission of that gap.

**3. Sovereign default and debt-service crisis.** A government borrows beyond what its revenue can service, and the default (or the austerity used to avoid one) breaks the state's own continuity function for its citizens. Argentina has defaulted on external debt at least nine times since independence (1976, 1982, 1989, 2001, 2014, 2020, 2023), with the 2001–02 default (~$100bn, then the largest sovereign default in history) triggering the deposit freeze in mechanism 2. Greece required three EU/IMF bailouts (2010, 2012, 2015) totaling over €280bn after understated deficit figures came to light, imposing capital controls (€60/day withdrawal limits, 2015) and unemployment above 25% at the worst point. Sri Lanka's April 2022 default on $51bn — its first ever — followed fuel and food shortages and mass protests, resolved via its 17th IMF program since independence. Ghana defaulted on most external debt in December 2022, spending roughly half of government revenue on debt service before restructuring; Zambia's 2020 default took nearly four years to resolve under the G20 Common Framework, one of the slowest sovereign workouts on record. Pakistan avoided default only via a $3bn IMF standby (June 2023) and $7bn Extended Fund Facility (2024) after reserves fell to barely a month of import cover. The World Bank's *International Debt Report 2024* found low-income countries' interest payments alone consumed roughly 6% of export revenue in 2023 — the highest since 2000 — and, separately, UNCTAD's *A World of Debt* (2023) found 3.3 billion people live in countries now spending more on debt-interest payments than on health or education combined: a structural continuity failure distinct from any single default event, because it forecloses the next shock's fiscal room before that shock arrives. (These are two different metrics from two different reports and should not be read as one claim.)

**4. Asset bubbles and market-structure collapse.** Markets that price an asset above any income stream it can plausibly generate eventually correct, and the correction destroys capital that was never backed by real value in the first place. The South Sea Bubble (Britain, 1719–20) is the first recorded instance at national-fiscal scale: stock backed by exaggerated trade-monopoly claims and a government debt-swap scheme rose from ~£128 to £1,000 in months before collapsing, wiping out investors including Isaac Newton and prompting the Bubble Act (1720), which restricted joint-stock company formation for over a century. The 2007–09 Global Financial Crisis is the modern instance at the largest scale: US subprime mortgages, repackaged into securities rated far above their real default risk, triggered Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy (Sept 2008, $600bn+ in assets, the largest US bankruptcy filing in history) and a near-collapse of the global banking system, requiring the $700bn TARP bailout and coordinated central-bank intervention. FTX (Nov 2022) is the same mechanism inside an asset class with no deposit-insurance-equivalent at all: a $32bn valuation collapsed within days once it emerged customer funds (~$8bn) had been misused, with founder Sam Bankman-Fried convicted of fraud in 2024 — worth separating from the mortgage instance because the underlying failure was outright misappropriation of customer assets rather than mispriced collateral, the distinction mechanism 5 turns on.

**5. Fraud exploiting a trust or verification gap.** Distinct from mechanism 4 because the loss is not a mispriced market correcting — it is deliberate misrepresentation that a slower verification process failed to catch before redemptions or a market shock exposed it. Bernard Madoff's Ponzi scheme (exposed 2008) fabricated roughly $65bn in account balances over decades, collapsing only when 2008-crisis redemption requests outran new investor inflows; it is the largest Ponzi fraud on record and the paradigm case of a regulator (the SEC had received specific whistleblower warnings from 1999 onward) failing to act on an available signal. FTX above is the same mechanism recurring in a newer, less-regulated asset class.

**6. State-imposed currency and convertibility shocks.** Distinct from mechanisms 1–3: here the state does not lose control of the currency, it exercises control over its citizens' claims on that currency, by decree rather than market failure. The US Executive Order 6102 (1933) compelled private holders to sell gold to the Federal Reserve at $20.67/oz, after which the government revalued gold to $35/oz — an effective one-directional expropriation of anyone who complied, justified as breaking the Depression-era deflationary constraint of the gold standard. The 1971 Nixon Shock ended dollar-to-gold convertibility entirely, closing the gold-anchored era and opening a new, still-unresolved category of floating-currency value risk that none of Bretton Woods's architects had needed to price. India's 2016 demonetization is the same mechanism at a different scale and stated purpose: overnight invalidation of 500 and 1,000 rupee notes (86% of currency in circulation by value, targeting black money and counterfeiting) produced a severe cash-liquidity shock across the ~90% of transactions that were cash-based, and a documented GDP growth slowdown in following quarters.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **Bank of England founded (1694).** Chartered to fund government war debt, it became the founding model of a modern central bank — a public–private hybrid with money-issuance authority — that every later central bank was built on.
- **Bagehot's lender-of-last-resort doctrine (1866).** Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* articulated that a central bank should lend freely against good collateral at a penalty rate during a panic, to stop a run without rewarding the recklessness that caused it — still the operating doctrine for crisis response.
- **Federal Reserve System (1913), prompted by the Panic of 1907.** A liquidity crisis resolved only by J.P. Morgan personally organizing private bank support exposed the US's lack of a lender of last resort, directly motivating the Fed's creation.
- **Deposit insurance — FDIC (1933) and Glass-Steagall.** Passed directly in response to the 1930–33 failures documented in mechanism 2; Glass-Steagall separated commercial from investment banking to reduce speculative risk-taking with insured deposits, and deposit insurance removed the incentive to run on a bank at the first rumor of trouble.
- **Bretton Woods, IMF and World Bank (1944).** Created a fixed exchange-rate order pegged to gold-backed dollars, plus the IMF as an international lender of last resort for balance-of-payments crises — the institution repeatedly invoked in every sovereign-default and currency crisis above.
- **Basel Accords (1988, 2004, 2010–11).** International bank capital-adequacy standards set minimum reserves against risk-weighted assets, aimed directly at the undercapitalized leverage that produced the 2008 crisis.
- **Inflation targeting and central-bank independence (from 1990).** New Zealand's 1989 Reserve Bank Act was the first to legislate an explicit inflation target and legal independence from fiscal authority, removing the temptation to print money for short-term political gain that drove mechanism 1's hyperinflation episodes.
- **Currency boards and dollarization as commitment devices.** Countries with weak monetary credibility (Hong Kong 1983–present; Ecuador 2000; Zimbabwe 2009) surrendered domestic money-printing power entirely as a hard commitment against relapse into mechanism 1.
- **Bankruptcy and sovereign-debt restructuring law as orderly-failure mechanisms.** US Chapter 11 reorganization and sovereign-restructuring frameworks (used in Greece 2012, and Argentina's various defaults) let insolvent firms and states restructure debt through a legally ordered process instead of chaotic default, preserving some continuity of function and creditor recovery.
- **Post-2008 reforms — Dodd-Frank (2010), stress testing, resolution regimes.** Created "living wills" for systemically important banks and orderly-resolution authority, aimed at making a bank failure survivable by the system rather than eliminating failure itself.

### Where it worked

- **FDIC deposit insurance, USA.** Bank failures ran at roughly 500+ a year through the early 1930s (peaking near 4,000 in 1933 alone); after federal deposit insurance took effect in January 1934, annual bank failures fell to single digits for most of the following four decades, and no insured depositor has ever lost a cent of an insured deposit in FDIC's 90-plus-year history. The tier's cleanest positive control — a single instrument removing the *incentive* to run, not merely responding to runs after they start.
- **New Zealand inflation targeting (1990–present).** Annual CPI inflation ran near 15–18% through much of the 1980s; the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act (1989) set an explicit target band and legislated independence, and inflation fell to and stayed near the 1–3% target band through the 1990s and 2000s, becoming the template over 30 other central banks adopted, including the UK (1992) and Canada (1991).
- **Hong Kong's currency board / US-dollar peg (1983–present).** Adopted after a 1983 confidence crisis drove the Hong Kong dollar down roughly 33% in weeks, the peg (HK$7.8/US$1) has held for over four decades, including through the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis when every other regional peg (Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea) broke — a measured survival where the comparison group is the crisis itself.
- **The Brady Plan (1989) and the end of Latin America's 1980s debt crisis.** Commercial bank loans to Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, and others — effectively unpayable after 1982 — were converted into tradeable "Brady bonds" partly collateralized by US Treasury zero-coupon bonds. Mexico's 1989 restructuring cut its bank debt by roughly 35% and unlocked new capital-market access; sovereign borrowing spreads for restructured countries fell and GDP growth resumed within a few years across the cohort, ending what economists at the time called the "lost decade."
- **Israel's 1985 stabilization program.** Annual inflation reached 445% in 1984; a joint wage-price-exchange-rate freeze plus fiscal tightening (July 1985) brought it down to roughly 20% by 1986 and under 20% for most of the following decade — one of the fastest disinflations on record without a currency collapse or default.
- **Bolivia's 1985 New Economic Policy.** Following mechanism 1's ~24,000% peak, a currency float plus an immediate, severe fiscal-deficit cut brought monthly inflation from over 20% to under 2% within about a year — evidence that even the most extreme hyperinflation episodes are stoppable fast once deficit monetization itself is stopped, not merely slowed.
- **A limited positive control, not a clean one: Argentina's Convertibility Plan (1991–2001).** A hard peso–dollar currency board ended an inflation run that had reached roughly 3,080% in 1989, holding inflation in single digits for a decade — genuinely one of the fastest disinflations here — but the same rigidity that stopped inflation prevented the exchange-rate adjustment the economy needed by 2001, and the plan's collapse produced the corralito documented in mechanism 2. Worth recording as a control that worked on its own terms and then generated the next entry on the threat list — the closest financial-continuity analogue to tier 1's "the previous solution's side effects are usually the current problem."
- **A non-state win, India: Bandhan Bank's transition out of the 2010 Andhra Pradesh microfinance crisis.** The crisis itself is the negative baseline this instance is measured against: over-indebted informal borrowers, coercive collection, and no prudential regulation collapsed the sector's loan portfolio from $5.4bn to $4.3bn (2010–11) and its borrower base from 32.5 million to 26.4 million, with over 200 suicides linked to the collapse. Bandhan, then an NBFC-MFI serving the same population, converted into a full, RBI-supervised, priority-sector-compliant universal bank in 2015 rather than continuing as an unregulated lender. By March 2024 it served 33 million customers, three-quarters still in its microfinance/micro-enterprise segment, and posted its first full profitable year (₹1,111 crore, FY2024). The same ownership/incentive pattern tier 1 found in Amul — a for-profit structure that stayed with the originally underserved population rather than moving upmarket once it had scale — but here the mechanism is regulatory conversion (NBFC-MFI to bank) rather than cooperative ownership, and it is the first tier-2 instance of a private, for-profit actor reaching the harmed population directly and profitably, not merely a state programme or a nonprofit.

## Where this fails today

**2019–2026 — Lebanon's ongoing currency and banking collapse.** The lira has lost over 98% of its value since 2019; the central bank's official peg finally gave way to market rates in 2023. Banks have frozen depositors out of their own dollar accounts since 2019, forcing withdrawals through a devalued "lollar" system; the World Bank has called it one of the top three most severe economic crises globally since the mid-1800s. No comprehensive bank restructuring law had passed as of 2025.

**2023–2026 — Sudan's war-driven currency collapse.** Civil war since April 2023 has destroyed the banking system in Khartoum and other cities; the Sudanese pound has depreciated sharply on parallel markets and inflation has repeatedly run above 100% year-on-year, per IMF regional estimates, compounding a humanitarian crisis with millions displaced.

**2024 — Zimbabwe's sixth currency in 15 years.** After the RTGS dollar/Zimbabwe dollar lost most of its value again (2023 annual inflation near 175%), the government launched the gold-backed "ZiG" (Zimbabwe Gold) in April 2024. The ZiG itself devalued roughly 40% within months, repeating the country's post-2008 pattern of serial currency failure.

**2023–2025 — Argentina and Venezuela's continuing inflation crises.** Argentine annual inflation hit 211% in 2023 before the Milei government's shock devaluation (peso down ~54% in December 2023) and austerity program; Venezuela's bolívar depreciated through 2023–24 even after two redenominations (2018, 2021), with inflation cooling under de facto dollarization before pressures reportedly resumed in 2024–25 — the same non-monotonic pattern flagged in mechanism 1, not a straight-line worsening.

**March–May 2023 — US regional bank failures.** Silicon Valley Bank ($209 billion in assets) and Signature Bank ($110 billion) failed within days of each other in March 2023 — the second- and third-largest bank failures in US history — driven by uninsured deposit runs after unrealized losses on long-duration bonds became public. First Republic Bank ($229 billion) failed weeks later in May 2023. The FDIC invoked a "systemic risk exception" to guarantee uninsured deposits, an implicit admission that 2010-era deposit insurance limits hadn't kept pace with modern digital bank-run speed.

**2022 — Sri Lanka's first-ever sovereign default.** Sri Lanka defaulted on $51 billion in external debt in April 2022 amid fuel and food shortages and mass protests; it reached a $2.9 billion IMF bailout in March 2023, its 17th IMF program since independence.

**2022–2024 — Ghana and Zambia debt restructurings.** Ghana defaulted on most external debt in December 2022 and spent roughly half its government revenue on debt service before restructuring; Zambia defaulted in 2020 and took nearly four years to complete restructuring, finalized in 2024, one of the slowest sovereign workouts under the G20's Common Framework.

**2023 — Pakistan's near-default.** Pakistan avoided default only via a $3 billion IMF standby arrangement in June 2023, followed by a $7 billion Extended Fund Facility in 2024, after foreign reserves fell to barely a month of import cover.

**November 2022 — FTX collapse.** The cryptocurrency exchange FTX, once valued at $32 billion, collapsed within days after revelations that customer funds (roughly $8 billion) had been misused; founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in 2024. Unlike bank deposits, crypto exchange balances carried no FDIC-style insurance — a new, largely unregulated category of continuity risk.

**2021–2023 — Global inflation shock.** US CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% (June 2022), the Eurozone at 10.6% (October 2022), and the UK at 11.1% (October 2022) — the highest readings in four decades across major economies, driven by pandemic stimulus, supply-chain disruption, and the energy shock from Russia's invasion of Ukraine, eroding real savings and wages worldwide even without outright currency collapse.

**Ongoing — developing-world debt-service crisis.** The World Bank's *International Debt Report 2024* found low-income countries' interest payments consumed roughly 6% of export revenue in 2023, the highest since 2000; UNCTAD's *A World of Debt* (2023) separately found 3.3 billion people live in countries spending more on debt-interest payments than on health or education combined — a structural continuity failure distinct from single-event defaults, leaving less fiscal room for the next shock.

### India: the aggregate looks solved, the failures are one tier down

The headline national number has genuinely improved and says less than it appears to. Scheduled commercial banks' gross NPA ratio fell to 2.15% by September 2025, a 12–15 year low, with gross NPA in absolute terms down from ₹5.71 lakh crore (FY23) to ₹4.81 lakh crore (FY24) (RBI *Financial Stability Report*; PIB). Against that improving aggregate, the actual deposit-lock failures of the last decade sit specifically in the cooperative and small-private-bank tier, where capital buffers and supervision are weaker: Punjab and Maharashtra Co-operative (PMC) Bank collapsed in 2019 on a concealed exposure to a single real-estate group (HDIL), locking depositors out under a withdrawal cap and a phased release plan running a decade; Yes Bank required a March 2020 SBI-led ₹10,000 crore rescue after asset-quality concealment; Lakshmi Vilas Bank was placed under a ₹25,000 withdrawal-cap moratorium in November 2020 and merged into DBS. This is the aggregation-masking mechanism from tier 1 in a new form, and inverted from how it usually reads: the national number is not hiding a worse national reality, it is hiding a distinct, weaker sub-system that the national number was never built to isolate. Cooperative-bank-specific NPA and failure-rate series were not found in this pass at the same granularity as the SCB data — a measurement gap that matters, because without it there is no way to state whether the cooperative tier's failure rate is itself rising, falling, or stable while the SCB aggregate improves.

The state's own institutional response to the underlying 2015–18 "twin balance sheet" NPA crisis is the National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited (NARCL, incorporated 2021), a public "bad bank" that buys and works out large stressed-asset pools from banks' books — the closest Indian analogue in this file to FDIC/Glass-Steagall, though it acts on distressed assets after the fact rather than removing the incentive to run in advance, and is too recent for a measured before/after to be written here.

### India: small-scale fraud is a recurring, not historical, mechanism

Saradha Group (2013, West Bengal, Assam, Odisha, Jharkhand, Tripura) mobilized roughly ₹3,500 crore from an estimated 1.7 million investors through unregistered collective-investment schemes promising above-market returns; Rose Valley's parallel scheme is estimated at anywhere from ₹17,000 crore to over ₹40,000 crore depending on source — a greater-than-2x spread this file is not resolving. Combined, the two cases alone account for ₹30,000 crore-plus in losses, and neither is fully resolved a decade-plus later (The Wire, 2023 retrospective). This is mechanism 5 (fraud exploiting a trust or verification gap) recurring at a scale below what a national regulator's routine surveillance catches, and it is ongoing rather than historical — the file finds no evidence the underlying mechanism (informal collective-investment schemes offering above-market returns, verified only after collapse) has been closed.

### India: the retail-market mechanism is a measured wealth transfer, not (yet) a bubble collapse

The user's framing of mechanism 4 — retail participation growing until an inflated market corrects and takes people's money with it — is the right global mechanism, but the current best-evidenced India instance is not a bubble collapse; it is a continuous, already-measured transfer. SEBI's September 2024 study found 93% of individual futures-and-options traders lost money across FY22–FY24, an aggregate retail loss of ₹1.8 lakh crore over three years (₹1.06 lakh crore in FY25 alone), an average loss of ₹1.1 lakh per trader — while proprietary trading desks (₹33,000 crore gains) and foreign portfolio investors (₹28,000 crore gains) profited from the same market over the same period, 96–97% of it via algorithmic trading. The mechanism here is not mispricing that later corrects; it is a continuously running transfer from a slower, less-informed counterparty to a faster, better-resourced one, inside a market structure the regulator itself has now measured and published. Crypto exposure is real and growing — retail investors are roughly 75% of India's crypto-holder base as of 2025, with typical portfolios in the ₹50,000–2 lakh range — but no comparable India-specific loss dataset was found in this pass, so the crypto half of the user's point 3 remains plausible but unquantified here.

### India: credit is growing in aggregate and still failing on allocation

The user's point that smaller business and farmers find credit harder to get, against exploding personal and large-corporate credit, does not survive as a simple statement, though a version of it holds underneath. Official priority-sector lending grew 85% in absolute terms (₹23 lakh crore to ₹42.7 lakh crore, 2019–2024), with agricultural credit up from ₹8.87 to ₹18.28 lakh crore and MSME credit from ₹10.99 to ₹21.74 lakh crore over the same period (PIB/RBI) — the opposite direction from "tougher to get." But the relative gap remains large by an independent measure: IFC/World Bank estimates India's total viable MSME finance demand at roughly $819 billion, of which only $289 billion is met, leaving a $530 billion unmet gap — so both things are true at once, aggregate disbursement growth and a persistently large relative shortfall, which is itself the tier-1 aggregation-masking mechanism (a rising national total says nothing about whether the gap it sits inside is closing). Personal and unsecured credit did explode as described: retail loans grew at a 24.8% CAGR (2021–23), with unsecured lending reaching roughly a third of ₹41 lakh crore in total personal credit by March 2023 — prompting the RBI to raise risk weights on unsecured consumer credit from 100% to 125% in November 2023, a rare instance of a regulator acting ahead of a crisis rather than after one. The "bank money diverted to private equity and private debt, creating opacity" half of the user's point is real but is better evidenced through two specific cases than through the P2P sector he names: IL&FS's September 2018 default (₹91,000 crore group debt, an AAA-rated quasi-sovereign infrastructure financier collapsing within days of its rating, wiping out an estimated ₹8.48 lakh crore in investor wealth across banks, NBFCs, and mutual funds simultaneously) and the April 2020 Franklin Templeton wind-down of six debt mutual-fund schemes, which trapped over ₹25,000 crore of retail money in illiquid, opaque private-credit and lower-rated corporate paper, resolved only via Supreme Court-mandated repayment completed by February 2023. Gold loans — up 71% to ₹1.72 lakh crore by December 2024 — are a further symptom worth adding: a small borrower pledging gold rather than obtaining unsecured formal credit is direct evidence of the same allocation gap, without needing an NPA event to show it. P2P lending is real but small at the scale of the other mechanisms (RBI caps of ₹50 lakh per lender and ₹10 lakh per borrower); the RBI banned default-loss-guarantee arrangements in April 2024 and fined LenDenClub and LiquiLoans roughly ₹2 crore each (August 2024) after finding products marketed as risk-free — the mechanism the user names is correctly identified, just at a smaller scale than IL&FS or Franklin Templeton.

### India: IL&FS as the concentrated instance of the user's fifth point

The user's point 5 — government-linked contracts concentrating in a few listed companies, with a failure there threatening to take the wider economy down — is best evidenced in India not by the case the user was likely picturing (Adani, on the numbers found) but by IL&FS. Disclosed bank exposure to the Adani group is comparatively contained: State Bank of India's exposure was reported at roughly 0.9–1% of its loan book (~₹27,000 crore), and LIC's at ₹6,183 crore, under 1% of assets under management — small enough that the "systemic on disclosed exposure" version of the concern is not supported by this pass, though the separate, unresolved question raised by the 2023 Hindenburg allegations (opacity in offshore-FPI ownership structure) was outside this search's scope and is not settled here either way. IL&FS is the cleaner and stronger case: a quasi-sovereign infrastructure financing and development company (majority-owned by LIC, ORIX Japan, and Abu Dhabi Investment Authority), AAA-rated by domestic agencies days before its September 2018 default, which had used a structure of roughly 300 subsidiary entities to roll over debt and mask stress. The default simultaneously exposed banks, NBFCs (triggering DHFL's collapse in its wake), mutual funds, and pension money, and functions as this file's clearest single instance of a state-adjacent financing vehicle whose failure propagated across the financial system through the exact multi-level channel — debt, contracts, and public listing status all in one entity — that the user's point describes. It is also a strong candidate for a future finance × infrastructure/employment cross-need node, on the same "one policy object, multiple needs" logic as tier 1's farm-power and construction nodes, though confirming that requires the tier-2 employment-livelihood file to be complete first.

**Where a negative control belongs in this file, not a positive one.** The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) is India's orderly-failure mechanism, the analogue to Chapter 11 in the evolution section above, and it is not working as designed: its 330-day statutory resolution limit has risen in practice from 566 days (March 2024) to 688 days (September 2025) — worsening, not improving, per the tier-1 finding already recorded in `../tier1-physiological/00-summary.md`. The overrun's cost falls asymmetrically on creditors and workers, whose recoverable value erodes with time, while benefiting promoters and distressed-asset buyers, whose negotiating leverage grows the longer resolution drags. Recorded here rather than under "Where it worked" for that reason.

### India: stored risk, not yet realised

**Cooperative-bank supervision remains a measurement gap, not a solved problem.** PMC, Yes Bank, and Lakshmi Vilas Bank were each caught only after concealment had gone on for years; the national NPA improvement documented above is a scheduled-commercial-bank number and does not by construction cover the roughly 1,500 urban cooperative banks operating under a lighter supervisory regime, for which no comparable failure-rate series was found in this pass. A future failure in this tier is not precluded by the improving national aggregate — it is simply not visible in it.

**Commercial-landscape pass.** Where a countable, licensable object exists, capital has followed it: NARCL and private asset-reconstruction companies work distressed corporate debt for a fee once a default is public, and litigation/insolvency-resolution funding for large creditors exists in nascent form (the same funders — LegalPay, FightRight — flagged in `02-rule-of-law-property.md` for commercial disputes serve institutional creditors in IBC proceedings, not small depositors or retail fraud victims). Retail fraud victims (Saradha, Rose Valley scale) and small depositors locked out by a cooperative-bank failure have no equivalent market: recovery runs through state-run claims processes or, in the cooperative case, RBI-administered phased withdrawal schedules, with no priced product found serving this population directly — the same instrument-keyed-to-the-wrong-object pattern found throughout tier 1, applied here to financial-fraud victims rather than environmental-harm victims. The retail F&O and crypto markets are the inverse case: a large, well-capitalised commercial sector (brokers, algo-trading desks, crypto exchanges) serves and profits from the same retail population the user's point 3 describes as being harmed, meaning the paying customer and the harmed party are frequently the *same person* — a departure from the "harmed party has no wallet" pattern that dominates tier 1, and closer to a case where the product's structure itself (F&O leverage, 96%+ algo-dominated order flow) produces the harm as a byproduct of serving a real, paying customer rather than through neglect of an unserved one. Gold-loan NBFCs (Muthoot, Manappuram) are a real, profitable, at-scale commercial response to the small-borrower credit gap named in point 4 — but they monetize the borrower's asset rather than closing the underlying access gap to unsecured formal credit, making this an instance of a market serving the adjacent, collateralizable version of the need rather than the need itself.

The MSME credit gap has one clean counter-example where the gap is actually closing rather than growing in aggregate: **TReDS**, the invoice-discounting platform mandated for CPSE MSME payments, cleared ₹1.9 lakh crore cumulative (₹48,000 crore in FY2024–25 alone, up 62% year-on-year) across 80,000+ registered MSMEs and 80+ competing financiers, collateral-free and at market-discovered rates. The state manufactured the instrument (mandating the settlement channel); private capital did the rest — the same "obligation constitutes a market" logic tier 1 found in ZLD and CEMS (`../tier1-physiological/00-summary.md` §3), applied here to receivables rather than emissions. Bandhan Bank (above, under "Where it worked") is the equivalent finding on the lending side: a for-profit actor that reaches the same over-indebted population the 2010 AP microfinance crisis harmed, profitably, at 33 million customers. Between TReDS and Bandhan, this file's most consistent commercial finding is narrower than tier 1's "no entry where payer and beneficiary are the same person" — here they sometimes are, but only where a regulator first built a market structure (TReDS) or forced a lender into prudential regulation (Bandhan) rather than leaving the segment to unregulated NBFC-MFI lending or informal channels.
