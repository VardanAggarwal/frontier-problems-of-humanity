# Financial/Economic Continuity — Tier 2 (Safety)

Tier 2 need: a buffer against unpredictable future threats to resources. Money, savings, and banks are the storage medium for that buffer. This file covers how the medium itself has repeatedly failed — through inflation, bank collapse, default, confiscation, and fraud — and what institutions humanity built in response.

## How the threat has manifested

**1719-1720 — South Sea Bubble / Mississippi Company, Britain & France.** Company stock backed by exaggerated trade monopoly claims and government debt-swap schemes; South Sea stock rose from ~£128 to £1,000 in months, then collapsed, wiping out investors including Isaac Newton. First recorded systemic speculative-fraud collapse with national fiscal fallout; Britain passed the Bubble Act (1720) restricting joint-stock company formation for over a century.

**1923 — Weimar Germany hyperinflation.** Reparations under the Treaty of Versailles plus deficit-financed money printing drove prices up roughly a billionfold between 1922-23; by November 1923 one US dollar equaled 4.2 trillion marks. Wages were paid daily, sometimes twice daily, because money lost value between shifts. Savings, pensions, and fixed-income contracts were wiped out; ended by the Rentenmark currency reform (Nov 1923), backed nominally by land and industrial assets.

**1933 — US gold confiscation, Executive Order 6102.** Franklin D. Roosevelt ordered private holders to sell gold coin, bullion, and certificates to the Federal Reserve at $20.67/oz; the government then revalued gold to $35/oz, effectively expropriating value from citizens who complied. Justified as a Depression-era measure to break the deflationary gold-standard constraint on money supply.

**1930-1933 — US banking panics.** Roughly 9,000 US banks failed (about a third of all banks) as depositors, uninsured, rushed to withdraw amid no lender-of-last-resort safety net; a wave of runs deepened the Great Depression's contraction of the money supply by roughly a third (per Friedman & Schwartz).

**1946 — Hungary hyperinflation, the worst ever recorded.** Prices doubled every 15 hours at peak (July 1946); the pengő was replaced by the forint at an exchange rate of 400,000 quadrillion (4×10^29) pengő to 1 forint. Caused by war destruction, Soviet reparations demands, and unconstrained note issuance.

**1971 — Nixon Shock / end of Bretton Woods gold convertibility.** US suspended dollar-to-gold convertibility, ending the postwar fixed exchange-rate system; ushered in the era of floating fiat currencies and a new class of currency-value risk that had not existed under the gold anchor.

**1976, 1982, 1989, 2001, 2014, 2020, 2023 — Argentina's repeated sovereign defaults.** Argentina has defaulted on external debt at least nine times since independence; the 2001-02 default (~$100 billion, then the largest sovereign default in history) triggered bank deposit freezes ("corralito"), a peso devaluation of over 70%, and mass unemployment.

**1985 — Bolivia hyperinflation.** Annual inflation reached roughly 24,000% (some estimates near 60,000% at the 1984-85 peak), driven by debt crisis and monetary financing of deficits; ended by a 1985 stabilization ("New Economic Policy") pegging the currency and cutting the fiscal deficit.

**1993-1994 — Yugoslavia hyperinflation.** During the breakup of Yugoslavia and international sanctions, monthly inflation hit roughly 313 million percent in January 1994 (prices doubling every ~34 hours); a 500-billion-dinar banknote was issued. Resolved by adopting the "Novi Dinar" pegged to the German mark.

**1997-98 — Asian Financial Crisis.** Currency pegs in Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, and others collapsed under speculative attack and short-term dollar-debt exposure; Thai baht lost over half its value in months, triggering IMF bailout packages tied to austerity conditions across the region.

**2001-2002 — Argentina bank deposit freeze ("corralito").** Government froze bank accounts to stop a run, limiting withdrawals to ~$250/week; savers who had trusted "dollarized" peso deposits saw them forcibly converted to devalued pesos ("corralón"), destroying middle-class savings.

**2007-2009 — Northern Rock collapse and Global Financial Crisis.** Northern Rock suffered the UK's first bank run in over a century (Sept 2007) after losing access to wholesale funding markets; it was nationalized in 2008. Globally, the US subprime mortgage collapse triggered Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy (Sept 2008, $600+ billion in assets, the largest bankruptcy filing in US history) and a near-collapse of the global banking system, requiring the $700 billion TARP bailout and coordinated central bank interventions.

**2008 — Bernard Madoff Ponzi scheme exposed.** Roughly $65 billion in fabricated account balances collapsed when redemption requests during the financial crisis outran new investor inflows; the largest Ponzi fraud in history, sentenced to 150 years.

**2008-2009 — Zimbabwe hyperinflation.** Peak monthly inflation estimated at 79.6 billion percent (Nov 2008); the government issued a 100-trillion-Zimbabwean-dollar banknote. Driven by unconstrained central bank money printing to cover fiscal deficits and land-reform-linked economic collapse; the currency was abandoned in 2009 in favor of the US dollar and other foreign currencies.

**2010-2015 — Greek sovereign debt crisis.** Greece required three EU/IMF bailout packages (2010, 2012, 2015) totaling over €280 billion after revealing understated deficit figures; imposed severe austerity, capital controls (2015 bank holiday and €60/day withdrawal limits), and a ~unemployment rate above 25% at its worst.

**2013 — Cyprus bank bail-in.** As part of a €10 billion EU/IMF bailout, uninsured deposits above €100,000 at Bank of Cyprus and Laiki Bank were seized/converted to equity (a "bail-in") — the first time in the Eurozone crisis that depositors, not just taxpayers, directly absorbed bank losses; roughly 47.5% of large deposits at Bank of Cyprus were converted.

**2016 — India demonetization.** Overnight, the government invalidated 500 and 1,000 rupee notes (86% of currency in circulation by value) to fight black money and counterfeiting; caused severe cash-liquidity shock, GDP growth slowdown, and hardship for the ~90% of transactions that were cash-based.

**2016-present — Venezuela hyperinflation.** Annual inflation exceeded 1,000,000% by 2018 per IMF estimates; driven by oil-revenue collapse, currency controls, and unconstrained bolívar issuance. Triggered mass emigration (over 7 million people by the 2020s) and repeated currency redenominations (removing zeros in 2018 and 2021).

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

**1694 — Bank of England founded.** Chartered to fund government war debt, it became the founding model of a modern central bank — a public-private hybrid with money-issuance authority — that later central banks worldwide were built on.

**1866 — Bagehot's "lender of last resort" doctrine.** Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* articulated the principle that a central bank should lend freely against good collateral at a penalty rate during panics, to stop runs without rewarding recklessness — still the operating doctrine for crisis response.

**1907 — Panic of 1907 and its aftermath.** A liquidity crisis resolved only by private intervention (J.P. Morgan personally organizing bank support) exposed the US's lack of a lender of last resort, directly motivating the creation of the **Federal Reserve System in 1913**.

**1933 — Glass-Steagall Act and FDIC.** Passed directly in response to the 1930-33 bank failures: Glass-Steagall separated commercial from investment banking to reduce speculative risk-taking with deposits, and the **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (1933)** insured deposits up to a set limit, ending the incentive to run on a bank at the first rumor of trouble. Deposit insurance is the direct institutional answer to bank runs.

**1944 — Bretton Woods system and founding of the IMF and World Bank.** Created a fixed exchange-rate order pegged to gold-backed dollars, plus the **International Monetary Fund** as an international lender of last resort for countries facing balance-of-payments crises — the institution repeatedly invoked in Asian (1997), Greek (2010), and Argentine crises above.

**1980s-2000s — Basel Accords (Basel I 1988, II 2004, III 2010-11).** International bank capital-adequacy standards set minimum capital reserves banks must hold against risk-weighted assets, designed to prevent the kind of undercapitalized leverage that caused the 2008 crisis.

**1970s-1990s — Inflation targeting and independent central banks.** After the 1970s stagflation and the hyperinflation record above, central banks (starting with New Zealand in 1990) adopted explicit inflation targets and legal independence from fiscal authorities, to remove the temptation to print money for short-term political gain that drove Weimar, Hungary, Yugoslavia, and Zimbabwe.

**Currency boards and dollarization as commitment devices.** Countries with weak monetary credibility (Ecuador 2000, Zimbabwe 2009, Argentina's 1991-2001 convertibility peg) adopted currency boards or outright dollarization — surrendering domestic money-printing power entirely — as a hard commitment mechanism against hyperinflation, trading monetary sovereignty for credibility.

**Bankruptcy law as orderly-failure mechanism.** Modern bankruptcy codes (US Chapter 11 reorganization, sovereign debt restructuring frameworks used in Greece 2012 and Argentina's various defaults) let insolvent firms and states restructure debt in a legally ordered process rather than through chaotic default, preserving some continuity of function and creditor recovery instead of total collapse.

**Post-2008 reforms — Dodd-Frank Act (2010), stress testing, resolution regimes.** Created "living wills" for systemically important banks, stress tests, and orderly-resolution authority so a failing bank could be wound down without a repeat of the uncontrolled 2008 contagion — an attempt to make bank failure survivable rather than eliminate it.

## Where this fails today

**2019-2026 — Lebanon's ongoing currency and banking collapse.** The lira has lost over 98% of its value since 2019; the central bank's official peg finally gave way to market rates in 2023. Banks have frozen depositors out of their own dollar accounts since 2019, forcing withdrawals through a devalued "lollar" system; the World Bank has called it one of the top three most severe economic crises globally since the mid-1800s. No comprehensive bank restructuring law had passed as of 2025.

**2023-2026 — Sudan's war-driven currency collapse.** Civil war since April 2023 has destroyed the banking system in Khartoum and other cities; the Sudanese pound has depreciated sharply on parallel markets and inflation has repeatedly run above 100% year-on-year, per IMF regional estimates, compounding a humanitarian crisis with millions displaced.

**2024 — Zimbabwe's sixth currency in 15 years.** After the RTGS dollar/Zimbabwe dollar lost most of its value again (2023 annual inflation near 175%), the government launched the gold-backed "ZiG" (Zimbabwe Gold) in April 2024. The ZiG itself devalued roughly 40% within months, repeating the country's post-2008 pattern of serial currency failure.

**2023 — Argentina and Venezuela's continuing inflation crises.** Argentine annual inflation hit 211% in 2023 before the Milei government's shock devaluation (peso down ~54% in December 2023) and austerity program; Venezuela's bolívar kept depreciating through 2023-24 even after two redenominations (2018, 2021), with IMF-tracked inflation still running well above 100% annually.

**March-May 2023 — US regional bank failures.** Silicon Valley Bank ($209 billion in assets) and Signature Bank ($110 billion) failed within days of each other in March 2023 — the second- and third-largest bank failures in US history — driven by uninsured deposit runs after unrealized losses on long-duration bonds became public. First Republic Bank ($229 billion) failed weeks later in May 2023. The FDIC invoked a "systemic risk exception" to guarantee uninsured deposits, an implicit admission that 2010-era deposit insurance limits hadn't kept pace with modern digital bank-run speed.

**2022 — Sri Lanka's first-ever sovereign default.** Sri Lanka defaulted on $51 billion in external debt in April 2022 amid fuel and food shortages and mass protests; it reached a $2.9 billion IMF bailout in March 2023, its 17th IMF program since independence.

**2022-2024 — Ghana and Zambia debt restructurings.** Ghana defaulted on most external debt in December 2022 and spent roughly half its government revenue on debt service before restructuring; Zambia defaulted in 2020 and took nearly four years to complete restructuring, finalized in 2024, one of the slowest sovereign workouts under the G20's Common Framework.

**2023 — Pakistan's near-default.** Pakistan avoided default only via a $3 billion IMF standby arrangement in June 2023, followed by a $7 billion Extended Fund Facility in 2024, after foreign reserves fell to barely a month of import cover.

**November 2022 — FTX collapse.** The cryptocurrency exchange FTX, once valued at $32 billion, collapsed within days after revelations that customer funds (roughly $8 billion) had been misused; founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in 2024. Unlike bank deposits, crypto exchange balances carried no FDIC-style insurance — a new, largely unregulated category of continuity risk.

**2021-2023 — Global inflation shock.** US CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% (June 2022), the Eurozone at 10.6% (October 2022), and the UK at 11.1% (October 2022) — the highest readings in four decades across major economies, driven by pandemic stimulus, supply-chain disruption, and the energy shock from Russia's invasion of Ukraine, eroding real savings and wages worldwide even without outright currency collapse.

**Ongoing — developing-world debt-service crisis.** The World Bank and UN have repeatedly flagged that a large share of low-income and African countries now spend more on external debt service than on health or education combined, a structural continuity failure distinct from single-event defaults, leaving less fiscal room for the next shock.
