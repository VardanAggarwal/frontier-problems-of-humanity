# Rule of Law & Property/Contract Rights (Tier 2 — Safety)

Tier definition: a buffer against unpredictable future threats to claims and resources. Rule of law converts "what I hold, I might lose to arbitrary power" into a predictable, bounded, appealable process. When it fails, ownership and agreements become contingent on who holds power, not on codified right.

## How this need has been threatened

**1. Absolutist monarchies (16th–18th c.).** Under droit du roi in Bourbon France, the crown could seize property, imprison subjects by lettre de cachet without trial, and override parlements. Louis XIV's 1673 edict required judicial rulings to be registered by royal decree before taking effect — courts had no independent finality. Property and liberty existed at the sovereign's pleasure, not as enforceable claims.

**2. Aryanization of Jewish property, Nazi Germany (1933–1939).** Starting with the April 1933 boycott and accelerating through the 1938 "Decree on the Utilization of Jewish Property," Jewish-owned businesses were forcibly transferred to non-Jewish owners at below-market prices, backed by law that legalized the theft rather than restraining it. By 1939 an estimated 100,000+ Jewish-owned firms had been Aryanized. This is the paradigm case of law itself becoming the instrument of expropriation rather than its check.

**3. Weimar Article 48 emergency powers (1930–1933).** Article 48 of the Weimar constitution let the president rule by decree in an "emergency." Hindenburg invoked it over 250 times from 1930–1932; Hitler's February 1933 Reichstag Fire Decree used it to suspend habeas corpus, press freedom, and property protections entirely, converting a constitutional safeguard into the legal path to dictatorship.

**4. Soviet collectivization (1929–1933).** Forced collectivization stripped an estimated 25 million peasant households of land, livestock, and tools without compensation or legal recourse; "kulaks" (a legally undefined, arbitrarily applied label) were deported en masse — roughly 1.8 million by 1932. Resistance in Ukraine contributed to the Holodomor famine (1932–33), killing an estimated 3.9 million. Property rights were abolished by decree, not adjudicated away.

**5. Stalinist show trials (1936–1938).** The Moscow Trials convicted Bukharin, Zinoviev, Kamenev and others on fabricated charges via confessions extracted under torture, with verdicts predetermined by the NKVD and Politburo before proceedings began. An estimated 680,000+ people were executed during the Great Terror (1937–38) under legal forms that retained courts, judges, and verdicts while eliminating any independent fact-finding.

**6. Cultural Revolution, China (1966–1976).** Mao's mobilization of Red Guards suspended ordinary courts and legal process; property was seized and people were "tried" in mass struggle sessions with no defense, appeal, or codified charge. The Ministry of Public Security's 1967 directives explicitly subordinated police and courts to revolutionary committees. Estimates of deaths range from 500,000 to 2 million, alongside the near-total collapse of civil and criminal legal process for a decade.

**7. Colonial dual legal systems — French Algeria (1865–1962) and British India.** The 1865 Sénatus-consulte in Algeria created separate legal statuses for "French subjects" (indigènes) and citizens, denying Muslim Algerians full property and civil rights under French civil law while subjecting them to the discriminatory "Code de l'indigénat" (1881), which allowed summary punishment without ordinary judicial process. Colonial land ordinances (e.g., 1873 Warnier Law) facilitated transfer of communal Algerian land to European settlers via legal mechanisms designed for that purpose.

**8. Amin's expulsion of Uganda's Asians (1972).** Idi Amin's decree gave Uganda's ~50,000 Asian residents 90 days to leave, with businesses and property confiscated and redistributed by state fiat, no judicial hearing, and no compensation — a modern peacetime mass expropriation executed entirely by executive decree.

**9. Post-Soviet oligarch state capture, Russia (1990s).** The 1995–96 "loans-for-shares" scheme transferred majority stakes in Yukos, Norilsk Nickel, and other state firms to insiders at a fraction of market value through rigged auctions, with courts and regulators captured by the same actors benefiting from the transfers. The World Bank and academic literature (Hellman, 1998, "Winners Take All") coined "state capture" for this pattern: private actors purchasing the rule-making and enforcement functions of the state itself.

**10. Zimbabwe fast-track land reform (2000–2001 onward).** Under the Fast Track Land Reform Programme, roughly 4,000 white-owned commercial farms were seized, often violently, with the 2000 constitutional referendum and subsequent Land Acquisition Act amendments explicitly removing courts' power to review compensation or block seizures. Agricultural output collapsed by an estimated 50% within years; the 2000s hyperinflation (peaking near 89.7 sextillion percent, Nov. 2008) followed the broader property/institutional collapse.

**11. Chinese Cultural Revolution redux via modern eminent domain abuse — but more illustrative: Democratic Republic of Congo and Mobutu-era "Zaireanization" (1973–1974).** Mobutu nationalized thousands of foreign (mostly Belgian, Greek, Portuguese) owned businesses and redistributed them to political allies ("acquéreurs") without compensation, collapsing commercial confidence and productivity; many acquired firms failed within two years for lack of technical capacity.

**12. Contract enforcement breakdown in state collapse — Somalia (1991–2000s) and Lebanon (2019–present).** With the Somali state's collapse in 1991, there was no court system to enforce contracts, forcing reliance on clan-based xeer customary law and private security instead of formal legal recourse. Lebanon's 2019 financial collapse saw banks unilaterally impose informal capital controls in defiance of depositors' contractual withdrawal rights, with courts unable or unwilling to enforce judgments against banks — a live case of contract rights nullified by systemic breakdown rather than by decree.

**13. French Revolution's abolition of feudal law (August 4–11, 1789).** The National Assembly's August Decrees abolished the entire feudal property order — seigneurial dues, tithes, venal offices — overnight, without compensation in most cases, illustrating that legal rupture threatens settled property claims even when done in the name of liberty; nobles who had held legally sanctioned rights for centuries lost them by decree with no due process, feeding the Vendée counter-revolution (1793).

**14. Argentine "corralito" and emergency decrees (2001–2002).** Facing bank collapse, the government froze bank deposits by decree (Dec. 2001) and later forcibly converted dollar deposits to pesos at below-market rates ("corralón," Feb. 2002), unilaterally rewriting private contracts between depositors and banks — an instance of a democratic, ostensibly rule-of-law state using emergency decree power to override contractual property claims.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **Magna Carta (1215).** Clause 39 ("No free man shall be... imprisoned... except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land") is the foundational precedent that even the sovereign is bound by law, establishing due process as a check on arbitrary seizure of person and property.

- **Habeas corpus development (Habeas Corpus Act, 1679, England).** Codified the right to challenge unlawful detention before a court, closing the gap that let monarchs imprison without trial.

- **Separation of powers and constitutionalism (Montesquieu, 1748; U.S. Constitution, 1787; French Declaration of Rights of Man, Article 17, 1789).** Dividing legislative, executive, and judicial power — and enshrining property as a right violable only "when public necessity... legally requires" with prior compensation — installed structural checks against the abuses of the ancien régime it followed.

- **Napoleonic Code (1804).** Codified uniform civil and property law across France, replacing a patchwork of feudal, customary, and Roman-derived rules with predictable, written statute applied equally — the template later adopted or adapted by dozens of civil-law countries.

- **Independent judiciary norms (Act of Settlement, 1701, England; entrenched globally through 19th–20th c. constitutions).** Security of judicial tenure, removable only by impeachment, insulated courts from executive retaliation for unfavorable rulings.

- **Torrens title system (South Australia, 1858).** Robert Torrens's state-guaranteed land registration replaced fragile chains of private deeds with a single indefeasible government-backed title, eliminating a major source of property disputes and fraud; adopted across Australia, much of Canada, parts of the U.S., and internationally.

- **International investment law and bilateral investment treaties (first BIT: Germany–Pakistan, 1959; ICSID Convention, 1965).** Created enforceable, depoliticized arbitration for cross-border property and contract disputes, protecting foreign investors from expropriation without recourse to (often captured) domestic courts.

- **Post-communist property restitution, Eastern Europe (1990s).** Czechoslovakia's 1991 Restitution Act, Germany's 1990 reunification treaty provisions, and similar laws in Hungary, Poland, and the Baltics returned or compensated for property nationalized under communism, using documented claims processes rather than renewed arbitrary seizure to correct the prior expropriation.

- **Truth and reconciliation commissions with property/reparations components (South Africa's TRC, 1995–2002; Chile's Rettig Commission, 1990).** Institutionalized fact-finding and, in some cases, restitution or compensation for property and rights lost under authoritarian rule, without full-scale revolutionary rupture.

- **Anti-corruption and state-capture institutions (Hong Kong ICAC, 1974; UN Convention Against Corruption, 2003; World Bank/EBRD state-capture indices post-1998).** Built specialized, independently resourced bodies and international norms to target the corruption of enforcement itself — the specific failure mode of oligarchic capture.

- **Modern eminent domain compensation doctrine (U.S. Fifth Amendment "Takings Clause," 1791; expanded case law through 20th c.).** Formalized that government seizure of property must be for public use and with "just compensation," embedding due process into the one legitimate channel for state expropriation.

### Where it worked

- **Georgia's property-registration reform (2004–2007).** Post-Rose Revolution reforms replaced a corrupt, multi-agency land-registration system with a single self-financing National Agency of Public Registry; property registration fell to roughly 10 minutes, and Georgia rose from 132nd to 18th (or, per a second World Bank citation of the same period, 137th to 11th — the two figures are not reconciled here) on the *Doing Business* ease-of-registration ranking between 2004 and 2007, the fastest-reforming country in the index for that period.
- **South Korea's land reform (1950).** Tenancy among farm households fell from 49% to 7%, and cultivated land under tenancy from 65% to 18%, within six years (1950–1956); owner-cultivated households rose sixfold, from 349,000 (1949) to 1,812,000 (1950). One of the few land reforms executed fast enough and completely enough to leave no landlord class to re-capture the gain — the same instrument Zimbabwe's 2000 fast-track reform used to opposite effect, above.
- **Taiwan's rent-reduction and land-transfer program (1949–1953).** Tenant rents, previously 50–70% of crop yield, were capped at 37.5% by the 1949 Act; over the following four years 143,568 hectares transferred from 106,049 landlords to 194,823 tenant-purchasers, and brown-rice production rose roughly 47% (1948–1952) — reform stabilized rather than depressed output, unlike the Zimbabwe and Soviet-collectivization cases above.
- **A limited positive control, not a clean one: Peru's urban land-titling program (COFOPRI, from 1996).** 1.2 million titles were issued to informal urban landholders in the first four years, but the central hypothesis behind the program — that formal title would unlock formal credit — did not hold: of 200,313 Lima households titled in 1998–99, only ~24% had obtained any financing by 2002, and financing from private banks specifically was "almost nil" (title alone did not substitute for the collateral and income documentation banks required). Worth recording for the same reason Argentina's Convertibility Plan is recorded in `03-financial-economic-continuity.md`: a real, measured institutional change that solved the problem it was built for (documented tenure) while leaving the problem it was sold on (credit access) unsolved.
- **India: RERA (Real Estate Regulation Act, 2016).** Project delays in major metros fell roughly 40% since enforcement (Anarock, 2024); the mandated 60-day complaint-disposal window replaced consumer-court timelines that previously ran years, and by September 2025 state RERA authorities had registered 1.51 lakh projects and disposed of 1.47 lakh complaints. India's clearest positive control in this file, parallel to the LWE-decline entry already in `01-violence-protection.md`.

## Where this fails today

**Global rule-of-law decline is now measured, not anecdotal.** V-Dem's 2025 Democracy Report found 72% of the world's population now lives under autocratizing or fully autocratic regimes, with liberal democracy's global average back to 1998 levels — an 18-year rollback. Freedom House's *Freedom in the World 2025* recorded the 19th consecutive year of net global decline in political rights and civil liberties, with countries declining outnumbering those improving for the fifth straight year.

**Judicial independence is under direct political attack in multiple states.** Poland's 2017–2023 "reforms" under the Law and Justice party created a politically controlled Disciplinary Chamber that suspended judges for rulings displeasing the executive; the European Court of Justice and European Commission both found this violated EU judicial-independence law, and unwinding it remains unfinished under the Tusk government elected in late 2023. Hungary's Orbán government has packed the Constitutional Court and National Judicial Office since 2011–2012, with the EU's 2022 rule-of-law conditionality mechanism freezing roughly €22 billion in funds over the issue. Israel's 2023 "judicial overhaul" — curbing the Supreme Court's "reasonableness" review, passed July 2023 — triggered the largest sustained protest movement in the country's history and was itself struck down by the Court in January 2024, an ongoing constitutional crisis. El Salvador's Bukele-aligned legislature removed and replaced the entire Constitutional Chamber and Attorney General in a single May 2021 vote, enabling the unconstitutional consecutive-term ruling that let Bukele seek reelection in 2024.

**Land rights insecurity continues at scale.** The World Bank estimates roughly 70% of the world's population lacks legally documented rights to their land. Deforestation-linked land grabbing in the Brazilian Amazon accelerated under weakened enforcement in 2019–2022, with illegal mining and logging displacing indigenous communities (INPE deforestation data shows record 2021–2022 clearing); across Sub-Saharan Africa, large-scale land acquisitions for agribusiness and mining continue to displace smallholders with customary but undocumented tenure, per Land Matrix's ongoing tracking (over 700 concluded large-scale deals globally as of 2024).

**Legal systems have collapsed outright in active conflict and post-conflict states.** Afghanistan's post-August 2021 Taliban takeover dissolved the constitutional legal system, replacing courts and property/contract law with unaccountable Taliban edicts and gender-based exclusion from legal standing. In Gaza, UN OCHA and satellite assessments through 2024–2025 documented the destruction of an estimated 60%+ of buildings with no compensation mechanism or functioning court to adjudicate claims. Ukraine has lost documented title and use of an estimated fifth of its territory since Russia's 2022 invasion, with occupied-zone property simply reassigned by occupying authorities.

**States are seizing private assets by decree rather than adjudication.** Russia's 2022–2023 "external administration" decrees (Presidential Decree No. 302, April 2023) transferred control of Western firms' Russian subsidiaries — including Danone's and Carlsberg's local units — to state-appointed managers without court process, while dozens of Western companies (Renault, McDonald's) exited under forced-sale terms far below asset value, reprising loans-for-shares-era expropriation with a geopolitical rationale instead of an economic one.

### India: forest rights claims stall while diversion accelerates

The Forest Rights Act (2006) was built to convert de facto tribal occupation of forest land into documented title — Individual Forest Rights (IFR) and Community Forest Resource (CFR) rights — reversing a century of colonial-forest-law dispossession. Implementation has not kept pace with either the claim pool or the counter-flow of diversion. MoTA's own Monthly Progress Reports put national claims received at roughly 63 lakh, with ~45 lakh titled — but rejection-rate figures disagree by close to 2x across report vintages (one March 2022 cut implies ~21% rejected; a differently-scoped aggregate implies ~46%), a gap this file is not resolving rather than picking a side of. State-level variance is sharper than any national average shows: a five-state field study (Call for Justice, 2024) found IFR approval rates ranging from 72% in Odisha to just 5% in Karnataka, with Karnataka also under-allocating land even on approved claims (avg. 0.8 acres granted). CFR is worse in absolute terms: CFR-LA's decadal review — on its own "crude," methodologically bounded estimate — found only 3% of minimum potential CFR area recognized in the Act's first decade, against a potential universe of roughly 60,000 villages and 6.26 crore people (ATREE).

Running against this stalled claims process is an accelerating diversion of forest land to non-forest use under the Forest (Conservation) Act: government's own figures show diversion rising ~29% in 2019–24 against the preceding five years (to 96,113 ha), with mining, hydropower/irrigation and roads the largest categories; a differently-scoped Down To Earth tally puts the 12-year total at 2,15,943 ha against the government's 10-year 1,73,984 ha — again flagged as a discrepancy, not reconciled. The Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act (2023) narrowed the statutory definition of "forest" and exempted categories of strategic/linear infrastructure from clearance requirements — critics read this as permitting diversion of "deemed forest" without the gram sabha consent FRA otherwise requires. MoTA, the ministry that administers FRA, formally objected during the amendment's passage; the objection was overridden. This is the tier's instrument-keyed-to-the-wrong-object mechanism in a new form: the remedy (title recognition) and the counter-instrument (diversion clearance) run on separate, uncoordinated tracks inside the same government, with the diversion track moving faster.

Hasdeo Aranya, Chhattisgarh, is the documented case of that gap collapsing into procedural fraud rather than mere neglect: the state's own Scheduled Tribes Commission, probing a 2021 complaint, found in 2024 that gram sabha consent for coal mining (adjacent to the Adani-operated Parsa block) had been forged — signatures of deceased people included, a mining clause inserted into meeting minutes after the meeting had concluded, and sarpanches coerced to sign — a potential SC/ST Atrocities Act offense, not merely an administrative lapse. (Cross-referenced in `01-violence-protection.md`, where the same finding is cited for its violence dimension; this file carries the property-rights dimension of the same event.)

### India: land acquisition law diluted by the states that adopted it

The Land Acquisition Act (1894) had no consent requirement and no rehabilitation obligation behind its "public purpose" clause — the same absolutist-instrument pattern as the historical cases above, minus the monarchy. The 2013 replacement (LARR) added exactly those two things: a Social Impact Assessment before acquisition, and 70–80% consent thresholds depending on project type. What followed is a state-by-state unwinding of both, via the same federal mechanism (Article 254(2) presidential assent) that is supposed to let states adapt central law to local conditions: Gujarat, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh exempted national security, defence, rural infrastructure, affordable housing and industrial-corridor projects from both SIA and consent outright; Jharkhand cut the gram sabha consent quorum from half to one-third; Telangana, Uttar Pradesh and Andhra Pradesh cut the SIA public-hearing notice period from three weeks to one. A central 2015 attempt to enact the same exemptions nationally was repromulgated twice by ordinance and then allowed to lapse in the Rajya Sabha under sustained opposition — the state route succeeded where the central route failed, which is itself a finding about where the blocking coalition is strong and where it isn't. A Supreme Court PIL challenging the state amendments has had notices issued to five states since December 2018, with no resolution as of this writing.

Ground-level resistance to acquisition under both the old and new regimes is not anecdotal at this point. Nandigram (West Bengal, 2007) killed at least 14 in police firing over a proposed 4,000-acre SEZ; Bhatta Parsaul (Uttar Pradesh, 2011) killed 4 (2 villagers, 2 police) over land acquired at roughly ₹850/sq m for the Yamuna Expressway and resold near ₹10,000/sq m — a price gap that fed directly into LARR's drafting; the Niyamgiri case (Odisha, 2013) is the counter-instance where the law worked as designed, with the Supreme Court holding all twelve affected gram sabhas competent to decide and all twelve rejecting Vedanta's bauxite mining, after which the environment ministry withdrew clearance; POSCO's proposed steel plant near Paradip (Odisha) was abandoned in 2017 after sustained local resistance that included a bomb blast killing 3; Amaravati farmers in Andhra Pradesh sustained a 1,631-day protest (Dec 2019–June 2024) after a change of state government reversed the capital-city land-pooling plan under which 33,000 acres had already been pooled. Land Conflict Watch's ongoing tracker puts the current aggregate at roughly 1,000+ live land conflicts, 10–14 million people affected, and ₹3.45 trillion in stalled investment — the two figures in that range (1,084 vs. 921 conflicts; 14.1M vs. 10.5M people) come from the same tracker at different snapshot dates and are not reconciled here.

The "public purpose" clause has also been the specific point of documented abuse: the Supreme Court in 2025 held it fraud on the eminent-domain power for land acquired for a stated public purpose (a grain market, Narela, Delhi) to be privately reverted to the original owner by agreement, and the broader SEZ-era pattern — land acquired under public-purpose justification and routed to private commercial developers — is the most-cited version of the same mechanism. On the commercial side of documentation rather than dispute: DILRMP (government-funded) reports 98.5% of rural land records digitized nationally as of 2024, and SVAMITVA issues drone-mapped title to village households — both state-funded services with no market-priced customer, and no commercial land-conflict-risk-assessment sector was found serving affected communities directly; developer-side land due-diligence exists but sizing data was not found in this pass.

### India: judicial process integrity — case-level critique against a null systemic finding

Three specific cases anchor a broader public perception of judicial bias, and each survives scrutiny differently. The Supreme Court's unanimous 2019 Ayodhya title ruling (*M Siddiq v Mahant Suresh Das*) awarded the disputed site for a Ram temple on possessory and ASI-excavation grounds while explicitly finding the December 1949 placement of idols inside the mosque "amounted to violation of law" — a finding of illegality attached to no punishment or reversal. Legal scholars, not only activists, have pressed on the resulting contradiction: Gautam Bhatia argues the Court prioritized "peaceful resolution" over resolving whose claim descended from an unlawful act; Faizan Mustafa (then Vice-Chancellor, NALSAR) called parts of the reasoning "laughable," specifically the finding of no evidence of Muslim worship 1528–1857, and characterized the outcome as applying different evidentiary standards to each side — while also noting the ruling contained real concessions to the losing side (compensatory land, no precedent extended to other disputed-site claims). This is a documented, credentialed critique of judicial reasoning, not a settled finding of bias; the judgment's own defenders read the same record as a possessory ruling properly separated from the unresolved criminal question.

The Ram Mandir Trust's financial-irregularity allegations (June 2026) are the least resolved of the three: missing donation-box counts, deleted CCTV footage from the cash-sorting room, an FIR filed June 25 for misappropriation, and the Trust's General Secretary and a trustee resigning "taking moral responsibility" — alongside a separate, contested allegation of a land deal reselling at more than 9x its purchase price within minutes, which the Trust denies. A state SIT probe is active; there is no court finding yet in either direction, and this file is not treating the allegations as established.

The lenient-sentencing pattern is real but partially self-correcting on the record: the Pune Porsche case (2024) saw a Juvenile Justice Board initially set bail conditions including a 300-word road-safety essay for a drunk minor driver who killed two people, triggering public backlash after video surfaced of the family celebrating; the Bombay High Court subsequently ordered continued detention and two JJB members were removed for procedural lapses. The Salman Khan hit-and-run case (2002 incident) shows the opposite trajectory without correction: a 2015 sessions-court conviction (5 years) was reversed by the Bombay High Court the same year on chain-of-custody grounds, and the state's appeal against acquittal has been pending in the Supreme Court since 2016 — nine years without resolution either way.

Set against these three cases, the file's own numbers-hygiene standard requires holding a finding that complicates them: Novosad et al.'s study of over 5 million criminal cases (2010–2018) found no statistically significant in-group bias by judges' own religion or gender at the verdict level. That does not clear the judiciary of the disparities already documented in `01-violence-protection.md` — Muslim undertrials at 17.4–17.7% against a 14.2% population share, SC/ST undertrials near 32%, a 76%-lower-caste/minority skew on death row (NLU Delhi, 2016) — it relocates where in the pipeline the disparity most plausibly forms: policing, charging, and bail-setting upstream of the judge, rather than the verdict itself. Compositional and structural evidence sits alongside this: only 3% of High Court judges are SC/ST and 14% are women (India Justice Report, 2025); the National Judicial Appointments Commission, which would have added non-judicial oversight to appointments, was struck down 4:1 by the Supreme Court itself in 2015, restoring the self-selecting collegium system; and CJI Ranjan Gogoi's retirement (Nov 17, 2019, eight days after delivering the Ayodhya verdict) was followed roughly four months later by a presidential Rajya Sabha nomination he says was not a quid pro quo — an undisputed timeline attached to a contested inference, held here as exactly that rather than resolved either way.

### India: delay as a cost asymmetry, independent of verdict bias

A separate mechanism from case-level bias, and better evidenced: whoever can afford to wait tends to win, regardless of the merits. Government — union and state combined — is 46–50% of all pending cases nationally (Law Commission's 230th Report, 2009; Department of Justice reply to Lok Sabha, Dec 2021), rising to an estimated 73% of admitted cases at the Supreme Court specifically (via DoJ data). Daksh's land-acquisition litigation study found the asymmetry is not just volume: government-party appeals took **1,376 days** to dispose against **555 days** for private-party appeals — roughly 2.5x. Two readings compete for why, and the sources don't resolve which: risk-aversion (an official who settles risks audit or vigilance action; one who appeals, however weak the case, risks nothing) versus deliberate delay-as-strategy (outlasting a claimant who cannot sustain years of litigation). One data point cuts against a pure delay-benefits-government reading: Haryana pays farmers a premium specifically for *not* suing over acquisition compensation — the state itself treating litigation avoidance as cheaper than fighting a claim, which only makes sense if delay is costly to the government too, not free.

The cost asymmetry by claimant income is direct and quantified: litigants earning under ₹1 lakh/year spend roughly 10% of income on litigation costs against 3–5% for the ₹5–10 lakh bracket (Daksh). India ranked 163rd of 190 countries on the World Bank's Enforcing Contracts measure (*Doing Business 2020*, its last edition), improved from 186th in 2015 but still near the bottom globally. Estimates of the aggregate economic cost of judicial delay disagree sharply by source and scope and are not reconciled here: Daksh's 2016 survey put it at 0.5% of GDP conservatively, 1.5–2% including litigant travel and wage loss; a separate 2025 Daksh estimate puts ₹24.7 lakh crore (7.5% of GDP) tied up in commercial-tribunal backlogs alone — a different, narrower slice, not a like-for-like comparison with the earlier figure. Insolvency resolution shows the same mechanism outside land and criminal courts entirely: the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code sets a 330-day statutory limit for corporate resolution; actual average time has risen from 566 days (Mar 2024) to 688 days (Sept 2025) — worsening, not improving — with sources agreeing the overrun favours promoters and distressed-asset buyers, whose leverage grows as asset value erodes, over creditors and workers, who cannot wait it out.

Two gaps are worth naming rather than filling with a proxy. First, Galanter's "why the haves come out ahead" repeat-player framework — the classic account of why routine litigants (government, large corporates) out-perform one-shot individual litigants structurally, independent of case merit — has never been empirically tested against Indian court data specifically; the government-appeal-duration gap above is the closest available proxy, not a direct test. Second, no dataset quantifies claimant cost-to-income specifically for land-acquisition cases, or documents instances of tribal or farmer claimants withdrawing or settling under financial duress during litigation — plausible given the income-based cost-share data above, but not yet measured.

**Commercial-landscape pass.** Across all four India sections above, the paying-customer asymmetry is the same one found throughout tier 1: on forest and land rights, the party seeking to acquire (mining, infrastructure, industrial developers) pays the state for clearance — a real, well-capitalised market — while the party whose rights are at stake (forest-dwelling claimants, displaced farmers) has no mechanism to pay for title-processing or legal support, leaving that side entirely donor/NGO-funded (Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy, CFR-LA, Land Conflict Watch, ARCH). Land-record digitization (DILRMP, SVAMITVA) is real capital deployment but state-funded, not a priced market. A litigation-funding industry does exist in India (LegalPay, FightRight, LegalFund, active since ~2019, legitimacy affirmed by a 2022 Delhi High Court ruling) — but it serves commercial arbitration and insolvency claimants exclusively; no funding product was found serving individual or farmer litigants facing exactly the delay costs documented above, the same instrument-keyed-to-the-wrong-object pattern the file's other sections carry. Legal aid on the individual side is state or NGO-provided (NALSA) rather than market-priced. This reproduces the tier-1 finding that mandates constitute markets only where responsible parties are countable and inspectable (`../tier1-physiological/00-summary.md`) — land acquisition and forest diversion have that structure (a finite set of developers and mining leases), which is why a due-diligence market exists there in nascent form; delay itself has no countable object to attach a compliance industry to, which is consistent with the funding market serving only litigants large enough to be worth underwriting individually.
