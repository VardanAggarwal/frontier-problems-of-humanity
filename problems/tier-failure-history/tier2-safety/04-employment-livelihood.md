# Employment / Livelihood Security (Tier 2 — Safety)

Tier definition: a buffer against unpredictable future threats to resources. Livelihood is the main channel through which people convert labor into resources. When that channel breaks — suddenly, structurally, or by force — the safety buffer collapses even if food and shelter aren't yet gone.

## How the threat has shown up

- **1811–1816, England — Luddite risings.** Textile workers smashed stocking- and lace-frames as mechanized looms cut skilled weavers' wages and threw hand-loom workers out of work; Parliament made frame-breaking a capital offense in 1812. First large-scale organized backlash against technological displacement (E.P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class*, 1963).
- **1930s, US Great Plains — Dust Bowl.** Drought plus over-plowed topsoil destroyed farm livelihoods across Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, and Colorado; roughly 2.5 million people left the Plains states in the 1930s, with ~200,000 migrating to California (Worster, *Dust Bowl*, 1979; US Census migration data). Agricultural livelihood collapse compounded by ecological failure.
- **1929–1939, global — Great Depression.** US unemployment peaked near 25% in 1933 (Bureau of Labor Statistics historical series); Germany hit ~30% by 1932. Mass unemployment on this scale had no existing institutional buffer, feeding political radicalization.
- **1947 (partition) and ongoing 19th–20th c. — indentured servitude.** Britain shipped over 1.3 million Indian indentured laborers to Caribbean, Mauritius, Fiji, and South Africa between 1834 and 1917 after slavery's abolition, under contracts that functioned as coerced, non-consensual livelihood (Tinker, *A New System of Slavery*, 1974). An inversion of livelihood security: labor security engineered for the employer, not the worker.
- **19th–20th c., French colonial Africa — corvée labor.** Forced unpaid labor obligations (route-building, plantation work) imposed on colonial subjects, e.g. under France's *indigénat* code until 1946; workers had no ability to withhold labor or negotiate terms — the structural opposite of a safety buffer.
- **1980–2010, US Rust Belt — deindustrialization.** Steel and auto manufacturing employment in Pittsburgh, Youngstown, Detroit, and Flint collapsed from the late 1970s; US manufacturing employment fell from ~19.5 million (1979) to ~11.5 million (2010) (BLS). "Youngstown Sheet and Tube" mill closure (1977, "Black Monday") became the era's symbol.
- **1984–1985, UK — miners' strike.** Margaret Thatcher's government moved to close uneconomic coal pits; the National Union of Mineworkers struck for a year, ultimately losing. UK coal employment fell from ~700,000 (1950s) to under 10,000 by the 2000s; entire towns (South Wales, Yorkshire, Nottinghamshire) lost their only major employer (Beynon & Hudson, *The Shadow of the Mine*, 2021).
- **1990–1994, reunified Germany — Treuhandanstalt deindustrialization.** The Treuhand agency privatized or liquidated ~8,000 former East German state firms; East German employment fell by ~40% and regional unemployment in some districts (e.g., Sachsen-Anhalt) reached ~35% by the mid-1990s (Sinn & Sinn, *Jumpstart*, 1992). One of the fastest deliberate deindustrializations on record.
- **1960s–1980s, South and Southeast Asia — Green Revolution displacement.** High-yield seed varieties and mechanization raised output but favored large landholders with capital access; many smallholders and landless laborers in Punjab, the Philippines, and Indonesia lost land or wage-labor demand even as aggregate food production rose (Shiva, *The Violence of the Green Revolution*, 1991; Lipton & Longhurst, 1989).
- **2007–2009, global — financial crisis.** US unemployment rose from 4.7% (Nov 2007) to 10.0% (Oct 2009) (BLS); Spain and Greece later exceeded 25%, with youth unemployment above 50% in both. Triggered sovereign debt crises tied directly to lost livelihoods and lost tax bases.
- **2008–present — gig economy precarity.** Uber (founded 2009), TaskRabbit, and similar platforms classified millions of workers as independent contractors, excluded from unemployment insurance, minimum wage, and employer-side benefits by design. California's AB5 (2019) and Prop 22 (2020, reversing key parts of AB5 for app-based drivers) mark the ongoing legal fight over this classification.
- **2011–2012, Eurozone periphery — Greek depression.** Greek unemployment peaked near 27.5% (2013, Eurostat) after austerity-conditioned bailouts; youth unemployment exceeded 58%. A near-Depression-scale shock inside a modern welfare state, testing its buffers to failure.
- **2020, global — COVID-19 shock.** US unemployment spiked from 3.5% to 14.7% in two months (April 2020, BLS) — the fastest employment collapse in the post-war record — as lockdowns shut hospitality, retail, and travel sectors overnight.
- **2022–present, global — generative-AI displacement anxiety.** Surveys (e.g., Goldman Sachs 2023 estimate of ~300 million jobs exposed globally to generative AI automation) and disputes like the 2023 Hollywood WGA/SAG-AFTRA strikes (explicitly over AI use in scriptwriting and likeness rights) mark a new, still-unresolved wave of technological-displacement anxiety.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **1911, UK — National Insurance Act.** Introduced compulsory unemployment insurance for ~2.25 million workers in cyclical trades (building, engineering, shipbuilding), funded by worker/employer/state contributions. Widely cited as the first state-run unemployment insurance scheme at national scale (Gilbert, *British Social Policy 1914–1939*).
- **1935, US — Social Security Act.** Created a joint federal-state unemployment insurance system (Title III/IX) alongside old-age insurance, direct New Deal response to Depression-era mass unemployment; state UI programs began paying benefits from 1938.
- **1933–1942, US — WPA and CCC.** The Works Progress Administration employed roughly 8.5 million people over its life on public infrastructure and arts projects; the Civilian Conservation Corps employed ~3 million young men (1933–1942) on conservation work. Direct state job-creation as a livelihood backstop, not just income replacement.
- **19th–20th c., industrializing world — labor unions and collective bargaining.** UK Trade Union Act (1871), US Wagner Act (1935, guaranteeing collective bargaining rights), and equivalents elsewhere gave workers a structural counterweight to unilateral employer decisions on wages and layoffs — turning livelihood risk into a negotiated, rule-bound process.
- **1938, US — Fair Labor Standards Act.** Established the first federal minimum wage (25 cents/hour) and the 40-hour work week, setting a floor under livelihood income regardless of employer bargaining power; over 90 countries now have some form of statutory minimum wage.
- **1960s–present, OECD — active labor market policy (ALMP).** Sweden's Public Employment Service model (developed from the 1950s under the Rehn-Meidner framework) pioneered retraining and job-placement subsidies over passive cash payments; Denmark's "flexicurity" model (formalized 1990s–2000s) combines easy hiring/firing with generous retraining and income support.
- **2017, Finland — basic income pilot.** Kela ran a two-year randomized trial (2017–2018) giving 2,000 unemployed people €560/month unconditionally; results showed modest wellbeing gains and no significant net change in employment (Kela final report, 2020).
- **2011–present, Kenya — GiveDirectly UBI trial.** GiveDirectly's long-running unconditional cash transfer study across ~295 villages (from 2017, still tracked) is among the largest randomized UBI-style experiments in a low-income setting, testing livelihood-buffer effects at scale outside formal welfare states.
- **2019–2020, California — AB5 and Prop 22.** AB5 sought to reclassify gig workers as employees (entitling them to UI, minimum wage, benefits); Prop 22 partially reversed this for app-based drivers while mandating limited benefits (healthcare subsidy, minimum earnings guarantee) — an early, contested attempt at "portable benefits" for gig work.
- **2020s, multiple US states and EU — portable benefits proposals.** Washington State's portable benefits legislation (SB 5992, 2023) and the EU Platform Work Directive (adopted 2024) both attempt to attach unemployment-style protections to workers rather than to a single employer, addressing the structural gap gig work created.
- **1962, US — Trade Adjustment Assistance / Manpower Development and Training Act.** Early federal recognition that trade- and technology-driven job loss needs targeted retraining and relocation subsidies, not just generic unemployment insurance — a direct institutional answer to deindustrialization-style displacement.

## Where this fails today

- **2023–2025, global tech sector — AI-linked layoffs.** Challenger, Gray & Christmas tracked over 1,000 US layoffs explicitly citing AI as a factor in 2023–2024; by May 2025 its report attributed ~20,000 US job cuts that month partly to AI adoption. Klarna's CEO said in 2024 its AI tools let it shrink headcount by ~40% via attrition; IBM said in 2023–2024 it paused hiring for ~7,800 back-office roles it expected AI to replace. No unemployment-insurance category exists yet for "AI-displaced," so these workers fall into the same generic UI system built for factory layoffs.
- **2023–2026, US and EU — continued gig-classification battles.** The US Department of Labor's 2024 independent-contractor rule (effective March 2024) tightened the test for who counts as an employee, only for legal challenges to keep it unsettled through 2025. The EU Platform Work Directive, adopted in 2024, gives member states until December 2026 to transpose a presumption of employment for platform workers — meaning as of mid-2026 most gig workers in the EU still lack the protection on paper. Uber and Lyft settled a $328 million wage-theft case with New York State in 2023, evidence of ongoing, unresolved misclassification harm, not a closed chapter.
- **2020s, Middle East and North Africa — youth unemployment crisis.** ILO's 2024 World Employment and Social Outlook put MENA youth unemployment near 24%, among the highest of any region; World Bank data show it has stayed above 20% every year since 2010. Southern Europe remains similarly exposed: Eurostat recorded Spain's youth unemployment at ~25.8% and Greece's at ~22.9% in early 2025, more than a decade after their debt-crisis peaks, showing labor-market scarring that never fully healed.
- **2020s, Sub-Saharan Africa — informal-sector youth crisis.** ILO estimates roughly 85% of Sub-Saharan Africa's employment is informal, with youth (15–24) facing the highest exposure; the region needs to create an estimated 15–20 million new jobs a year through 2030 (World Bank, "Africa's Pulse," 2023) just to absorb new labor-market entrants, against actual formal job creation far below that.
- **2020–2024, US and Europe — post-pandemic labor-market scarring.** Long-term unemployment (27+ weeks) in the US stayed elevated well after headline unemployment recovered, and BLS data show labor-force participation among prime-age workers did not return to its pre-2020 trend until 2023; the OECD's 2023 Employment Outlook flagged persistent scarring in low-wage service sectors (hospitality, retail) hit hardest by 2020 shutdowns.
- **2020s, global auto sector — EV-transition deindustrialization.** The EU's 2035 ban on new internal-combustion-engine car sales, plus the shift to EVs, is projected by the European Association of Automotive Suppliers to put ~500,000 EU auto-supply-chain jobs at risk by 2030, because EV powertrains need roughly 30% fewer assembly hours than combustion engines. Ford, Stellantis, and Volkswagen all announced plant closures or thousands of job cuts between 2023 and 2025 tied explicitly to the EV transition, echoing the Rust Belt pattern but on a compressed, policy-driven timeline.
- **Ongoing — global informal-economy exposure.** ILO's 2023 estimate puts ~2 billion workers, roughly 58% of global employment, in informal work with no unemployment insurance, no severance, and no enforceable minimum wage. This is the baseline condition for a majority of the world's workforce, not an edge case — the livelihood-security buffer this section tracks simply does not exist for most people alive today.
