# Employment / Livelihood Security (Tier 2 — Safety)

Tier definition: a buffer against unpredictable future threats to resources. Livelihood is the main channel through which people convert labor into resources. When that channel breaks — suddenly, structurally, or by force — the safety buffer collapses even if food and shelter aren't yet gone.

## How the threat has shown up

- **1811–1816, England — Luddite risings.** Textile workers smashed stocking- and lace-frames as mechanized looms cut skilled weavers' wages and threw hand-loom workers out of work; Parliament made frame-breaking a capital offense in 1812. First large-scale organized backlash against technological displacement (E.P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class*, 1963).
- **1930s, US Great Plains — Dust Bowl.** Drought plus over-plowed topsoil destroyed farm livelihoods across Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, and Colorado; roughly 2.5 million people left the Plains states in the 1930s, with ~200,000 migrating to California (Worster, *Dust Bowl*, 1979; US Census migration data). Agricultural livelihood collapse compounded by ecological failure.
- **1929–1939, global — Great Depression.** US unemployment peaked near 25% in 1933 (Bureau of Labor Statistics historical series); Germany hit ~30% by 1932. Mass unemployment on this scale had no existing institutional buffer, feeding political radicalization.
- **1947 (partition) and ongoing 19th–20th c. — indentured servitude.** Britain shipped over 1.3 million Indian indentured laborers to Caribbean, Mauritius, Fiji, and South Africa between 1834 and 1917 after slavery's abolition, under contracts that functioned as coerced, non-consensual livelihood (Tinker, *A New System of Slavery*, 1974). An inversion of livelihood security: labor security engineered for the employer, not the worker.
- **19th–20th c., French colonial Africa — corvée labor.** Forced unpaid labor obligations (route-building, plantation work) imposed on colonial subjects, e.g. under France's *indigénat* code until 1946; workers had no ability to withhold labor or negotiate terms — the structural opposite of a safety buffer.
- **1980–2010, US Rust Belt — deindustrialization.** Steel and auto manufacturing employment in Pittsburgh, Youngstown, Detroit, and Flint collapsed from the late 1970s; US manufacturing employment fell from ~19.5 million (1979) to ~11.5 million (2010) (BLS). "Youngstown Sheet and Tube" mill closure (1977, "Black Monday") became the era's symbol.
- **1984–1985, UK — miners' strike.** Margaret Thatcher's government moved to close uneconomic coal pits; the National Union of Mineworkers struck for a year, ultimately losing. UK coal employment fell from ~700,000 (1950s) to under 10,000 by the 2000s; entire towns (South Wales, Yorkshire, Nottinghamshire) lost their only major employer (Beynon & Hudson, *The Shadow of the Mine*, 2021).
- **1990–1994, reunified Germany — Treuhandanstalt deindustrialization.** The Treuhand agency privatized or liquidated ~8,000 former East German state firms; East German employment fell by ~40% and regional unemployment in some districts (e.g., Sachsen-Anhalt) reached ~35% by the mid-1990s (Sinn & Sinn, *Jumpstart*, 1992). One of the fastest deliberate deindustrializations on record.
- **1960s–1980s, South and Southeast Asia — Green Revolution displacement.** High-yield seed varieties and mechanization raised output but favored large landholders with capital access; many smallholders and landless laborers in Punjab, the Philippines, and Indonesia lost land or wage-labor demand even as aggregate food production rose (Shiva, *The Violence of the Green Revolution*, 1991; Lipton & Longhurst, 1989).
- **2007–2009, global — financial crisis.** US unemployment rose from 4.7% (Nov 2007) to 10.0% (Oct 2009) (BLS); Spain and Greece later exceeded 25%, with youth unemployment above 50% in both. Triggered sovereign debt crises tied directly to lost livelihoods and lost tax bases.
- **2008–present — gig economy precarity.** Uber (founded 2009), TaskRabbit, and similar platforms classified millions of workers as independent contractors, excluded from unemployment insurance, minimum wage, and employer-side benefits by design. California's AB5 (2019) and Prop 22 (2020, reversing key parts of AB5 for app-based drivers) mark the ongoing legal fight over this classification.
- **2011–2012, Eurozone periphery — Greek depression.** Greek unemployment peaked near 27.5% (2013, Eurostat) after austerity-conditioned bailouts; youth unemployment exceeded 58%. A near-Depression-scale shock inside a modern welfare state, testing its buffers to failure.
- **2020, global — COVID-19 shock.** US unemployment spiked from 3.5% to 14.7% in two months (April 2020, BLS) — the fastest employment collapse in the post-war record — as lockdowns shut hospitality, retail, and travel sectors overnight.
- **2022–present, global — generative-AI displacement anxiety.** Surveys (e.g., Goldman Sachs 2023 estimate of ~300 million jobs exposed globally to generative AI automation) and disputes like the 2023 Hollywood WGA/SAG-AFTRA strikes (explicitly over AI use in scriptwriting and likeness rights) mark a new, still-unresolved wave of technological-displacement anxiety.

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **1911, UK — National Insurance Act.** Introduced compulsory unemployment insurance for ~2.25 million workers in cyclical trades (building, engineering, shipbuilding), funded by worker/employer/state contributions. Widely cited as the first state-run unemployment insurance scheme at national scale (Gilbert, *British Social Policy 1914–1939*).
- **1935, US — Social Security Act.** Created a joint federal-state unemployment insurance system (Title III/IX) alongside old-age insurance, direct New Deal response to Depression-era mass unemployment; state UI programs began paying benefits from 1938.
- **1933–1942, US — WPA and CCC.** The Works Progress Administration employed roughly 8.5 million people over its life on public infrastructure and arts projects; the Civilian Conservation Corps employed ~3 million young men (1933–1942) on conservation work. Direct state job-creation as a livelihood backstop, not just income replacement.
- **19th–20th c., industrializing world — labor unions and collective bargaining.** UK Trade Union Act (1871), US Wagner Act (1935, guaranteeing collective bargaining rights), and equivalents elsewhere gave workers a structural counterweight to unilateral employer decisions on wages and layoffs — turning livelihood risk into a negotiated, rule-bound process.
- **1938, US — Fair Labor Standards Act.** Established the first federal minimum wage (25 cents/hour) and the 40-hour work week, setting a floor under livelihood income regardless of employer bargaining power; over 90 countries now have some form of statutory minimum wage.
- **1960s–present, OECD — active labor market policy (ALMP).** Sweden's Public Employment Service model (developed from the 1950s under the Rehn-Meidner framework) pioneered retraining and job-placement subsidies over passive cash payments; Denmark's "flexicurity" model (formalized 1990s–2000s) combines easy hiring/firing with generous retraining and income support.
- **2017, Finland — basic income pilot.** Kela ran a two-year randomized trial (2017–2018) giving 2,000 unemployed people €560/month unconditionally; results showed modest wellbeing gains and no significant net change in employment (Kela final report, 2020).
- **2011–present, Kenya — GiveDirectly UBI trial.** GiveDirectly's long-running unconditional cash transfer study across ~295 villages (from 2017, still tracked) is among the largest randomized UBI-style experiments in a low-income setting, testing livelihood-buffer effects at scale outside formal welfare states.
- **2019–2020, California — AB5 and Prop 22.** AB5 sought to reclassify gig workers as employees (entitling them to UI, minimum wage, benefits); Prop 22 partially reversed this for app-based drivers while mandating limited benefits (healthcare subsidy, minimum earnings guarantee) — an early, contested attempt at "portable benefits" for gig work.
- **2020s, multiple US states and EU — portable benefits proposals.** Washington State's portable benefits legislation (SB 5992, 2023) and the EU Platform Work Directive (adopted 2024) both attempt to attach unemployment-style protections to workers rather than to a single employer, addressing the structural gap gig work created.
- **1962, US — Trade Adjustment Assistance / Manpower Development and Training Act.** Early federal recognition that trade- and technology-driven job loss needs targeted retraining and relocation subsidies, not just generic unemployment insurance — a direct institutional answer to deindustrialization-style displacement.

### Where it worked

- **2008–2010, Germany — Kurzarbeit (short-time work).** German GDP fell 6.6% peak-to-trough in 2008–09, one of the sharpest contractions among major economies, driven by an export collapse. Unemployment rose only 0.5 percentage points (7.4% to 7.9%) before resuming its pre-crisis downward trend — commentators called it Germany's "jobs miracle." The mechanism: the state paid workers ~60% of lost wages for hours cut, letting employers hold headcount steady and reduce hours instead of laying off; usage peaked at ~1.5 million workers across 63,000 employers in May 2009. Contrast the US over the same shock, with no equivalent wage-subsidy instrument: unemployment rose from 4.7% (Nov 2007) to 10.0% (Oct 2009), a ~5.3pp jump against Germany's 0.5pp, despite a comparable-scale GDP shock (Bitkom/IAB research; CEPR "German labour-market miracle"; BLS). The instrument converts an employer's layoff decision into a subsidized hours-reduction decision — it doesn't create jobs, it prevents the destruction of existing employment relationships during a temporary demand shock, which is exactly what a safety-tier buffer is supposed to do. It has since been copied or reactivated by most OECD economies for COVID-19 (Germany itself put 7.3 million workers on it in May 2020).

- **1900s–present, Nordics and Belgium — the Ghent system (non-state).** Unemployment insurance administered by trade unions rather than the state, subsidised from public funds but held by the union. The measured effect is on the buffer's *carrier*, not only its coverage: OECD.Stat trade-union density in 2019 stood at ~91% in Iceland, ~67% in Denmark, ~65% in Sweden and ~59% in Finland — all Ghent or quasi-Ghent — against an OECD average near 16%. Belgium, a partial case, sat at ~49%. The institution that pays the worker when work stops is the same institution that bargains while it continues, so the constituency does not demobilise between shocks. This is the tier's clearest evidence that a livelihood buffer does not have to be state-carried to work, and it is the structural opposite of India's arrangement, where the buffer is state-carried, thin, and the union is a supplicant to it.
- **1972, India — SEWA, and the invention of a category that could hold a union (non-state).** SEWA registered as a trade union of *self-employed* women, at a time when Indian labour law recognised unions only of employees against an employer. The before/after is categorical rather than numerical: before 1972 a woman with no employer had no legal vehicle for collective claim; after it, self-employment became an organisable status. Every later Indian instrument that attaches protection to a worker rather than to an employment relationship — welfare boards, e-Shram, the gig-worker chapter of the Code on Social Security — runs on the category SEWA forced open. Its later parametric heat-insurance cover for 21,000 exposed women (see `../tier1-physiological/04-shelter-thermoregulation.md`) is the same move applied to a priced risk.
- **2023–2025, India — IFAT and the first statutes for platform work (activism → institution, partial).** Before 2023, no Indian statute recognised platform workers as a category with claimable entitlements. The Indian Federation of App-based Transport Workers, with the Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Union and the Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy, took a drafted law to state governments; Rajasthan passed the first Act in 2023, Karnataka followed with an Ordinance and then the Karnataka Platform-based Gig Workers (Social Security and Welfare) Act, 2025, Telangana produced a draft Bill in 2025, and the gig-worker chapter of the central Code on Social Security, 2020 commenced on 21 November 2025. Two years, four jurisdictions, from zero. It is entered here as a *partial* control and read again in the failure section below, because what was won is the statute and what was not won is its execution — Rajasthan's rules were never notified, Karnataka's Act is under challenge by Swiggy, Zomato, Blinkit and Zepto, and the central levy's rate remains unnotified. The one place the machinery has actually moved money is Karnataka, where the High Court declined to stay the Act and directed the platforms to deposit the second-quarter welfare fee.
- **2024–2025, India — SEBI's derivatives curbs (regulator, honest partial).** After a first study established the loss pattern, SEBI tightened index-derivatives rules from October 2024 (fewer weekly expiries, higher contract sizes, upfront premium collection). Unique individual traders in the F&O segment fell ~20% to 6.7 million. But net individual losses over the same window still rose 41%, to ₹1,05,603 crore in FY25 from ₹74,812 crore in FY24. The instrument moved participation and did not move the loss, which is the useful half of the result: the count of exposed people is reachable by regulation, the per-head damage was not reached by this regulation.

## Where this fails today

- **2023–2025, global tech sector — AI-linked layoffs.** Challenger, Gray & Christmas tracked over 1,000 US layoffs explicitly citing AI as a factor in 2023–2024; by May 2025 its report attributed ~20,000 US job cuts that month partly to AI adoption. Klarna's CEO said in 2024 its AI tools let it shrink headcount by ~40% via attrition; IBM said in 2023–2024 it paused hiring for ~7,800 back-office roles it expected AI to replace. No unemployment-insurance category exists yet for "AI-displaced," so these workers fall into the same generic UI system built for factory layoffs.
- **2023–2026, US and EU — continued gig-classification battles.** The US Department of Labor's 2024 independent-contractor rule (effective March 2024) tightened the test for who counts as an employee, only for legal challenges to keep it unsettled through 2025. The EU Platform Work Directive, adopted in 2024, gives member states until December 2026 to transpose a presumption of employment for platform workers — meaning as of mid-2026 most gig workers in the EU still lack the protection on paper. Uber and Lyft settled a $328 million wage-theft case with New York State in 2023, evidence of ongoing, unresolved misclassification harm, not a closed chapter.
- **2020s, Middle East and North Africa — youth unemployment crisis.** ILO's 2024 World Employment and Social Outlook put MENA youth unemployment near 24%, among the highest of any region; World Bank data show it has stayed above 20% every year since 2010. Southern Europe remains similarly exposed: Eurostat recorded Spain's youth unemployment at ~25.8% and Greece's at ~22.9% in early 2025, more than a decade after their debt-crisis peaks, showing labor-market scarring that never fully healed.
- **2020s, Sub-Saharan Africa — informal-sector youth crisis.** ILO estimates roughly 85% of Sub-Saharan Africa's employment is informal, with youth (15–24) facing the highest exposure; the region needs to create an estimated 15–20 million new jobs a year through 2030 (World Bank, "Africa's Pulse," 2023) just to absorb new labor-market entrants, against actual formal job creation far below that.
- **2020–2024, US and Europe — post-pandemic labor-market scarring.** Long-term unemployment (27+ weeks) in the US stayed elevated well after headline unemployment recovered, and BLS data show labor-force participation among prime-age workers did not return to its pre-2020 trend until 2023; the OECD's 2023 Employment Outlook flagged persistent scarring in low-wage service sectors (hospitality, retail) hit hardest by 2020 shutdowns.
- **2020s, global auto sector — EV-transition deindustrialization.** The EU's 2035 ban on new internal-combustion-engine car sales, plus the shift to EVs, is projected by the European Association of Automotive Suppliers to put ~500,000 EU auto-supply-chain jobs at risk by 2030, because EV powertrains need roughly 30% fewer assembly hours than combustion engines. Ford, Stellantis, and Volkswagen all announced plant closures or thousands of job cuts between 2023 and 2025 tied explicitly to the EV transition, echoing the Rust Belt pattern but on a compressed, policy-driven timeline.
- **Ongoing — global informal-economy exposure.** ILO's 2023 estimate puts ~2 billion workers, roughly 58% of global employment, in informal work with no unemployment insurance, no severance, and no enforceable minimum wage. This is the baseline condition for a majority of the world's workforce, not an edge case — the livelihood-security buffer this section tracks simply does not exist for most people alive today.

### India: a channel under pressure with no buffer behind it

The headline says nothing is wrong. PLFS 2024-25 puts the usual-status unemployment rate for persons aged 15+ at **3.2%** — 2.5% rural, 5.1% urban. Every other number in this section is worse, and the reason the headline holds is definitional: usual status counts a person as employed if they worked in any gainful activity for the major part of the preceding year, including **unpaid work in a household enterprise**. India's labour-force participation rate rose from ~50% in PLFS 2017-18 to ~61% in PLFS 2024-25, and that rise is driven almost entirely by rural women entering unpaid family work and own-account agriculture. A measure that reads distress absorption as employment will always report a healthy labour market during a livelihood squeeze. **Read the 3.2% as the tier-1 aggregation mechanism operating on a definition rather than on a geography.**

**The buffer this file exists to track does not exist for most Indians.** India has no general unemployment insurance. The Atal Beemit Vyakti Kalyan Yojana pays a partial relief for a limited period, and only to workers already covered by ESIC — that is, a slice of the formal, factory-adjacent workforce. Against ILO's estimate that ~58% of global employment (roughly 2 billion workers) is informal, India's informal share is far higher — on the order of ~90% of the workforce, the figure carried by ILO and by the NCEUS work before it, though the precise share moves with whether "informal sector" or "informal employment" is the definition used. For that population job loss produces no claim on anything. Inter-state migrant workers have no portable livelihood entitlement at all; One Nation One Ration Card made *food* portable in 2019-20 and nothing equivalent exists for income. Germany's Kurzarbeit, in the positive-control section above, converts an employer's layoff decision into a subsidised hours decision. India has no instrument that attaches at that decision point for the overwhelming majority of its workers, so every shock below transmits straight through to the household.

**AI is compressing entry, not employment.** TCS cut more than 12,000 jobs in July 2025 and its net headcount fell by over 23,000 in the fiscal year to March 2026, concentrated in middle and senior management. The top five IT services firms — TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech, Tech Mahindra — recorded a **combined net reduction of 6,981 in FY26**, reversing two years of headcount growth. NITI Aayog puts over 60% of India's *formal-sector* jobs as exposed to automation by 2030; the IMF's comparable global figure is ~40%, with ~26% for low-income countries, so India's formal segment is unusually exposed precisely because it is the segment built on codified, remotely-delivered process work. But the mechanism visible so far is a **hiring freeze, not a firing wave**: net reductions run mostly through attrition and management cuts while fresher intake and lateral hiring are throttled. The displaced party is therefore the person who has not yet been hired, which is not a category any unemployment instrument in the world is built to detect. This is the same failure as the tier-1 file's *instrument keyed to the wrong object*: severance, UI and retrenchment law all attach to an existing employment relationship, and the harm here is the non-formation of one.

**The metro white-collar income multiplier is real in mechanism and unmeasured in magnitude.** Urban consumption growth decelerated to 4.7% year-on-year in 2QFY26 from 5.9% in 1QFY26, and analysts attributed the deceleration partly to weaker real salary and wage growth at BSE500 companies alongside a contraction in passenger-vehicle sales. The chain from a metro salaried job to the drivers, cooks, salon workers, delivery riders and maintenance staff it supports is not disputed by anyone; **no Indian study quantifies it.** There is no local-multiplier estimate for Indian metros of the kind Moretti produced for US cities. The consequence is that a white-collar contraction and its second-order service-employment effect will be reported as two unrelated stories, and the second one will surface only as a slowdown in someone's FMCG volumes. *Absence of measurement is the finding here, not a gap in this research.*

**Gig work: rising headcount, falling unit income, and a remedy stuck at rules-notification.** India's gig workforce reached roughly 12 million in FY2024-25, against NITI Aayog's 7.7 million baseline for FY2020-21 — a ~55% increase in four years. Close to **40% earn under ₹15,000 a month**, with sharp income volatility and no institutional credit or insurance. The e-Shram portal had over 312 million registrations by December 2025, which is a registration rail rather than an entitlement. The user's own reading — that shrinking outside options reduce platforms' incentive to concede — is the correct mechanism, and it needs one amendment: the state's actual instrument here is not job creation but the **aggregator levy**, and it is live. The Code on Social Security, 2020 fixes a statutory contribution of not less than 1% and not more than 2% of an aggregator's annual turnover, capped at 5% of what it pays out to gig workers, across nine aggregator categories in Schedule 7. Karnataka's High Court declined to stay its state Act and ordered Swiggy, Zomato, Blinkit and Zepto to deposit the second quarter's welfare fee. So leverage exists. What does not exist is notification: **Rajasthan's 2023 Act — the first in the country — never had its rules framed after the state government changed**, and the central levy's rate, benefit structure and operational rules remain unnotified as of 2026. Tier 1's *won the law, lost the execution* recurs here without modification, on a two-year rather than a thirty-year timescale.

**The four labour codes, in force 21 November 2025 — the largest single change to this need in the decade, and it runs the other way.** On that date the Government of India commenced all four codes together — the Code on Wages, 2019 and the Industrial Relations, Social Security and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Codes, 2020 — consolidating 29 existing labour laws. Four things in them bear directly on livelihood security, and they do not point in the same direction.

- **The retrenchment threshold moved from 100 to 300.** Under the Industrial Relations Code, an establishment now needs prior government approval for layoff, retrenchment or closure only at 300 or more workers, up from 100; the standing-orders requirement moves to the same 300 threshold. The stated purpose is hiring flexibility for mid-sized firms. The direct effect is that a large band of Indian establishments can now dismiss at will, in a country with no unemployment insurance behind the dismissal.
- **Lawful striking became substantially harder.** The 60-day advance strike-notice requirement, previously confined to public utility services, now applies to **all** industrial establishments, with the strike permissible only inside a 14-day window named in the notice; strikes and lock-outs are barred during and for seven days after conciliation, and during and for sixty days after tribunal proceedings. Recognition as sole negotiating union is set at 51% of workers. Taken together these lengthen the runway a union must telegraph before acting and consolidate bargaining into a single majority union.
- **Fixed-term employment is now a formal category** with wage and benefit parity and gratuity eligibility after one year — a genuine improvement over contract-labour intermediation, and simultaneously a legitimisation of term-limited hiring as the default form. The OSH Code raises the contract-labour applicability threshold from 20 workers to 50. The Wages Code sets a statutory floor wage and redefines "wages" so that basic pay must be at least 50% of total remuneration, which raises the PF and gratuity base — the one unambiguously pro-worker provision with an immediate cash consequence.
- **The gig-worker chapter is the pro-worker half, and it is the half that has not commenced in substance.** The retrenchment threshold took effect on commencement. The aggregator levy did not: rates, benefits and operating rules are still pending notification. **That asymmetry is the finding** — within a single statute, the provision that removes a protection was self-executing and the provision that adds one requires a further discretionary act that has not happened.

Underneath all of it sits an execution gap of a different kind. Labour is on the Concurrent List, so each state and union territory must frame and notify its own rules. As of May 2026, **Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh and Delhi had notified rules across all four codes; Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh under three of four; most other states remain at draft stage.** A code that is nationally "in force" and locally unenforceable in most of the country is not a settled remedy; it is a two-speed regime in which the compliance-heavy states are also the industrialised ones.

The representation channel that would ordinarily contest this was bypassed rather than defeated. The codes were passed in 2019 and 2020 without tripartite consultation, and **the Indian Labour Conference — the country's apex tripartite forum — has not been convened since 2015.** Ten central trade unions declared 21 November 2025 a "black day" and struck nationwide on 26 November 2025 alongside the Samyukt Kisan Morcha, calling the overhaul a fraud on the working class; the RSS-affiliated Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh welcomed the codes. The movement is split at the top, the forum where it would have had a seat is dormant, and the party with the strongest interest in the 300-worker threshold is the party that wrote it. In tier-1 terms this is *authority mismatched to harm* in its purest form — the body holding the power over the object is not merely failing to contain the source, it **is** a party to it.

**Employment has become a subsidised object.** The Employment Linked Incentive scheme, approved by the Union Cabinet on 1 July 2025 with an outlay of **₹99,446 crore**, targets 3.5 crore formal jobs between 1 August 2025 and 31 July 2027, of which 1.92 crore are meant to be first-time entrants; Part A pays a first-time employee one month's wage up to ₹15,000, Part B pays employers for additional employment over two years, extended to four for manufacturing, all disbursed through EPFO. Two problems follow. First, **EPFO payroll additions measure formalisation, not creation** — an existing informal worker enrolled for the first time registers identically to a new job, so the scheme's own success metric cannot distinguish the two. Second, the user's standing critique of production-linked incentives applies directly here: *"these schemes incentivize output over employment and focus on capital-intensive sectors that cannot generate the millions of jobs India requires annually"* (Vardan, Slate, ~Jun 2026). ELI is the corrective — an incentive keyed to headcount rather than output — which makes it the test case for whether the objection was about the *keying* or about the underlying demand for labour. That test has not yet returned a result, and its metric is compromised in the way just described.

**Self-employment is rising as distress, and it is rural and female, not the laid-off urban professional.** The share of self-employed among employed women rose to **67.4% in 2023-24 from 51.9% in 2017-18**, and to 73.5% from 57.7% in rural areas. Among rural women, own-account workers and employers went from 19% to 31.2% and unpaid helpers in household enterprises from 38.7% to 42.3% over the same period. Own-account earnings fell over the same window. This is the mass phenomenon, and it is differently shaped from the retrenched salaried professional starting a venture: it is unpaid or barely-paid labour absorbed inside a household enterprise because household income fell. The commonly-cited entrepreneurship story and this one use the same PLFS line item.

**The credit channel that actually funds small enterprise contracted, and its recovery excludes the marginal borrower.** Microfinance, not venture capital, is the capital supply for the population above. The industry's gross loan portfolio **fell 13.9% to ₹3,81,200 crore at March 2025** from ₹4,42,700 crore a year earlier; loan originations dropped 42% by volume and 35% by value in Q3FY25; portfolio at risk beyond 31 days rose **163% to ₹43,075 crore**, taking the 31–180 day PAR rate from 2.1% to 6.2%. Contraction continued to a ~17% year-on-year decline in portfolio outstanding by March 2026 before the first quarterly growth in five quarters. The shape of the recovery is the finding: 30-plus-days delinquency fell to 2.35% (March 2026) from 6.64% a year earlier **because lenders moved to larger tickets and borrowers with existing credit histories — loans above ₹75,000 rose to 41% of originations from 26%.** The book healed by shedding the new and marginal borrower, which is precisely the person entering self-employment because they lost other work.

**Venture capital concentrated, but it was never the channel for this population.** Indian startups raised ~$11 billion in 2025 with the number of rounds down ~39% to 1,518; H1 2026 saw $5.2 billion across 501 deals, in which the **three largest rounds alone took 31% of all capital deployed**, and 13 rounds above $100 million were recorded in FY26 against 23 in FY25. Concentration is confirmed. The correction to the user's framing is that the drain on savings he describes does not run through VC competition — the people he means were never inside the VC-addressable set. It runs through the microfinance contraction above and through the next item.

**Household savings are being routed into a segment that reliably destroys them.** SEBI's July 2025 study of ~9.6 million individual traders found **91% lost money in equity derivatives in FY25**, with aggregate net losses of **₹1,05,603 crore, up 41% from ₹74,812 crore in FY24**, and an average per-person loss of ₹1.1 lakh. Two consecutive years produced the same nine-in-ten pattern. The counter-evidence is written above as a partial positive control: after SEBI's October 2024 curbs the count of unique F&O traders fell ~20% to 6.7 million, so the exposed population is reachable by regulation even though the aggregate loss kept rising. The livelihood relevance is that this is where a retrenched worker's severance goes.

**Quick commerce and the retail base — the claim holds, the measurement does not exist.** The All India Consumer Products Distributors Federation reported in October 2024 that roughly **200,000 kirana stores had shut in the preceding year**, 90,000 of them in metros, with closures distributed 45% metro / 30% tier-1 / 25% tier-2-and-below, against a base of ~13 million kirana stores of which over 10 million sit in tier-2 and smaller cities. Two caveats belong in the file rather than in a footnote. AICPDF is a **distributors' federation** — an interested party whose members are disintermediated by the same shift — and the figure is a survey estimate with no published methodology. More importantly, **no official series measures retail-establishment closure in India at all**, so a body with a stake in the answer is the only source of a number on it. The direction is corroborated by nothing independent, and that is itself the state of the evidence.

**Rural India: the structural transition is running backwards.** Agriculture's share of employment rose to 45.6% in 2019-20 from 42.5% two years earlier, and rural India has since become the principal source of India's employment growth. An economy that moves workers *into* agriculture is not industrialising; it is absorbing people who could not be absorbed elsewhere. The public buffer thinned at the same time: **MGNREGA person-days per household fell to 44.62 in 2024-25 from 52.08 in 2023-24**, against a statutory entitlement of 100, with insufficient allocation and delayed payments as the mechanism — demand rationed by funding rather than met. Rural wages did not compensate: Wage Rates in Rural India data show **annual real wage growth below 1% for almost all agricultural and non-agricultural occupations**, over a period in which the economy grew ~4.6% and the farm sector ~4.2% a year (2019-20 to 2023-24). One part of the user's reading does not survive: the COVID reverse-migration wave reversed, and the durable rural absorption since is women entering unpaid family work and own-account agriculture, not returnee men staying. Rural female LFPR rose from 26.4% to 47.6% between 2018-19 and 2023-24. The stress is real and its carrier is different from the one assumed.

**The creator economy is too small to function as an income channel.** India has roughly 4–4.4 million active creators, of whom **fewer than 10% earn a meaningful income**, and 88% report that platforms supply under three-quarters of their total income. The entire influencer-marketing industry was worth ₹3,000–3,500 crore in 2025, projected to ₹4,500–5,000 crore by 2027. Nano-creators (1,000–10,000 followers) are 61.1% of the population and micro-creators (10,000–100,000) another 32.5%. Set against a workforce of roughly 600 million, the **aggregate** — not merely the winner count — is a rounding error. The user's reading that it helps very few is right; the sharper version is that even if distribution were perfectly equal the pool could not constitute a livelihood for a measurable share of anyone.

**Youth carry the adjustment.** The ILO and Institute for Human Development's *India Employment Report 2024* found youth to be **82.9% of India's unemployed**; the share of the unemployed holding secondary or higher education nearly doubled from 35.2% in 2000 to 65.7% in 2022; youth unemployment (15–29) rose from 5.7% in 2000 to 17.5% in 2019; and one in three young people were not in education, employment or training in 2022, disproportionately women. PLFS 2024-25 puts **graduate unemployment at 11.2%, roughly 3.5 times the 3.2% headline.** Education raises the unemployment rate in India, which inverts the relationship that holds in most economies and identifies the problem as a shortage of jobs at the education level supplied, not a shortage of skills. The user's second mechanism — experienced workers accepting cheaper roles and crowding out entrants — **could not be verified**; no Indian series measures displacement of freshers by experienced hires. It is consistent with the frozen fresher intake in IT described above, and it is recorded here as a hypothesis with indirect support rather than a finding.

**Casual labour: frozen incomes, and a "shortage" that is a wage floor.** Casual daily-wage workers are about 20% of the workforce, earning roughly ₹10,000 a month at 22 working days against ₹22,699 for regular wage workers. Real wage growth below 1% a year, above, applies to them directly. The user's observation of a rural labour shortage alongside urban labour surplus needs both hypotheses held rather than one chosen. Against a general shortage: rural female LFPR nearly doubled to 47.6% and agriculture's employment share rose, so the rural labour pool expanded rather than emptied. For a real localised shortage: it appears at **peak agricultural operations in migrant-dependent states**, where the binding constraint is the wage offered at a specific fortnight, not the number of workers in the district. The reconciliation is that labour is abundant in aggregate and scarce at particular times and prices — which makes this a wage-floor and seasonal-mobility problem rather than a supply problem, and it is why MGNREGA's wage rate functions as the de facto rural reservation wage and why its erosion transmits directly into private wage bargaining.

**The rupture in the social fabric arrives as legislation.** The user's fourth global failure mode — competition for shrinking income opportunities dividing people along class, caste, religion and region — has a specific Indian legal form: **domicile reservation in private-sector employment.** Haryana's 2021 Act reserved 75% of private jobs paying under ₹30,000 a month for holders of a state domicile certificate; the Punjab and Haryana High Court quashed it, the Supreme Court set aside that order and remanded the matter, leaving the question live. Jharkhand enacted a parallel 75% statute in 2021; Karnataka's Cabinet approved a Bill in 2024 for 75% of non-management and 50% of management roles. The mechanism is exactly as described: a receding pool of formal jobs converts into a contest over *who has standing to hold one*, and the state supplies the sorting criterion. Beneath the statutory layer, the private allocation of jobs already discriminates before any law applies — Thorat and Attewell's correspondence study (*Economic and Political Weekly*, 2007) sent matched applications to private-sector job advertisements as upper-caste Hindu, Dalit and Muslim candidates and found discrimination operating at the **first stage of the application process**, before any interview. A market that sorts by caste at the résumé screen, plus a legislature that sorts by domicile at the statute, is the tier-4 esteem layer overwriting a tier-2 need — the same over-writing tier 1 identified when it found that not one of its six failure modes was randomly distributed.

**Hiring friction is the user's framing, and neither term of it is measurable in India.** His formulation — *open roles × period open for* is lost employment, lost salary and lost business outcome — is the right identity, and it is the standard search-and-matching one: vacancy-days per durable placement, with market tightness θ = v/u and a matching function `hires = A · u^α · v^(1−α)` whose residual A is matching efficiency. India publishes neither term. The only official vacancy series is the Quarterly Employment Survey under AQEES, which covers 12,038 establishments and 3.15 crore workers — roughly 5% of a ~60 crore workforce, and only non-farm units with 10+ workers. It reported about 1.85 lakh vacancies, a vacancy rate near **0.6%**, against a US JOLTS rate of **4–4.5%** in the same period. India does not publish an eightfold-tighter labour market than the United States; the series is measuring a fifth of the formal sector and calling it the market. Against that 0.6%, ManpowerGroup's employer survey puts Indian talent shortage near **80%** — the highest band it reports anywhere. **The contradiction is the finding.** Both numbers cannot be describing the same object, and the state's number is the one with no duration attached: QES records a vacancy count at a point in time, never how long a role stayed open, so the *period open for* half of the user's identity has no series at all. Note also that the shortage figures are vendor-produced — ManpowerGroup sells staffing and Wheebox sells assessments, and Wheebox's employability measure moves from 42.6% to 56.35% in four years with no fixed definition behind it. A 14-point improvement in four years on an undefined construct is not evidence about the labour market.

**The National Career Service has run for eleven years without publishing a placement figure.** In answer to the Lok Sabha on 3 August 2026, the government reported 10.05 crore vacancies "mobilised" on NCS and 6.64 crore registered jobseekers, of whom **17.68 lakh** were active. Mobilised vacancies and registrations are both inputs. The one output that would establish whether the exchange matches anyone — placements made — is not in the answer, and is not in any NCS annual disclosure. This is *aggregation masks failure* operating at the level of the metric rather than the geography: the reported number rises monotonically with effort and cannot fall when performance falls.

**EPFO's own payroll data shows the matching failure the vacancy series cannot.** EPFO's monthly provisional payroll release publishes an identity — net additions = new subscribers − exited + rejoined — and the components, not just the headline, are in the appendix. Read as a series across FY2017-18 to FY2024-25, they describe a labour market doing progressively more work for the same result:

| FY | New (lakh) | Exited (lakh) | Rejoined (lakh) | Net (lakh) | Rejoined ÷ new | Exited ÷ new | Joining events ÷ net |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2018-19 | 139.4 | 123.0 | 44.6 | 61.1 | 0.32 | 0.88 | 3.01 |
| 2020-21 | 85.5 | 97.8 | 89.4 | 77.1 | 1.05 | 1.14 | 2.27 |
| 2022-23 | 115.0 | 133.7 | 157.2 | 138.5 | 1.37 | 1.16 | 1.97 |
| 2023-24 | 109.9 | 149.9 | 171.4 | 131.5 | 1.56 | 1.36 | 2.14 |
| 2024-25 | 113.9 | 183.4 | 199.3 | 129.8 | 1.75 | 1.61 | 2.41 |

Between FY2022-23 and FY2024-25, gross joining events (new + rejoined) rose about **15%** and exits about **37%**, while net additions *fell* 6% — 138.5 lakh to 129.8 lakh. Joining events per net job bottomed at 1.97 in FY2022-23 and has risen to 2.41. By FY2024-25 the fund records **1.75 rejoiners for every first-time entrant** and **1.61 exits for every new subscriber**: the formal sector is now overwhelmingly recirculating people who were already inside it, and it takes two and a half joining events to produce one additional formal job. That is a falling-matching-efficiency series, national, monthly, eight years long — and it exists as a by-product of a pension administrator's accounting, not because anyone set out to measure the thing. This is the user's own earlier framing arriving in official data: his notes hold *"skilled labor shortages emerge while unskilled workers compete for limited jobs"* and *"job tenure has decreased dramatically"*, both written years before EPFO's components were published in this form.

**The attrition premise fails on the surveys and survives on the payroll data, and the reason is which population each measures.** HR-survey attrition is flat-to-falling: Deloitte's *India Talent Outlook 2026* puts total attrition at 17.4% (2022) against 17.6% (2025e). But the composition inverts — voluntary attrition falls from 17.0% to 11.8% while involuntary rises from 4.0% (2023) to 4.6%. Employees have stopped choosing to leave and employers have started choosing for them. That is **job-lock**, not mobility, and it is the opposite of what "rising attrition" is usually taken to mean while being consistent with everything else in this file. The surveys and EPFO disagree because they measure different populations: survey panels are large white-collar employers reporting rates, EPFO is gross national flow across every covered establishment. Both are right about their own object. The user's premise holds where it matters — churn is rising in the aggregate — and fails in the form it is usually asserted, which is voluntary quitting.

**Formal employment is discontinuous, and EPFO publishes the measure while mislabelling it.** The Annual Report reports both average monthly contributing members and members who contributed at least once in the financial year: **5.67 crore against 7.37 crore in FY2023-24** (5.32 crore against 6.85 crore in FY2022-23). The gap means **23.1% of the annual roll is out of the fund in an average month**, up from 22.5%. Nearly a quarter of India's formally employed population is not formally employed at any given moment. This is the closest thing India has to a spell-length measure of formal-sector employment, and it is published as a footnote to a membership count. Worse, the Annual Report 2023-24 (p. 61) defines "contributing members" as those "making regular contributions" and then gives the figure as those who "contributed at least once in the financial year." The two definitions are not the same measure, and the headline **7.37 crore overstates average monthly formal employment by about 30%.** Every citation of that number — and it is the number cited — reports a labour market a third more continuously employed than EPFO's own appendix says it is.

**The buffer that does exist rejects roughly a third of the claims made on it, and the rejection rate depends on which office receives it.** EPFO's Form 19 is a final settlement of the provident fund balance and requires two months of continuous unemployment; Form 13 is a transfer to a new employer's account and implies no gap. Form 19 rejection was **33.8% in FY2022-23 and 30.4% in FY2023-24** — 24.39 lakh and 21.38 lakh claims rejected on workloads of 72.21 and 70.33 lakh. Form 13 rejection *rose* over the same period, 23.7% to 26.3%. Rejection is not uniform: on identical national rules, Form 19 rejection runs at **39.1% in Delhi and Chennai, 36.7% in Mumbai-1 and 35.8% in Haryana against 24.2% in Kerala, 25.1% in Gujarat and 25.6% in Andhra Pradesh** — a 15-point spread produced by administration, not by claimants. For a *safety*-tier file this is the sharper finding than the churn metric: the one income buffer India's formal workers have is their own accumulated savings, the rule to access it is unemployment, and a third of the requests to access it fail at the counter. The mechanism is *instrument keyed to the wrong object* — the claim is adjudicated as a documentation and KYC-matching problem, and the worker's unemployment, which is the qualifying condition, is not what is being assessed.

The same two forms give the only India-wide read on whether a separation involves a gap. Form 13 transfers were **60.1% of claimed separations in FY2022-23 and 62.6% in FY2023-24**, so the majority of formal-sector job changes are job-to-job with no break. Form 19 settlements were 31–34% of all recorded exits, which puts a **floor** — not an estimate — under the share of exits involving a spell of two months or longer. It is a censored indicator in both directions: a worker who is unemployed but does not claim is invisible, and a worker whose claim is rejected is counted as not having been unemployed.

**Time to rejoin is not measured in India, and PLFS does not collect it.** The duration of unemployment — the quantity that turns a churn rate into a welfare loss, and the quantity the user asked for directly — has no Indian series. This is not a publication gap. PLFS Schedule 10.4 records employment status as a code (81/82 for unemployed) with no length attached; the schedule's only duration field is duration of *training*. India therefore cannot produce the statistic the US reports weekly as long-term unemployment, and the global section of this file cites 27+ week shares for the US and EU that have no Indian counterpart at any confidence. EPFO holds the raw material — every UAN has a last contribution date and a next one, so exit-to-rejoin distributions are computable inside the fund today — and publishes only aggregate rejoiner counts. The gap between "computable from data already held" and "not published" is the finding: *solution at hand, blocked*, where the blocker is disclosure policy rather than statistics.

**What is not measured, stated as a finding.** Nine gaps recur across the modes above and none is incidental. There is **no official series on retail-establishment closure**, so the only number on kirana shutdowns comes from an interested trade body. There is **no local-multiplier estimate for Indian metros**, so the second-order service employment attached to white-collar income is unobservable by construction. There is **no independent series on gig-worker earnings** — every figure in circulation originates with a platform, a consultancy or an NGO, and none is produced by a statistical agency, which is remarkable for a category the state has now legislated for twice. There is **no measurement of fresher displacement by experienced hires**, the mechanism most often asserted about the youth market. And the headline unemployment rate itself is a usual-status measure that classifies unpaid family work as employment, so **the single most-cited number in this file is the one least capable of detecting the failure the file describes.** Four further absences sit around the hiring channel specifically. There is **no vacancy-duration series** — QES counts open roles at a point in time and never how long they stayed open, so the second term of the user's identity cannot be computed for any Indian sector. There is **no collection of unemployment duration at all**: PLFS Schedule 10.4 carries a status code, not a spell length, so the shortfall is at the instrument, not the publication. There is **no NCS placement figure** in eleven years of operation, only mobilised vacancies and registrations. And there is **no published time-to-rejoin**, although EPFO can compute it per UAN from data it already holds. In tier-1 terms this is *compensation substitutes for counting* in a new form: the state has built a subsidy layer (ELI, ₹99,446 crore) and a registration layer (e-Shram, 312 million) on top of a detection layer it has not built.

#### Who is commercially active, and who pays

**Hiring and matching.** Blue-collar and mass-hiring platforms are the densest commercial category: Apna reached a $1.1 billion valuation in 2021 on a $100 million Series C led by Tiger Global, WorkIndia raised ₹97 crore in a Series B led by Avishkaar Capital, Vahan raised a $10 million Series B in 2024. Formal-staffing firms (Quess, TeamLease) operate at scale in the same space. **In every one of these, the paying customer is the employer**, and the product sold is a reduction in the employer's cost of acquiring or of not directly employing labour. The worker is the inventory.

**Upskilling** monetised the aspirant directly — the one model in this need whose customer *was* the harmed party — and it is the category that collapsed, with Byju's as the terminal case. That is a data point about the payer hypothesis rather than about edtech: the model that charged the unemployed person is the model that failed, and it failed in the direction of extracting from savings that the earlier modes in this section show to be already under attack.

**Where capital deployed and failed.** Job-matching unit economics break on the same thing repeatedly: churn. A blue-collar placement that dissolves in weeks cannot support an acquisition cost, so revenue drifts toward subscription from employers and toward volume staffing, which pushes the product further from the worker's interest. Nothing in this category has produced a durable business whose revenue rises when the worker's income rises.

**Over-served against measured harm.** Hiring platforms and staffing are dense; **income continuity is empty.** The density sits on top of a market nobody can price: with no vacancy-duration series and no unemployment-duration collection, neither a platform nor its customer can measure the cost of a role staying open, which is why hiring products are sold on applicant volume and cost-per-hire rather than on time-to-fill against lost output. There is no significant Indian commercial product that pays a worker when work stops. Gig-worker fintech (earned-wage access, insurance attached to platform IDs) exists but is distributed *through* the aggregator, which means the aggregator remains the customer and the product cannot be adversarial to it. Insurance against livelihood loss for the informally employed is essentially unwritten, because the trigger is unverifiable in the absence of the detection layer named above.

**Failure modes with no commercial actor**, and why: unemployment income replacement for informal workers (no verifiable trigger, no payer); migrant livelihood portability (the beneficiary moves between jurisdictions and no single one is accountable for them); fresher entry into a frozen white-collar market (the harmed party has no income to pay from — this is the pure "cannot pay" case); rural seasonal wage floors (the counterparty is a diffuse set of small farmers). Against tier 1's amendment that *"cannot pay" has consistently meant "is not organised,"* note the asymmetry: gig workers organised and produced three state statutes plus a central chapter in three years; the unemployed graduate is not organised anywhere and produced a subsidy scheme designed by the state, keyed to the employer, and measured by EPFO enrolment. **The mandate hypothesis holds too** — the Karnataka welfare fee, deposited under High Court order, is a compliance obligation manufacturing a payer where none existed, and it is the only mechanism in this file moving money to workers on account of their precarity.

#### Representation: all five verdicts appear inside one need

This is the first need in the project where every verdict in the gate-4 taxonomy is instantiated at once, which makes employment the cleanest available test of the framework.

- **Present, moved the blocker, execution failed** — IFAT and the Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Union, with Vidhi as the drafting partner. Affected-led, correct unit (local affected: the counterparty is a nameable platform and its local fleet), and they converted mobilisation into statute in Rajasthan (2023), Karnataka (2025) and a Telangana draft (2025), plus the central Code's gig chapter. Then Rajasthan's rules were never notified, Karnataka's Act went to litigation, and the central levy rate remains unnotified. The residual problem is the leg-2 → leg-3 handoff, not mobilisation — tier 1's dominant activism failure mode, recurring.
- **Present at the right unit, unmoved** — the ten central trade unions. They are the constitutionally-correct representation for the industrial workforce, they struck nationally on 26 November 2025, and the apex tripartite forum in which they hold a seat has not met since 2015. Representation is thereby *eliminated* as the explanation for the labour codes, which isolates the blocker: the channel exists and has been left dormant by the party that convenes it.
- **Present but at the wrong unit** — AICPDF on kirana closures. A **distributors'** federation is the loudest claimant for a harm borne by **retailers**, and its members are separately disintermediated by the same shift. Adjacency, in the same shape as tier 1's Warrior Moms: not a criticism of the body, a statement that the harm's unit has no organisation of its own and an adjacent one is speaking for it.
- **Present and organised against the remedy** — the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh, which welcomed the labour codes the other ten central unions struck against. Unlike tier 1's instances (Western Ghats, farm power, ethanol), the affected here do not straightforwardly benefit from the cause; the split is political rather than material, which makes it a distinct sub-case worth carrying into later tiers.
- **Absent at every unit** — **the unemployed.** India has no organisation of unemployed people. Not a professional advocacy body, not a union, not a claimant of any kind at any unit. This is the only verdict that fails gate 4, and it is the largest population in this file. A second, narrower population sits in the same cell: the roughly 21–24 lakh formal workers a year whose provident-fund settlement claims are rejected. They are identifiable, individually documented, concentrated in specific regional offices, and have no claimant body of any kind — no union takes up PF rejection as a subject, and the grievance route is the rejecting institution's own portal. NITES (Nascent Information Technology Employees Senate) organises IT *employees* and has been the one voice on IT-sector layoffs, but its constituency by definition is people who still hold jobs. The contrast with gig workers is the whole argument: the group that organised got legislated for in three years; the group that did not got a scheme keyed to its employer.

### India: stored risk, not yet realised

- **Robotics and manufacturing automation.** McKinsey Global Institute's estimate that automation could displace up to 60 million Indian manufacturing workers by 2030, concentrated in textiles and electronics, is a **model, not an observation** — no Indian robot-density or installation series was located in this pass that would corroborate it. NITI Aayog's >60% formal-job exposure figure is likewise exposure, not displacement. The user's reading is the right shape: AI has landed on codified white-collar work first because that work is already digitised, and the physical-labour tier is protected for now by the slower maturation of robotics rather than by anything structural. A breakthrough there arrives against a workforce with a *lower* wage floor, no unemployment insurance, and no organisation — every buffer weaker than the one white-collar workers had.
- **The 300-worker retrenchment threshold.** In force from 21 November 2025, with no shock yet to exercise it. Its effect is unobservable until the next downturn, and by construction the retrenchments it permits will not require the approval that would have generated a record of them.
- **The unnotified halves of the labour codes.** The gig-worker levy rate, the benefit structure, and the state rules in most states. Every month they remain unnotified is a month in which the removed protections operate and the added ones do not.
- **Microfinance's shed borrowers.** The FY25-26 contraction healed the book by moving to larger tickets and established credit histories. The borrowers dropped in that process have not appeared in any series since; whether they moved to informal lending, to unpaid family work, or out of enterprise entirely is unmeasured.
- **Household balance sheets after ₹1.05 lakh crore of derivatives losses.** The losses are counted at the market; their transmission into household savings, education spending and small-business capital is not tracked at all.
- **Youth cohort scarring.** The 2019 European precedent in the global section — Spain at ~25.8% and Greece at ~22.9% youth unemployment in early 2025, more than a decade after their peaks — is the relevant base rate. India's currently-graduating cohorts are entering a frozen entry-level market; labour-market scarring is a lifetime-earnings effect that shows up in data twenty years later, and nothing in India is set up to detect it.
