# Violence Protection (Tier 2 — Safety)

Tier definition: a buffer against unpredictable future threats to body, claims, and resources — surviving today isn't enough if you can be destroyed tomorrow.

## How the need has been threatened

**1. Fall of Western Rome, 4th–5th c. CE.** As central legions withdrew from provinces (Britain c. 410 CE, Gaul and Spain through the 450s–470s), local populations lost the security guarantee the Roman state had monopolized for centuries. Bryan Ward-Perkins, *The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization* (2005), documents the collapse of Roman-era material security — coinage, pottery, building standards — as a proxy for the collapse of protection against raiding and land seizure.

**2. Viking raids on Western Europe, 793–11th c.** The 793 CE sack of Lindisfarne opened two centuries of coastal raiding across Britain, Ireland, and Francia, exploiting the absence of any naval or standing defense capable of intercepting raiders before they struck monasteries and villages (Peter Sawyer, *The Age of the Vikings*, 1971).

**3. Mongol conquests, 1206–1368.** The Mongol campaigns under Genghis Khan and successors killed an estimated 30–40 million people across Eurasia (China, Persia, Rus'), with entire cities such as Baghdad (1258) and Nishapur (1221) subjected to mass killing after capture — populations with no army capable of resisting a mobile, disciplined steppe force (John Man, *Genghis Khan*, 2004; Ian Frazier, *The New Yorker*, 2005 estimate synthesis).

**4. Colonial violence against indigenous populations, Americas, 1492–19th c.** Spanish conquest of the Aztec (1519–21) and Inca (1532–72) empires combined military violence, forced labor (encomienda), and disease; later, U.S. westward expansion included massacres such as Sand Creek (1864) and Wounded Knee (1890). David Stannard, *American Holocaust* (1992), and the U.S. Indian Wars record document populations stripped of any recognized claim to armed self-defense once treaty structures were unilaterally broken.

**5. Armenian genocide, 1915–1923.** The Ottoman government's deportation and massacre of Armenians killed an estimated 600,000–1.5 million people. Raphael Lemkin later cited this episode when coining the term "genocide" in 1944 (Taner Akçam, *A Shameful Act*, 2006).

**6. The Holocaust, 1941–1945.** Nazi Germany's industrialized extermination of six million Jews and millions of Roma, disabled people, and others under state administration (Wannsee Conference, Jan. 1942) is the paradigmatic case of a modern state itself becoming the threat, with no external body empowered to intervene until military defeat (Raul Hilberg, *The Destruction of the European Jews*, 1961).

**7. Kristallnacht, Nov. 9–10, 1938.** A state-organized pogrom across Germany and Austria destroyed over 7,000 Jewish businesses and 267 synagogues and killed at least 91 people, with police ordered to stand aside — an explicit demonstration that the state's coercive apparatus can be turned into the instrument of violence rather than protection from it.

**8. Partition of India, 1947.** The partition triggered communal massacres between Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs across Punjab and Bengal, killing an estimated 200,000–2 million people and displacing 10–20 million, as departing British colonial authority left no functioning joint security apparatus in the transition window (Yasmin Khan, *The Great Partition*, 2007).

**9. Rwandan genocide, April–July 1994.** Hutu extremists killed an estimated 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu in roughly 100 days, while the UN peacekeeping mission (UNAMIR) was denied a reinforced mandate and the UN Security Council declined to name it genocide until after the killing had largely ended (Roméo Dallaire, *Shake Hands with the Devil*, 2003; UN inquiry, 1999).

**10. Bosnian War and Srebrenica, 1992–1995.** Bosnian Serb forces under Ratko Mladić killed approximately 8,000 Bosnian Muslim men and boys at Srebrenica in July 1995 inside a UN-declared "safe area" guarded by a lightly armed Dutch UN battalion — a direct case of an internationally guaranteed protection zone failing to protect (ICTY, *Prosecutor v. Krstić*, 2001).

**11. Somali state collapse, 1991–present.** The fall of Siad Barre's government in 1991 left Somalia without a functioning central state for over a decade; clan militias and later Islamist insurgents (al-Shabaab, from 2006) filled the vacuum, and piracy off the coast (peaking 2008–2011) emerged as a byproduct of the absence of any state monopoly on coercion (Ken Menkhaus, "Somalia: State Collapse and the Threat of Terrorism," 2004).

**12. Post-Soviet Russian organized crime, 1991–late 1990s.** The collapse of Soviet policing and legal institutions produced a surge in mafia-run "protection" rackets (krysha) that substituted for absent state enforcement of contracts and property, with contract killings of business figures and journalists common through the 1990s (Vadim Volkov, *Violent Entrepreneurs*, 2002).

**13. Mexican drug cartel violence, 2006–present.** Since President Felipe Calderón's military offensive against cartels began in 2006, over 400,000 homicides have been recorded in Mexico (INEGI data through 2023), with cartels in many regions supplanting municipal police and extracting "protection" payments — organized crime again filling a security vacuum left by weak local state capacity.

**14. Libyan state collapse, 2011–present.** The NATO-backed overthrow of Muammar Gaddafi in 2011 fragmented the state's monopoly on force among competing militias; the 2014–2020 civil war and slave markets documented by CNN in 2017 illustrate how quickly the absence of a violence-monopolizing authority reopens historically abolished practices.

**15. Domestic violence in the absence of legal protection, pre-20th c. norm.** Under English common law's "rule of thumb" doctrine (commonly, though disputedly, traced to 18th-c. jurist Francis Buller) and coverture more broadly, a husband's use of force against a wife was for centuries outside the reach of the state's violence-monopoly at all — protection from intimate violence was not recognized as a state function until temperance and suffrage movements forced statutory change (e.g., England's Matrimonial Causes Act 1878 allowing separation on grounds of cruelty).

## How humanity evolved to deal with this threat

- **State monopoly on legitimate violence (theorized 1919).** Max Weber's *Politik als Beruf* (1919) formalized the modern state as the entity claiming a monopoly on legitimate physical force within a territory — the conceptual anchor for every subsequent institutional response: if violence is monopolized and made accountable, it stops being arbitrary.
- **Professional civilian policing — Peel's Metropolitan Police, 1829.** Sir Robert Peel's Metropolitan Police Act created the first modern uniformed civilian police force in London, built on the "Peelian principles" (policing by consent, prevention over punishment) that became the template copied by U.S. cities (New York, 1845; Boston, 1838) and eventually most of the world.
- **Standing armies and national defense.** The shift from feudal levies to permanent professional armies (France's post-1445 compagnies d'ordonnance; the Prussian standing army under the Great Elector, 1644 onward) gave states continuous rather than ad hoc capacity to deter external violence, later formalized into UN Charter Article 51's right of collective and individual self-defense (1945).
- **Laws of war — Geneva Conventions, 1864–1949.** Henry Dunant's experience at the Battle of Solferino (1859) led to the First Geneva Convention (1864) and the founding of the ICRC; the four Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their 1977 Additional Protocols codified protections for civilians, POWs, and the wounded, creating an enforceable (if imperfectly enforced) international floor under wartime conduct.
- **UN peacekeeping, 1948–present.** Beginning with UNTSO (1948) and expanded after the Suez Crisis (UNEF, 1956), UN peacekeeping has deployed to over 70 missions; its record is genuinely mixed — Rwanda (1994) and Srebrenica (1995) are its canonical failures, while missions in Namibia (1989–90), Mozambique (1992–94), and Sierra Leone (1999–2005) are commonly cited successes, driving post-hoc reforms like the "Responsibility to Protect" doctrine (UN World Summit, 2005).
- **Truth and reconciliation mechanisms.** South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–2003), chaired by Desmond Tutu, traded amnesty for testimony to surface apartheid-era atrocities without full-scale retributive trials, becoming a template adapted (with mixed fidelity) in Rwanda's gacaca courts (2001–2012) and Sierra Leone's TRC (2002–2004).
- **International criminal accountability.** The Nuremberg (1945–46) and Tokyo (1946–48) tribunals first established that individuals, including heads of state, could face criminal liability for atrocity; the ad hoc ICTY (1993) and ICTR (1994) tribunals extended this to Bosnia and Rwanda, culminating in the permanent International Criminal Court (Rome Statute, 1998; operative 2002) — an enduring, if jurisdictionally contested, institution for prosecuting genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity.
- **Disarmament, demobilization, and reintegration (DDR).** Formalized as UN doctrine through the 1990s and consolidated in the UN's Integrated DDR Standards (2006), DDR programs (Mozambique 1992–94, Liberia 2003–09, Colombia's FARC demobilization 2016–17) convert combatants back into civilians to prevent post-conflict relapse into warlordism.
- **Domestic violence law evolution.** Statutory recognition of domestic violence as a state concern accelerated through the 20th century: the UK's Domestic Violence and Matrimonial Proceedings Act (1976), the U.S. Violence Against Women Act (1994), and India's Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act (2005) each extended the state's violence-monopoly obligation into a space historically treated as private and outside its remit.
- **Community policing.** From the 1980s, U.S. and UK forces shifted from reactive patrol toward community policing models (e.g., Chicago's CAPS program, 1993) intended to rebuild the legitimacy and local information flow that purely centralized state violence-monopolies had lost.

### Where it worked

- **Good Friday Agreement, Northern Ireland, 1998.** The Troubles killed roughly 3,532 people over 1969–1998 (Sutton database, Ulster University CAIN archive). Decommissioning of paramilitary arms and power-sharing government under the 1998 Agreement dropped conflict-related deaths to single digits annually within a few years — a measured before/after on the same violence-tracking methodology, not a change of counting rules.
- **Colombia's FARC demobilization, 2016–17.** The national homicide rate fell to 24.9 per 100,000 in 2017 (Colombian National Police), the lowest in over four decades, following the FARC peace agreement and demobilization of roughly 7,000 combatants. Partial relapse since — FARC dissident factions and other armed groups have re-escalated violence in parts of Cauca and Catatumbo — so this is a genuine positive control with a documented limit, not a permanent fix.
- **India's Left Wing Extremism (Naxal) decline.** MHA data show LWE-related violent incidents falling from over 2,200 (2009–10 peak) to under 400 (2023), and LWE-affected districts shrinking from 126 (2015) to 38 (2024) — the clearest India-specific positive control in this file, driven by a combination of security-force presence, targeted development spending, and surrender/rehabilitation policy.

## Where this fails today

Violence protection is not a solved historical problem — it is failing at scale right now, in 2026.

**Active armed conflicts.** Sudan's civil war, ignited April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces, has killed an estimated 150,000–400,000 people (analyst range; a Khartoum-state-only LSHTM study found 26,024 violent deaths April 2023–June 2024) and displaced 16.3 million people — 9.1M internally, 2.8M as refugees (UNHCR/IOM, end-2025). Ukraine's war, over four years old, has killed 16,431 civilians and injured 48,613 (UN OHCHR, cumulative to Feb 2026), with June 2026 alone recording 293 civilian deaths — the deadliest month since April 2022; 5.9M Ukrainians remain refugees and 3.7M are internally displaced (UNHCR). In Gaza, the Health Ministry (via UN OCHA) records 72,599 killed and 172,411 injured since October 7, 2023, with 1.4 million of Gaza's 2.1 million residents still displaced as of February 2026 — figures the UN has not independently verified. Myanmar's civil war since the February 2021 coup has killed over 100,000 people cumulatively (ACLED, July 2026), with 15,000 in 2025 alone, and displaced 3.5–3.6 million internally (UNHCR). Across the Sahel — Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger — jihadist violence produced over 25,000 conflict fatalities in 2024, the highest on record (ACLED); the region accounts for 51% of global terrorism deaths (Global Terrorism Index 2025).

**State collapse and security vacuums.** Haiti has no functioning national security apparatus: gangs control an estimated 85–90% of Port-au-Prince (UN, 2025), killed 5,600+ people in 2024, and have driven internal displacement from 315,000 (Dec 2023) to 1.4 million (late 2025, IOM). Libya and Sudan remain fractured among competing armed factions with no state monopoly on force, echoing the pattern documented in entries #11 and #14 above.

**Organized crime.** Mexico recorded roughly 30,000 homicides a year through 2022–2024 (SESNSP/INEGI), with cartels continuing to displace municipal policing in large swaths of territory. Ecuador's homicide rate hit a record 43–44 per 100,000 in 2023, prompting President Noboa to declare a state of emergency and "internal armed conflict" in January 2024 after prison riots and gang violence killed 62 people on New Year's Day alone; despite a brief 2024 dip, the rate re-escalated to a new record of 50.9 per 100,000 in 2025 (InSight Crime).

**Domestic and gender-based violence.** UN Women/UNODC estimate 50,000 women and girls were killed by a partner or family member in 2024 alone — roughly 137 per day. WHO estimates 840 million women (1 in 3) experience intimate-partner or sexual violence in their lifetime, with 316 million (11%) affected in the past year — evidence that entry #15's historical gap (violence protection not extending into the home) is only partially closed.

**Aggregate scale.** UNHCR's *Global Trends 2024* report counted 123.2 million forcibly displaced people worldwide at end-2024 — a record, though *Mid-Year Trends 2025* shows a decline to 117.3 million, the first meaningful drop in a decade, driven mainly by returns to Syria. UNODC's *Global Study on Homicide 2023* (its most recent edition) recorded 458,000 intentional homicides worldwide in 2021, the latest year with comprehensive data — no more current global count exists, itself a measurement failure layered on the underlying violence failure.

### India: violence sorts by identity — communal, caste, and the state's own custody

Violence protection in India fails unevenly across the population, and the largest measured category is not the one most visibly reported. Amnesty International's "Halt the Hate" tracker (902 reports, Sept 2015–June 2019) found roughly 70% caste-based (overwhelmingly against Dalits), not religion-based — communal violence is more visible in national media, caste violence is more frequent. Common Cause/Lokniti-CSDS's *Status of Policing in India Report* found ~50% of police personnel nationally believe Muslims are "naturally prone" to committing crimes, rising to 68% anti-Dalit and 56% anti-Adivasi bias among Gujarat police specifically. Muslims are 17.4–17.7% of undertrial prisoners against 14.2% of the population; SC/ST prisoners run near 32% of undertrials. This has a legal-instrument dimension, not just an attitudinal one: Habitual Offender Acts, the direct descendant of the colonial Criminal Tribes Act (1871, which criminalized ~13 million people across 127 communities "by birth" before repeal in 1952), remain active in 14 states and continue to disproportionately target the 313 Nomadic and 198 Denotified Tribes today.

On trend, the data does not support "constantly increasing" cleanly. NCRB's own communal-rioting count moved from 512 (2018) to 438 (2019) to 857 (2020, the anti-CAA/Delhi-riots year) to 378 (2021) — and India's own communal-killing toll fell 12% comparing 2014–21 to 2006–13 (NCRB via ThePrint's analysis). MHA stopped regularly publishing the series in the years since, so continuous comparison beyond ~2020 is not currently possible — a measurement gap, not evidence either way. What is not contested is impunity at the point of prosecution: of 2,373 recorded cases against police for human rights violations nationally, only 3 have ever ended in conviction, none since 2017, and cases of public felicitation of convicted lynching perpetrators (e.g., a sitting minister garlanding men convicted in the 2017 Ramgarh lynching) are documented, not alleged.

### India: land and eviction as state-instrumented violence

Development-induced displacement is estimated at 21.3–50 million people since independence depending on methodology and time window, with roughly 75% never adequately rehabilitated; tribal peoples are 8% of the population but over 40% of the displaced, consistent with the figure already cited in the tier-1 shelter file. Two live cases: the Supreme Court's February 2019 order to evict an estimated 1.18 million tribal families whose Forest Rights Act claims had been rejected was stayed within two weeks under public and UN pressure, and the underlying constitutional question — FRA against conservation law — remains before the Court. In Chhattisgarh's Hasdeo Aranya coal belt, the state's own Scheduled Tribes Commission found in November 2024 that gram sabha consent for mining had been forged, with tribal women sarpanches pressured into signing. Punitive home demolitions ("bulldozer justice") evicted 515,752 people in 2023 alone — 294 houses a day — with Muslim-owned property at 44% of demolitions against a 14% population share (Amnesty International, HLRN); the Supreme Court ruled the practice unconstitutional in November 2024. Against this, Sardar Sarovar's Gujarat resettlement package is a genuine positive control: independent research (NBER) finds resettled oustees measurably better off than non-resettled comparators, even though the displacement count itself is contested (42,000 families officially vs. ~85,000 by NGO estimate).

### India: due-process delay as violence's second act

India has 22 judges per million people against the Law Commission's 1987 recommendation of 50; roughly 31 million criminal cases are pending nationally (2024). This compounds specifically for crimes against women: the national rape conviction rate sits near 27% (NCRB 2022), falling to 13.2% in Delhi specifically with 90% court pendency in that city — a gap between national and metropolitan figures worth holding rather than resolving to one number. Average rape trials take 2.3 years, with 18% exceeding five. A study of 500 dowry-death judgments traced 42 acquittals directly to investigative failure — chargesheets filed 18 or more months after the death, mishandled evidence, missed witness summons. Forensic capacity is a specific bottleneck: as of 2017, over 12,000 DNA samples awaited testing across just three central labs, and only 16 of 31 state labs had DNA capability at all. Against this, fast-track courts funded by the Nirbhaya Fund disposed 94% of their 2023 caseload against 52% cumulative since 2018, and the Kathua case reached verdict in 17 months — evidence the gap is *coverage* (268 of 1,023 sanctioned fast-track courts still unfilled as of August 2024) rather than a limit of method.

### India: protest suppression and post-protest retaliation

Anti-CAA/NRC protests (Dec 2019–Feb 2020) killed 23–25 people, concentrated in Uttar Pradesh from police firing, with zero police arrests despite eight state investigation teams. At Thoothukudi (May 2018), police fired on anti-Sterlite protesters, killing 13; a 2020 judicial commission recommended action against 17 police officers including three IPS officers, with no convictions to date. UAPA convictions are rare against the full arrest pool but less rare among the small number of cases that actually reach a verdict — the gap between the two figures is itself the mechanism. Of 5,922 people arrested under UAPA 2016–19, only 132 (2.2%, NCRB) were ever convicted, a number depressed by chargesheets being filed in just 9% of UAPA cases in that window — most arrestees' cases simply never reach trial. Measured only among cases a court actually decided, NCRB's 2019 figure is 29.2% (80 convicted of 196 disposed that year), falling to 21.1% in 2020. Sedition, filed 165% more often in 2019 than 2016 (93 cases vs. 35), had a 3.3% conviction rate that same year. Read together: the process itself — years in jail before any verdict, whichever way it goes — is functioning as the penalty, independent of the eventual outcome. Bhima Koregaon/Elgar Parishad defendants were held 6.5 years pre-trial before 2025 bail, and Stan Swamy, 84 and with Parkinson's, died in custody in July 2021 after three bail denials. The 2021 Pegasus Project confirmed spyware targeting of 300+ Indian phone numbers, including 40+ journalists and several Bhima Koregaon defendants — surveillance continuing years after the triggering protest, not during it. The Supreme Court's May 2022 stay of Section 124A (sedition) is a genuine, working check: new filings dropped sharply afterward, and this should be read alongside the impunity pattern above rather than instead of it.

### India: violence's stored risk from the state's spending pattern

The global war-industry/military-spending loop above has a domestic mirror: India is the world's fifth-largest military spender ($92.1 billion, 2025, SIPRI), a budget line that competes directly with the judicial and forensic capacity shortfalls documented above (22 judges/million; three central DNA labs for the whole country). This is a resource-allocation observation, not a causal claim — no data here establishes that defense spending crowds out justice-system spending specifically — but it is the same "spend mismatched to source" mechanism named in the tier-1 summary, applied to a different need.

**The war-spending/war-industry loop.** Global military expenditure reached $2.887 trillion in 2025 (SIPRI), the eleventh consecutive year of increase and 2.5% of world GDP, the highest share since 2009 — US, China, and Russia together account for 51%. The Top 100 arms-producing companies' combined revenue hit $679 billion in 2024, up 26% over the decade, with SIPRI naming the Ukraine and Gaza wars directly as demand drivers; Lockheed Martin's revenue rose every year from $66.0bn (2022) to $75.1bn (2025). U.S. defense-industry lobbying reached $191 million in 2025 (OpenSecrets), up 24% year-on-year, and the Project On Government Oversight has tracked 672 former Pentagon and congressional officials now lobbying for defense contractors, with FY2021 contracts to firms employing ex-Pentagon staff totaling $89.3 billion. This is well-evidenced as *correlation with a plausible incentive mechanism* — money and career paths flow toward sustained conflict — not as proven causation, and it cuts against a simple reading: U.S. military spending itself fell 7.5% in 2025 even as its arms industry's revenue rose, and the Cold War arms race, the highest sustained spending ratio in history, produced no direct US–USSR war. Whether specific wars are fought *for* resources is separately contested: mainstream security-studies analysis (CFR, RUSI) frames Russia–Ukraine as territorial/security-driven, with resource control in occupied Donetsk and Zaporizhzhia a secondary, opportunistic dimension rather than the stated cause; a claimed resource motive in Gaza (offshore gas fields) is a minority position without institutional corroboration, and the U.S.–Iraq 2003 "war for oil" framing remains an open historiographical debate rather than settled fact.
