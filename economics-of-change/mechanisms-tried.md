# What's been tried, last 2-3 decades

Six mechanism clusters for funding/sustaining change where the harmed party isn't naturally the payer. Each entry: what was tried, hard outcomes, and whether it produced a durable (subsidy-free, post-exit) result.

## 1. Cash transfers / UBI

- **GiveDirectly**: $1B+ delivered. Kenya RCT — asset/consumption/food-security gains at 9 months, persisting 3 years. Uganda long-run — men given $380 in 2008 had 22% higher incomes in 2020 vs. control. GiveWell's Nov 2024 re-evaluation raised cost-effectiveness 3-4x on pooled RCT evidence (24,027 participants), but flags only 25-60% confidence the effect replicates in newer pilot variants (Uganda/Mozambique/Malawi).
- **Kenya long-run UBI RCT** (2017-2029, 23,000 people): lump-sum recipients started more/better businesses than 12-year monthly recipients — front-loaded capital beat steady income for enterprise formation.
- **Rich-world pilots** (Finland 2017-18, Stockton SEED, Denver Basic Income Project): real wellbeing/employment signals in some, none renewed at scale, none converted to a government/taxpayer-funded program.
- **Pritchett's critique**: cash-transfer RCTs optimize a marginal, individually-verifiable outcome while national poverty is driven by state capability (economic productivity, a responsive state) — cash transfers are 20% of what development economics should study, not the frontier.
- **Verdict**: cash transfers sidestep the payer-identity problem, they don't solve it. Cheap, high-fidelity delivery; zero examples found of a pilot converting into a self-sustaining, taxpayer-funded program.

## 2. Results-based financing / SIBs / DIBs

- **Peterborough SIB** (2010, first ever): reoffending fell 9.0% vs. 7.5% threshold; investors got ~3%/yr over 5 years. Works because the metric (recidivism, fixed window) is cheap and fast to verify.
- **Educate Girls DIB** (Rajasthan, 2015): hit 116% of enrollment target, 160% of learning target. The flagship case cited everywhere — precisely because there are so few others.
- **Scale**: only 217 impact bonds contracted worldwide as of 2021 (204 SIBs, 13 DIBs), across 30+ countries, since 2010. For a mechanism running 15 years, that's structural non-scaling.
- **Oxford GO Lab's own retrospective**: expected government cost savings "rarely realized in practice"; barriers are the "wrong pocket" problem (payer isn't the saver), high transaction costs, bespoke unscalable structuring.
- **Rwanda PBF** (health, national since 2008): genuinely scaled past pilot, raised utilization — but providers report it crowded out unmeasured services in favor of measured ones (Goodhart's law).
- **GAVI vaccine Advance Market Commitment** (~$1.5B, 2009): cleanest success in this cluster. Manufactured a payer (donor governments) for a single-dimensional, cheap-to-verify metric (doses delivered).
- **Verdict**: scales only where the outcome metric is single-dimensional, fast, and hard to game. Breaks or distorts on multi-dimensional real-world outcomes.

## 3. Blended finance / DFIs

- **Mobilization ratio, contested**: industry (Convergence) claims ~$4 private per $1 concessional. Independent number (ODI): <$0.75 per $1 in developing countries, **$0.37 in low-income countries** — less than half the input, in the poorest markets. 59% of surveyed development agencies don't even track this ratio.
- **Off-grid solar** (M-KOPA, d.light, Sun King): started on blended equity + concessional guarantees. Now shifting to securitized debt against real repayment data (Sun King $156M receivables deal, Standard Bank $200M M-KOPA facility) — the clearest "survived donor exit" signal found, though unproven through a full credit cycle.
- **Root Capital** (agri-SME lending, since 1999): $1.96B disbursed, 843 enterprises. 42% of borrowers graduate to commercial finance within 3 years — **58% don't**. Still runs on philanthropic "Resilience Grants" two decades in. Explicitly accepts permanent grant-dependency as the operating model, not a transitional phase.
- **Verdict**: the core claim (concessional capital catalyzes private capital that keeps flowing post-exit) is weakly evidenced. One sector (off-grid solar) shows a plausible path off subsidy; the median case (Root Capital) shows indefinite dependency.

## 4. Social enterprise / venture philanthropy

- **Acumen Fund** (2001-2020, 20 years, $115M into 123 companies): recovered $36.8M, portfolio valued at $68.6M — ~91 cents back per dollar, a net loss on financial terms. Acumen's own framing: patient capital beats grants on sustainability and beats VC on mission-fidelity, not on returns.
- **Base of the Pyramid** (Prahalad 2004) vs. **Karnani's "Mirage" critique**: Prahalad predicted multinational-scale profit serving the poor as consumers; Karnani argued distribution costs to dispersed poor populations are too high, and the real lever is treating the poor as *producers*, not consumers. Twenty years on, no MNC BoP program reached Prahalad's predicted scale.
- **B-Corp certification**: increases funding odds from impact investors (real signaling effect) but no gain in financial stability post-certification, equity ratio declines. Changes who invests, not whether the business becomes durable.
- **PAYGo off-grid energy**: strongest real-payer case in this cluster — genuinely converted low-income households into paying customers at scale — but survivors are still blended with concessional capital underneath the commercial revenue.
- **Verdict**: one genuine large-scale winner category (PAYGo energy), still blended. Patient capital sustains impact, does not compound capital (Acumen's own number).

## 5. Compliance-manufactured markets (beyond carbon)

- **SO2 Acid Rain Program** (US, 1990): actual cost $1.17-2B/yr vs. pre-program industry forecasts of $7.5-11.5B/yr. Emissions -43% by 2007 while coal generation rose 26%. 2005 analysis: benefits $122B/yr vs. ~$2B/yr cost. Clean success.
- **German bottle deposit (Pfand)**: 98% return rate on single-use containers, 99% on cans. Clean success.
- **India plastic EPR** (2022 rules): producers reported ~8M tonnes/yr packaging vs. CPCB's own 4.1M tonnes/yr waste-generation estimate — ~2x underreporting. CSE found ~700k fake recycling certificates in circulation. Informal waste-picker sector excluded from the framework entirely.
- **RECs (renewable energy certificates)**: same double-counting/fraud vulnerability as carbon credits, still fighting it with tighter serials/blockchain pilots rather than having solved it.
- **Net insight**: the discriminator isn't "mandate vs. no mandate" — all four had one. It's **whether the verified unit is proximate and physical** (a smokestack reading, a bottle scanned at the same register that sold it) **or distal and claims-based** (a forest that didn't get cut, a certificate for recycling done elsewhere). Distal claims-based credits are structurally fraud-prone regardless of verification infrastructure bolted on afterward.

## 6. Crypto public-goods funding

- **Gitcoin Grants**: ~$67M distributed to 5,000+ projects. Growth plateaued; abandoned pure quadratic funding after Sybil/collusion attacks (fake identities splitting into many small "independent" donations to fraudulently trigger the match) — now requires identity verification and manual review. The "breadth over depth" math only holds if identity is unforgeable; it isn't.
- **Optimism RetroPGF**: real scale — 4 rounds, >$100M allocated, $1.3B more reserved. But the voting mechanism changed every single round (quadratic → mean → median variants) — instability itself is the finding: nobody has landed on a stable answer to "who judges retroactive value."
- **Impact certificates/markets** (Cotton-Barratt lineage): still theoretical, no real-world launch found.
- **Verdict**: none of this has escaped crypto's own ecosystem — Gitcoin funds crypto builders, RetroPGF funds contributions to Optimism itself. No documented case of either mechanism redirected at a non-crypto, real-world non-paying-beneficiary problem at scale.

## 7. Capital-perspective durable cases (not government programs)

Added because tier 1's "what worked" was mostly state capacity (Bihar PDS, Jyotirgram, leaded petrol, polio) — this cluster asks where private/philanthropic capital, not the state, built something durable.

- **Aravind Eye Care System** (1976): founder Dr. G. Venkataswamy mortgaged his own home to start it — no donor capital at founding. ~60% of care free/subsidized; roughly 1 paying patient funds 2 free ones. Self-sustaining, running a surplus, for 50 years. HBS case study since 1993.
- **Narayana Health**: cost/volume model, 30-35 complex cardiac surgeries/day at ~$1,800 average cost. Yeshasvini micro-insurance (₹11/month, state-partnered, 4M+ enrolled) makes ~30% of patients payable. Now publicly listed (NSE: NH) — FY24 revenue ₹3,389cr, net profit ₹425cr (16% margin); FY25 revenue ₹5,580cr, margin 14%. Attempted export to Health City Cayman Islands hit a hard scale wall — 55,000 people can't generate the volume the cost curve needs — required a full ownership redesign, partner exited in 2017.
- **BRAC** (Bangladesh, ~50 years): enterprise arms (Aarong retail ~$150M/yr revenue, BRAC Bank, BRAC Dairy, BRAC Seed) generate ~80% of BRAC's annual budget, funding its own development programs. Each enterprise reinvests roughly half its surplus into BRAC's mission by charter.
- **Grameen Bank**: 92-97% owned by its own borrower-shareholders, profitable on its lending margin, dividends returned to borrowers. Independent research (Khandker/World Bank) found a real subsidy of 4-22% per loan outstanding during 1989-93, declining to ~4% by 1994 — self-reliant *now*, not from day one.
- **Lijjat Papad** (1959, founded on an ₹80 loan): ~₹1,600 crore revenue, 45,000 women, zero external capital in 65+ years, profits split equally among members, no hierarchy. The cleanest positive control found in this whole project.
- **IFFCO/KRIBHCO**: farmer-owned fertiliser cooperatives, 28.8% of India's fertilizer production, 36,000+ member societies, 55M+ farmers served. Elected boards village-to-state, professional management under member ownership — structurally identical to Amul. Weaker-sourced than Amul/Lijjat (mostly cooperative-sector or trade press found so far); flagged for a skeptical second pass.
- **Dhara (edible oil, "Operation Golden Flow")** — the sharpest negative case. Same NDDB parentage and distribution network as Amul, grew to >50% organized-market share within 3-4 years — then ownership drifted from farmer federation to a corporatized entity (Mother Dairy). Proves the failure variable isn't sector/perishability (Dhara had Amul's exact conditions); it's whether governance stays insulated from capture.

**The direct capital-market extraction case, for contrast**: **Compartamos Banco** (Mexico, 2007 IPO) and **SKS Microfinance** (India, 2010 IPO) ran the same "make the poor payable" mechanics as Grameen, then sold equity to outside investors. Compartamos: ~86% annual interest, 53% ROE, ~$460M profit to early shareholders on the IPO. SKS: aggressive growth and coercive collection in Andhra Pradesh, borrower suicides, a state regulatory crackdown, stock crash, seven consecutive quarterly losses. Ownership determined who captured the margin; going public added an independent growth-pressure mechanism that pushed toward extraction even where the underlying lending model (Grameen's) was sound.

**Net verdict**: real, capital-market-adjacent durable social change exists (Aravind, Narayana, BRAC, Grameen, Lijjat, IFFCO) — but every clean case shares one variable: governance structurally insulated from capture by outside capital, either by charter (BRAC, Grameen) or by cooperative law (Amul, Lijjat, IFFCO) making an IPO/external-equity route practically unavailable. See `lens.md` axis 2 for how this maps onto the Acemoglu/Johnson/Robinson inclusive-vs-extractive institutions framework (2024 Nobel).
