# The lens — working draft

Goal: given a social-change initiative, predict whether its economics will hold, before committing capital. Distilled from `mechanisms-tried.md` (six clusters, last 2-3 decades) and cross-checked against `../problems/tier-failure-history/tier1-physiological/00-summary.md` §6, an independent empirical source that converged on the same pattern without being built to test it.

## Three axes so far

**1. Verification proximity.** Is the unit being paid for physical and local (a smokestack reading, a bottle scanned at the same register that sold it), or distal and claims-based (a forest that didn't get cut, a certificate for recycling done elsewhere)? Proximate-physical mechanisms (SO2 trading, bottle deposits, GAVI's doses-delivered) survive. Distal-claims mechanisms (carbon offsets, plastic EPR certificates, RECs) get gamed regardless of how much verification infrastructure gets added afterward. This is `../CLAUDE.md`'s mechanism #7 ("compensation substitutes for counting") one level deeper: it's not just whether a measurement layer exists, it's whether the measured unit *can* be verified cheaply by construction.

**2. Ownership vs. payment — refined: capture-resistant governance, not sector or perishability, is the binding variable.** Making a beneficiary a *paying customer* (PAYGo solar, BoP consumer models, Aravind/Narayana's patients) reliably works but stays volume- or subsidy-dependent. Making a beneficiary the *owner of the surplus* (Amul, Lijjat Papad, BRAC, Grameen Bank) produces the durable, decades-long, subsidy-free cases. Tier 1's own §6 independently names this: "the pattern is ownership structure, not technology."

The sharper test is the within-pattern failures, because they kill the lazy version of this axis. **Dhara** (edible oil) had the *same* NDDB parentage and distribution advantage as Amul and still drifted to corporatized ownership (Mother Dairy) — so perishability/quality-testability isn't what makes cooperatives hold. **IFFCO** (fertilizer, not perishable at all) holds up fine at large scale. The variable that actually discriminates is whether governance stays insulated from capture — professional management structurally separated from whoever might want to extract, board seats that can't be politically or financially bought out.

**This maps cleanly onto Acemoglu/Johnson/Robinson's inclusive-vs-extractive institutions framework (2024 Nobel)** — not as a stretch, once stated correctly. Their mechanism: a narrow group captures power at a "critical juncture" and rewrites the rules to extract rents from the broader population that created the value. An **IPO or external-capital injection is exactly such a critical juncture** — it doesn't need to be an old incumbent defending itself; new capital becoming the narrow elite at that juncture is a direct instance of the mechanism, not a different causal direction. Compartamos (86% interest, $460M IPO windfall to early shareholders), SKS Microfinance (coercive collection, borrower suicides, regulatory crackdown after its 2010 IPO), and Dhara (governance drifted to a corporatized parent) are all inclusive institutions hit by a critical juncture that installed a new extractive elite. Grameen Bank and BRAC are the inclusive-institution side that held — member-owned, profit capped/reinvested by charter, no external shareholder claim ever created.

Two honest caveats on using AJR here: (1) their theory was built and evidenced at the *national* level — applying it to firm/cooperative governance is this project's own extension, not an established academic move (no citable literature found doing this). (2) The framework has a real, named blind spot worth carrying alongside it: Mushtaq Khan's critique (made explicitly at the 2024 Nobel announcement) that AJR's own positive-control cases — the US, Canada, Australia as "inclusive institutions" — were themselves built through indigenous dispossession and violence, and the framework has no mechanism to detect that split because it only measures institutional quality from the position of whoever already holds power. Use AJR for the vocabulary and the critical-juncture mechanism; don't treat it as validated ground truth.

**Practical form of axis 2, restated**: predict durability by asking *what happens to this structure at its first capital-raising event*. If the entity has no plausible path to an IPO/external-equity round (Amul, Lijjat — cooperative law structurally blocks it), the axis holds by default. If it does have that path (any VC-backed social enterprise, any MFI), the real question is whether the charter/ownership structure caps what a future capital injection can extract — not whether the founding intent was good.

**3. Metric dimensionality.** Single-dimensional, hard-to-game, fast-to-verify metrics (vaccine doses, recidivism in a fixed window) let results-based financing scale past pilot. Multi-dimensional real-world outcomes get gamed (Goodhart's law — Rwanda's PBF crowding out unmeasured care) or the mechanism just never leaves pilot stage (217 impact bonds globally in 15 years). The corollary: a mechanism's scalability can be estimated in advance from how many dimensions its outcome metric has, before a single dollar is committed.

## Two negative findings, worth keeping visible

- **Cash transfers sidestep the payer question rather than answer it.** Excellent at delivery fidelity, silent on durability — every example, rich or poor country, stayed donor/grant-funded.
- **Crypto-native mechanism design (quadratic funding, RetroPGF) hasn't escaped its own ecosystem** even with a captive, capital-rich audience and years of iteration — informative about how hard "manufacture a payer for a public good" actually is even under near-ideal conditions.

## Capital-perspective durable cases (as opposed to government programs)

Added after the user pushed back that tier 1's wins were mostly state capacity, not capital. Full detail in `mechanisms-tried.md` §7 — summary here:

- **Aravind Eye Care**: self-financed at founding (no donor capital — founder mortgaged his own home, 1976), 1 paying patient cross-subsidizes 2 free ones, self-sustaining 50 years. Volume- and cheap-labor-dependent — doesn't obviously port to small/high-labor-cost markets.
- **Narayana Health**: publicly listed, profitable (14-16% margin). Its own attempt to export the model to the Cayman Islands hit a hard scale wall (55,000 people can't drive the cost curve) — evidence this is extreme-scale-plus-cost-arbitrage, not a portable mechanism.
- **BRAC**: enterprise arms fund ~80% of its own budget, surplus capped/reinvested by charter, durable 50 years.
- **Grameen Bank**: 92-97% borrower-owned, profitable on lending margin now — but ran a real 4-22%-per-loan subsidy during its 1989-93 bootstrap phase. Self-sustaining *now* does not mean never subsidized.
- **Lijjat Papad**: the cleanest positive control found across this whole project — zero external capital in 65 years, profit split equally among 45,000 member-owners, no hierarchy.
- **IFFCO/KRIBHCO**: cooperative structure at large scale (28.8% of India's fertilizer), holding up over decades — weaker-sourced than Amul/Lijjat, flagged for a skeptical second pass.

## Open question: is Amul a repeatable structure?

Answered in the conversation that produced this file (see notebook/session context, 2026-07-31) — not yet written up as its own file here. Provisional summary: yes, with governance conditions that are necessary, not automatic. The structure (Anand pattern / Operation Flood) is: three-tier federation (village society → district union → state federation) separating local trust/collection from centralized brand/marketing; professional salaried management hired for competence and structurally insulated from the producer board's daily control (IRMA was created specifically to train these managers); quality-linked, near-immediate cash payment removing farmers' dependence on moneylenders/middlemen; one-time catalytic capital (Operation Flood monetized World Food Programme/EEC dairy aid into fixed assets — chilling plants, processing) rather than recurring subsidy. It was deliberately replicated nationally under Operation Flood (1970-1996) and worked at varying quality — strongest where Gujarat's governance conditions held, weakest where they didn't. The counter-case is instructive: Maharashtra's cooperative sugar factories used the same legal form but were captured by political elites into patronage vehicles, because the board wasn't insulated from political nomination the way Amul's was. **The cooperative form is necessary but not sufficient — governance safeguards against capture are the variable that decides whether it replicates Amul or degrades into rent-seeking.** Worth its own file once tested against a second domain (e.g. does the pattern show up anywhere outside dairy/agri-commodities).
